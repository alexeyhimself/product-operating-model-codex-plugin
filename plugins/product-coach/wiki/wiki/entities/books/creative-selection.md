# Creative Selection

> Inside Apple's design process — demos, prototypes, and iteration as the engine of great products (the author built the iPhone keyboard).

## Why it's recommended
On SVPG's **[[svpg-product-operating-model-introduction|Product Operating Model: An Introduction]]** reading list. A vivid illustration of [[product-discovery|discovery]] by demo/prototype — Apple exemplar.

**The endorsement in full (2018).** Cagan devoted a whole article to this book — [[2018-09-28-cagan-product-discovery-at-apple|Product Discovery at Apple]] — which is the wiki's primary provenance for the recommendation and clears CLAUDE.md §2 twice over (dedicated article + reading-list entry). *"I wanted to encourage product people everywhere to read it… I really do hope you'll all read this book."* What he says it demonstrates:

- **What the book conspicuously lacks, and he says it's no accident.** *"You won't see a single reference to product roadmaps, or to business cases, or to techniques for prioritizing features, or even the concept of product requirements."* This is the page's strongest use — a first-hand account of the most valuable company in the world discovering the iPhone keyboard, containing none of the artifacts most orgs treat as essential.
- **An engineer's account.** Kocienda's ~15-year arc to principal engineer, mostly as tech lead — strong material for [[empowered-engineers]], since the innovation is engineer-led throughout.
- **A three-of-four-risk worked example.** The keyboard carried *"obvious and major technical feasibility risk, but equally significant usability risk, and the most significant risk of all, the value of the overall device was clearly a function of whether this keyboard solution would be perceived as good enough."* The Newton is the cautionary precedent — *"doomed because they couldn't come up with an effective solution for entering text."* See [[the-four-big-risks]].
- **"Creative selection" defined** — Kocienda's term for an idea evolving through *"literally hundreds"* of prototype iterations, *"continuously guided in the right direction by feedback from different perspectives."*
- **It corrects the "Apple is pure taste" myth.** *"Most people think Apple does all their discovery work qualitatively, and while you'll see that is certainly a big part of it, they also intelligently use data to inform tough decisions"* — his example being how the team determined the optimal icon size for the original iPhone. A useful corrective for teams who conclude from the Jobs mythology that evidence doesn't matter.

Cagan ties the book to his own thesis: its three themes are *"precisely"* the three of strong teams — tackle risks up front, solve problems collaboratively, focus on results not output — and it is *"another great example of the type of engineering-powered innovations"* from [[2017-09-25-cagan-customer-inspired-technology-enabled|Customer Inspired; Technology Enabled]].

## The Steve Jobs myth
Cagan cites *Creative Selection* as the one book that shows what Apple's product discovery under Steve Jobs was *actually* like ([[2025-04-21-cagan-design-a-product-like-steve-jobs|How to Design a Product Like Steve Jobs]]). Jobs was **not** a top-down dictator handing engineers solutions; he set a very high bar and teams brought him prototypes weekly, iterating toward something good enough to ship. Kocienda's account of the original iPhone — a "100% touchscreen device" → ~100 hard problems for [[empowered-engineers|empowered engineers]] to solve — is the antidote to the dangerous "act like Jobs = boss everyone around" misreading. Jobs "didn't know the solutions; he just knew what would make a great product." Pairs with the 1995 Jobs *Lost Interview* (also a Cagan pick).
