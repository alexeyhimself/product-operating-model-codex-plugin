# Strategy and Focus Health — Diagnostic

> Coaches hear the same request over and over: *"help us fix our prioritization,"* or *"help us implement OKRs so people know what to work on."* This diagnostic exists to test whether that's the real problem, or whether — as [[2025-10-02-idiodi-cagan-coaching-product-strategy|Cagan puts it]] — *"you don't have a prioritization problem, you have a strategy problem."* Distinct from [[product-strategy-quality]] (which scores the org against the four canon pillars — focus, insights, actions, management, as durable artifacts) — this diagnostic is the faster, conversation-scoped check: the tell that reveals a missing strategy, the shape of the roadmap that's standing in for one, and whether declared focus actually survives the next executive request.

## Why it matters
A company that "can't prioritize" is not lacking a technique — it's lacking the choices a real strategy would already have made. [[2025-12-22-idiodi-lieberich-coaching-focus|Lieberich]] hears the same call so often he compares himself to Pulp Fiction's Mr. Wolf: *"just implement OKRs so everybody knows how to prioritize."* But OKRs, prioritization frameworks, and roadmap templates all fail the same way when there's no strategy underneath them: *"a tiny thing changes and everything is thrown overboard again."* Left unaddressed, this shows up downstream as one of [[2025-11-13-idiodi-hickman-coaching-roadmaps|Hickman's four roadmap anti-patterns]] — a roadmap that looks organized but is really just a negotiated list of stakeholder features, executive reactions, or capacity-filling. This diagnostic protects [[focus]], [[powered-by-insights]], and [[placing-bets]] by catching the symptom early, before it hardens into a roadmap architecture that's expensive to unwind.

## Signals & symptoms

**Healthy:**
- The team passes Lieberich's **litmus test for focus**: when an escalation or new request lands, they can explain why the current top priority is #1 and what the new thing would cost to take on instead.
- **2–3** focus areas, named plainly, that the whole org (not just leadership) can recite.
- Roadmap entries are **feature candidates** attached to a problem and a measure of success — not committed features attached to a date ([[2025-11-13-idiodi-hickman-coaching-roadmaps|Hickman]]).
- When a stakeholder's request doesn't fit this quarter's focus, the answer is a real **"not yet"** conversation with a tradeoff made explicit — not a flat no, and not a silent yes.
- Leaders can point to the **insight** — data, customer signal, or market read — behind each of the 2–3 bets, not just a preference.

**Warning:**
- People in the building say, unprompted, *"we have a prioritization problem"* or *"we need to implement OKRs."*
- The roadmap technically lists "outcomes," but the underlying planning conversation is still about features — an outcome-shaped roadmap with feature-shaped reality.
- A leadership offsite has produced **7–12 "pillars" or "big rocks,"** each one a run-on sentence — *"make the platform more stable, more secure, more revenue, best-in-class."*
- Plans get made crisply and thrown overboard the next time a new idea, a competitor move, or a senior request lands.

**Red flag:**
- The roadmap matches one of **Hickman and Idiodi's four roadmap anti-patterns**: **stakeholder-driven** (collect every request, prioritize the list, no discovery); **executive/competitor-driven** (the CEO Slacks "when are we shipping this?" every time a competitor launches — the **swoop-and-poop** move, a leader dropping a top-down reaction into the roadmap and moving on); **fill-the-bucket** (match tickets to engineering capacity, a capacity-planning exercise wearing a roadmap's clothes); **feature-factory** (ask why any item is on it and the honest answer is "because the boss said so").
- A skip-level ("grandfather") test — ask a front-line engineer *"what is the strategy, what are you doing here, why do you show up to work?"* — gets a blank stare or an answer that doesn't match leadership's.
- **12-big-rocks / best-in-class-everything**: the "focus" list still tries to be everything to everyone; nothing was actually cut.
- The org cannot answer, for a mid-priority item, **what problem it solves and how success will be measured** — Idiodi's two-question filter.

## Rubric
| Level | Name | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Prioritization theater** | "We have a prioritization problem" is said out loud, unprompted. Roadmap is stakeholder-driven, executive/competitor-driven, fill-the-bucket, or feature-factory (often more than one at once). No written narrative exists; nobody can name the insight behind any current priority. |
| **2** | **Big rocks, no focus** | A leadership offsite produced 7+ "pillars," each an everything-sentence in disguise. The plan is thrown overboard the moment a new idea, competitor move, or swoop-and-poop request lands. Roadmap items are labeled outcomes but planned as features. |
| **3** | **Real focus** | 2–3 named bets survive contact with a skip-level test. Roadmap entries are feature candidates tied to a problem and a measure, not committed dates. "Not yet" conversations happen instead of silent yeses or flat nos. Insights behind each bet are nameable. |
| **4** | **Focus as culture** | The litmus test passes reflexively, org-wide — anyone can explain why #1 is #1 and what a new request would cost. Swoop-and-poop requests get absorbed into the existing strategy narrative rather than reshuffling the roadmap. Leaders ask "who is this for?" and "what are you pretending not to know?" as habits, not interventions. |

## Socratic questions
_One at a time — don't lead the answer._
1. Has anyone in this building said, in the last month, "we have a prioritization problem" or "we need to get better at OKRs"? What were they actually asking for?
2. Read your current list of strategic priorities out loud. Count them. If it's more than three, which one would the CEO refuse to cut — and why isn't that the *only* one?
3. Pick one item at random from the roadmap. Can you say what problem it solves, how success will be measured, and what insight it's based on? If not, per Idiodi's rule — should it be there at all?
4. Do the grandfather test: ask the newest or most junior engineer what the strategy is and why they show up to work. Compare the answer to what leadership would say.
5. Which of the four roadmap anti-patterns — stakeholder-driven, executive/competitor-driven, fill-the-bucket, feature-factory — best describes how items actually land on your roadmap today?
6. Think of the last time a plan got thrown overboard the day after it was made. What landed — a competitor's launch, an executive's reaction, a sales deal? Was there a "not yet" conversation, or did the plan just evaporate?
7. When was the last time this organization had a hit that was *intentional* — something you made happen, not something that happened to you?

## What "good" looks like
A written narrative — even a rough one — exists behind the current strategy, naming what's being built, for whom, and what evidence backs it, in Amazon-six-pager spirit if not in that exact form. From it: 2–3 named bets the whole org, not just leadership, can recite, each with a nameable insight behind it. The roadmap communicates those bets as feature candidates tied to a problem and a measure of success, with dates reserved for genuine high-integrity commitments. When a new request lands — a competitor's move, an executive's reaction, a sales deal — the org can explain, in the moment, what it would cost to take on instead of the current focus, and either says "not yet" with a real tradeoff on the table or deliberately re-prioritizes. A skip-level conversation with a front-line engineer produces roughly the same account of "why we're doing this" that leadership would give.

## Interventions & experiments
- **At Level 1:** don't touch OKRs or prioritization frameworks yet — they'll just formalize the absence of a strategy. Run the written-narrative exercise as a **fact-finding tool**, not a document-production exercise ([[2025-12-22-idiodi-lieberich-coaching-focus|Lieberich]]): where it's easy to write reveals what's already clear; where it's hard reveals what to go find out. Convert the highest-visibility stakeholder-driven roadmap items into the transitional (problem, measure, feature-candidate) format — [[2025-11-13-idiodi-hickman-coaching-roadmaps|Hickman]]'s migration takes zero permission from anyone above you.
- **At Level 2:** run a focus workshop that forces the 7–12 pillars down to 2–3 real bets — if leadership won't make the cut, nothing else here will stick. Pair each surviving bet with its insight and its source. Start using "not yet" instead of silent yes/no when swoop-and-poop requests land, so the tradeoff becomes visible instead of absorbed silently.
- **At Level 3:** sustain with a **quarterly strategy narrative** that closes with an explicit "we are continuing our focus on X" or "changing our focus to Y" — Idiodi's practice of making every stakeholder's issue visible in the document even when the conclusion doesn't go their way. Use a **Product FAQ** for the genuinely irreconcilable questions ("as of this quarter, this is the best answer we have").
- **At Level 4:** keep the muscle alive with regular check-ins (roughly every six weeks, plus the quarterly and annual cadence) — alignment is fragile and dissipates the moment a new person joins or the market shifts. Use the **concentric-circles method** when re-aligning after a shock: start with the person closest to the decision, then bring in the next ring.
- **Common traps:** (1) treating "implement OKRs" as the fix when the real gap is upstream, in strategy; (2) confusing a long list cut to a shorter list (prioritizing) with an insight-driven set of bets (focusing); (3) letting the roadmap's format change (features → "outcomes") without the underlying planning conversation changing; (4) allowing swoop-and-poop executive requests to silently reorder the roadmap instead of forcing an explicit tradeoff conversation.
