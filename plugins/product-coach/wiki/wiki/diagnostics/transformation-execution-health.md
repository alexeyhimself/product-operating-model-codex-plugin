# Transformation Execution Health — Diagnostic

> Once a transformation (or a pilot inside one) is actually *running*, is it executing well, or has it drifted into one of the named anti-patterns coaches see over and over? Distinct from [[transformation-readiness]] (the preconditions to *start*) and [[model-maturity]] (where the whole org sits against the model). This diagnostic is for the messy middle: pilots that have launched, leadership that has announced intent, and the gap between what leaders say they want and what teams are actually experiencing day to day.

## Why it matters
Announcing a transformation and having leadership "fully behind it" changes nothing about execution — the [[transformation-anti-patterns|named anti-pattern catalog]] exists precisely because good intentions collapse into the same recognizable failure modes: renaming titles and calling it done ([[2026-02-19-idiodi-hickman-coaching-transformations|Hickman]]'s **installation, not adoption**), sanctifying a single pilot team instead of teaching what it learned ([[2025-12-11-idiodi-bufrem-coaching-pilot-teams|Bufrem]]'s **sacred golden child**), or cycling leaders between over-delegation and panic micromanagement ([[2026-03-19-idiodi-castenfors-coaching-transformation-anti-patterns|Castenfors]]'s **loop of transformation failure**). None of these show up on a readiness checklist taken before kickoff — they only show up once real teams, real leaders, and real stakeholders are actually doing the work. This diagnostic protects the [[pilot-teams|pilot-team pattern]] from decaying into theater and gives a coach a vocabulary for naming what's happening before it hardens into "this is just how we do transformation here."

## Signals & symptoms

**Healthy:**
- Bufrem's **three signs a pilot is working**: the numbers move, people want to join the team (the CEO starts getting emails), and the team catches itself in old habits ("that's how we used to do things, now we do something else").
- **Adoption**, not installation — people can point to skills they've grown (empathetic, business-minded, coachable PM traits; real trio collaboration), not just new titles or ceremonies.
- Leadership intent and team-level reality **match** when you ask both independently — a skip-level conversation with an IC produces the same account of "what we're doing and why" as the leadership deck.
- The pilot is protected (Bufrem's **island-with-a-boat**) but not sanctified — its lessons are actively being taught elsewhere, and a successful pilot leader gets moved to spread the pattern rather than kept in place as the only place it works.
- Leaders and teams are **both** being coached (the [[#the-two-muscles|two muscles]]), and the transformation is talked about as a **spiral**, not a project with an end date.

**Warning:**
- The org can name its transformation's vision but a skip-level asks "what's the strategy, what are you doing here" and gets a blank stare or a different answer than leadership's — [[transformation-anti-patterns#the-air-sandwich|the air sandwich]].
- More than one or two "North Stars" exist across the org — Castenfors's **17 North Stars** problem (if you can't say what's *not* a North Star, you don't have one).
- A pilot's own manager — not the CTO/CPO who signed the pilot off — is anxious and unlooped: *"my job is changing but I'm not in the pilot team."*
- Teams say the words "empowered" and "autonomous," but nobody has checked whether they have the skills to act on it yet.

**Red flag:**
- **Pilot theater** — a pilot that works, but nothing broader ever changes; the org is stuck in an infinite pilot cycle.
- **Pilot-team factory** — leadership decided "this is the way" and instantly stood up dozens of cookie-cutter pilot teams, killing the very learning the pattern exists to produce.
- **Sacred golden child** — every hard problem gets routed to the one team that's working, instead of the rest of the org learning from it.
- **Autonomy handed out before coaching** — "we're empowered, we can do whatever we want" → chaos → leaders abort back to micromanagement → the [[transformation-anti-patterns#the-loop-of-transformation-failure|loop of transformation failure]] restarts.
- **Product-management theater** — Product Owners relabeled Product Managers with no change in skill, coaching, or decision rights ([[product-management-theater]]).
- **Title theater** — the same relabeling pattern applied org-wide (business analysts → PMs, graphic designers → product designers, engineers → tech leads), named in SVPG's own written canon as one of seven forms of [[transformation#transformation-theater-the-wider-taxonomy|transformation theater]] ([[2024-02-27-hickman-transformation-theater|Hickman]]).
- **Transformation-in-a-bubble** — product and tech have changed; sales, marketing, customer service, HR, legal, and finance were never brought along, and are now surprised: *"where's my road map? What happened to my feature?"*

## Rubric
| Level | Name | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Installation theater** | Titles and ceremonies changed; competencies didn't. No real pilot exists, or the one that does has become a sacred golden child. Skip-level conversations reveal a wide air sandwich. Leadership has already cycled once through the loop of failure and landed back on micromanagement. |
| **2** | **Executing, unevenly** | A real pilot is running and shows *some* of Bufrem's three signs, but at least one named anti-pattern is live — autonomy handed out ahead of coaching, the pilot's middle-manager layer left anxious and unlooped, or 5+ "priorities" competing for the North Star label. Leadership intention and team reality still diverge in places a skip-level would catch. |
| **3** | **Executing with adoption** | The pilot (or pilots) shows all three of Bufrem's signs. Leaders and teams are being coached together, not just the team. Installation is visibly converting to adoption — people can describe skills grown, not just new job titles. No sacred golden child; the org is actively teaching what the pilot learned. |
| **4** | **Scaling exemplary** | Successful pilot leaders get **moved** to spread the pattern elsewhere (Idiodi's scaling move). The dream-team multiplier is visible — people who learned in the pilot are now teaching others. Anti-patterns are named openly, in real time, as a matter of course, and self-correct before they harden. The org survived at least one setback without reverting to the loop of failure — the culture bank ([[pilot-teams]]'s Datasite near-fail) was full enough. |

## Socratic questions
_One at a time — don't lead the answer._
1. Pick a pilot team. Walk through Bufrem's three signs: are the numbers actually moving? Is anyone outside the team asking to join it? Have they caught themselves mid-habit and course-corrected?
2. Do a skip-level. Ask an individual contributor: "what is the strategy? What are you doing here, and why?" Compare their answer to what leadership would say. How big is the gap?
3. Count your organization's "North Stars" right now. If the honest answer is more than one or two, which of them would you actually cut?
4. When you last called something "empowerment," had the team's skills, coaching, and context caught up — or was it a decision made and announced?
5. Which step of the [[transformation-anti-patterns#the-loop-of-transformation-failure|loop of transformation failure]] are you in right now, if any? *Innovate → patience → "are we there yet" → no results → abort to micromanagement → micromanagement stifles innovation → repeat.*
6. Is your best-performing pilot team teaching other teams what it learned, or is it quietly becoming the only team leadership trusts with anything hard?
7. If you renamed every "Product Owner" in the building to "Product Manager" tomorrow, what would actually be different about how they work in six months? If the honest answer is "nothing," that's Hickman's installation-not-adoption test.
8. Whose job outside product and tech has this transformation already changed — sales, marketing, customer service, HR, legal, finance? If the answer is "none of them yet," you may be transforming in a bubble.

## What "good" looks like
Pilots that show all three of Bufrem's positive signs, running on an island that's protected but not sealed off — a boat runs increasingly often as the team proves itself. Leadership and teams are coached together, on the two different muscles each needs (solving problems for the team; deciding-with-context for the leaders). Installation has visibly become adoption: people can describe new skills and new decision rights, not just new job titles. No single team is sanctified — the pattern is actively taught outward, including by physically moving successful pilot leaders into new parts of the org. North Stars number one or two, not seventeen. A skip-level conversation with the newest engineer on the team produces roughly the same account of "what we're doing and why" that leadership would give. Sales, marketing, HR, legal, and finance were brought into the change deliberately, not left to discover it by surprise.

## Interventions & experiments
- **At Level 1:** stop adding ceremony. Name out loud which step of the loop of transformation failure you're in — that alone often breaks the cycle (Castenfors's **golden rule**: name anti-patterns as they appear so people don't slip into them unconsciously). Pick (or re-pick) one real pilot using the ambition-ceiling and manager-support checks from [[pilot-teams|the political-selection criteria]]. Get a coach in the room with **both** the team and its leaders from day one.
- **At Level 2:** name the specific live anti-pattern and treat it as the intervention target, not the whole transformation. Autonomy-too-soon → slow down and coach the missing skill before handing over more scope (Idiodi's five-precondition test for real empowerment: skills → problem → measurement → context-and-tools → accountability). Anxious middle managers → loop them in explicitly; transformation has to happen at every layer, not just the pilot and the C-suite. Too many North Stars → force the cut before touching anything else.
- **At Level 3:** protect against the sacred-golden-child trap as you scale — the dream team's job is to *teach*, not to be the only ones who can do it. Use discovery demos to keep momentum visible through the transformation valley of despair, even before anything ships.
- **At Level 4:** invest in the leader-redeployment move deliberately — a leader who has seen good work and knows what it takes is the fastest way to spread the pattern into a new part of the org. Keep naming anti-patterns openly; the moment naming them stops, they stop being caught early.
- **Common traps:** (1) treating "we launched a pilot" as the finish line rather than the first demonstration; (2) mistaking a title change for a competency change; (3) letting one team's success excuse the rest of the org from changing; (4) transforming product-and-tech while sales, marketing, HR, legal, and finance find out by surprise.
