# Where the Model Applies

> The question every coaching conversation eventually reaches: *"does this work in my kind of org?"* The wiki has, until this ingest, said a great deal about what the product model **is** and comparatively little about where its edges are. Six SVPG articles — three explicitly framed as "probing the edges" (Traditional IT, Government, Outsourcing), plus Org Design, M&A, and Commercial vs. Internal Products — are Cagan's own direct answer, span 2024–2026, and are gathered here so the answer doesn't stay scattered across six separate pages.

## The argument
The model was built **from** and **for** product organizations — companies building technology-based products and services with a team of engineers ([[2024-07-09-cagan-the-product-model-in-traditional-it|Traditional IT]]). It is not universal by claim; Cagan is explicit that the book was intentionally scoped this way to avoid the over-promise/under-deliver failure of other models. But the underlying test for whether it applies **elsewhere** is narrow and mechanical, not cultural or sector-based:

> Does this team/org **build**, and does it have the **levers to impact outcomes**? ([[2024-07-09-cagan-the-product-model-in-traditional-it|canon]])

Three conditions disqualify a fit even where building happens: the team is severely limited by a specific third-party product it doesn't control; the team lacks ongoing daily access to engineers; the team has been given output and/or a date that takes precedence over the outcome. Where none of these hold, the model applies regardless of sector, profit motive, or org-chart shape — which is why it extends cleanly into government (non-profit-seeking) and, with real friction, into IT departments and outsourcing agencies (both traditionally "serve the business" cultures).

Two axes the wiki now treats as **orthogonal** to this fit test — real, but not fit questions themselves:
- **[[org-design-models|Org structure]]** (GM/Functional/Hybrid) — "whether or not the company embraces the product model is orthogonal to the org design they choose" ([[2024-07-30-cagan-the-product-model-and-org-design|canon]]). Neither model prevents nor guarantees product-model success; a reorg is rarely the fix a stalled transformation needs.
- **[[2026-04-09-cagan-commercial-vs-internal-products|Commercial vs. internal]]** — both are "products" carrying the same four risks; the bar differs (captive users lower it internally), and product discovery is the differentiator for competitive, commercial success — but this is a difference in *degree of difficulty*, not in whether the model applies.

And two are **special-case applications** of the model as a lens, not new territory to transform:
- **[[2025-01-21-cagan-fredell-ma-and-the-product-model|M&A]]** — using a product-organization assessment (the same category of instrument as [[transformation-readiness]] / [[model-maturity]]) as a due-diligence tool on an acquisition target, and as an integration-protection plan afterward.
- **Pilot teams** ([[pilot-teams]]) — the entry mechanism into *any* of the partial-fit contexts below; how you *start* applying the model somewhere it hasn't been tried is itself governed by [[2025-07-14-cagan-the-politics-of-pilot-teams|its own article]].

## Evidence

### Full fit — product organizations (baseline)
Already the wiki's default assumption throughout [[overview|the overview]] and every core concept/principle page. Not re-argued here.

### Partial fit, real friction
| Context | The advantage | The friction | Root source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional IT** | Increasingly doing real product-scale building; shifting to outcomes already underway | Historic cost-center framing; funding/staffing/governance models built for predictability, not outcomes; nomenclature mismatch (project vs. initiative; PMO- vs. empowerment-school project managers) | [[2024-07-09-cagan-the-product-model-in-traditional-it]] |
| **Government** | Genuinely mission-driven staff; public/press focus on outcomes and waste avoidance; captive audience | Dominant, contested viability risk (legal/policy/privacy/security/ethics); fraud risk; project-driven procurement (waterfall's actual historical origin); high stakeholder count with few empowered to say yes | [[2024-08-07-cagan-langer-the-product-model-in-government]] |
| **Outsourcing agencies** | Real product/engineering talent already on staff, serving clients daily | Client believes *they* are the PM; fixed-price/fixed-spec contracts structurally prevent outcome ownership; team durability across engagements is hard to protect | [[2024-08-13-cagan-kerievsky-the-product-model-in-outsourcing]] |

All three partial-fit contexts converge on nearly identical techniques for success: high-fidelity prototypes as the persuasion tool (not decks, not big documents); reframing project-based funding into a weekly-discovery/biweekly-delivery iteration cadence; continuously evangelizing outcomes so attention and funding don't evaporate; small, skilled, cross-functional teams; and — for Government and Outsourcing specifically — a leadership sponsor or trusted client relationship that can absorb the "no"s a large stakeholder set generates.

### Orthogonal axes, not fit tests
- [[org-design-models|Org design]] (GM / Functional / Hybrid) — see [[2024-07-30-cagan-the-product-model-and-org-design]].
- [[2026-04-09-cagan-commercial-vs-internal-products|Commercial vs. internal products]] — same four risks, different bar, discovery is the differentiator commercially.

### Special-case applications
- [[2025-01-21-cagan-fredell-ma-and-the-product-model|M&A due diligence]] — assess the *target's ability to keep creating product*, not just the product being bought; watch for "deal fever"; protect an acquired innovative culture from being crushed by the parent post-close.
- [[2025-07-14-cagan-the-politics-of-pilot-teams|Pilot teams]] — the practical mechanism for entering any partial-fit context for the first time; in large or acquisition-assembled companies, treat each business unit as its own transformation.

## Counterpoints
The strongest case against treating this as "the model applies almost everywhere": Cagan's own Traditional IT fit test is a real disqualifier, not a formality. An org that has genuinely lost the levers to impact outcomes — locked into a vendor's roadmap, denied daily engineer access, held to an output-and-date mandate that overrides outcome — is not a coaching problem to solve *within* the product model; applying the model's vocabulary there without the levers just produces a new flavor of [[product-management-theater|product-management theater]]. The honest answer in that case is naming the disqualifying condition, not forcing a fit. Similarly, the Outsourcing article is explicit that *most* outsourcing agencies serve non-product-model clients and are not a candidate for this article's advice at all — the piece is narrowly about agencies whose clients are willing to fundamentally change the engagement structure, which the article itself says is uncommon.

## Where this stands
As of this ingest, the wiki treats "does the model apply here" as answerable by a single mechanical test (build + outcome-levers) rather than by sector, profit motive, or company size — with org design and commercial/internal status explicitly ruled out as fit questions, and M&A and pilot-team politics established as the two special-case applications of the model as a lens/entry-mechanism rather than as new territory. Nothing here is contested within the SVPG canon; the open item is practical, not doctrinal: none of these six articles has yet been tested against a live coaching conversation in this wiki's own use, so the "techniques for success" convergence across Government/Outsourcing/Traditional IT is currently a wiki-synthesis observation, not something Cagan states as a unified claim across all three articles at once.

## Two additions from the 2024 Jones interviews

**The non-tech exemplar: [[john-deere-smart-industrial|John Deere]].** Jones's standing answer to *"this is for software companies, not for us"* ([[2024-04-03-jones-moving-to-the-product-operating-model-product-people]]) is a 190-year-old agricultural-equipment manufacturer where *"everything was absolutely legacy"* — and where an **engineer**, given customer context through a two-way tractor camera, invented machine-learning-targeted herbicide spraying. His framing: pretty much every company is now a tech-enabled company, including ones that predate the internet and predate computers.

**The corporate-IT limit, stated more sharply than elsewhere.** In [[2024-11-02-jones-from-features-to-outcomes-khodor]], asked about transformations that struggled, Jones raises traditional corporate IT organisations that are genuinely trying — chasing outcomes, time-to-value, pockets of ownership — but whose work is largely *integrating and configuring vendor tools* rather than building solutions:

> *"Here's a place where the product model can apply somewhat — there are certain aspects of it that can apply — but it certainly can't be as total a transformation as something that is more directly customer-facing."*

Consistent with [[2024-07-09-cagan-the-product-model-in-traditional-it|Cagan's build-plus-outcome-levers fit test]], and useful because it comes from the scaling side: an area can be an excellent fit for the **first pilot teams** and a progressively worse fit as the transformation spreads. Jones's other two scaling cautions belong here too — a transformation carried by one charismatic champion who leaves before critical mass, and **wartime energy that fades**, after which it is easy to slip back into the old ways.
