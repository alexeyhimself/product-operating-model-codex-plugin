# Governance and Mission-Locked Companies

> Great products are **necessary but not sufficient** for a great company — a company that has proven it can ship great products becomes an attractive target for people whose motivations aren't the mission. Corporate-governance structure is what determines whether the company can be captured and hollowed out anyway. Articulated by [[marty-cagan|Cagan]] in [[2026-07-01-cagan-great-products-bad-companies|Great Products, Bad Companies]] (Jul 2026), reacting to Eric Ries's 2026 book *[Incorruptible](https://www.incorruptible.co/)*.

## Why it matters
Product people are trained to obsess over customer value, discovery, and delivery — and almost never taught to look at their own company's governance structure, even though it materially decides whether the culture and mission they joined for can survive contact with investors and board members optimizing for a quick financial win rather than the company's long-term health. Cagan names this a blind spot in his own decades of work: *"governance has been a subject that I completely deferred to the judgement of corporate lawyers and business experts. That may have been a big mistake."*

## How it works

### The pattern: great products attract predators
A company that has demonstrated it can ship products customers love becomes visibly valuable — and that visibility attracts "the kind of people that infiltrate boards and leadership teams, replace founders and other missionary leaders, and destroy cultures and companies, all in the hope of a quick financial win." This isn't rare or hypothetical: Cagan describes it happening repeatedly across startups, scaleups, and large companies, and says he has personally tried and failed to talk board members out of replacing leaders over normal execution bumps (a missed quarter), arguing what matters is the longer-term trajectory.

### The missing piece: alternative governance structures
Cagan's argument, borrowed from Ries's *Incorruptible*, is that this outcome is not actually inevitable — it's a consequence of *choosable* governance structure. Some companies (some of Cagan's own favorites, old and new) are already built with structures designed to resist exactly this kind of capture. Ries's term for the result is a **"mission-locked" company**: one structurally protected from investors and board members whose goals diverge from the company's stated mission.

### Where this sits relative to strategic context
Governance isn't one of the six elements of [[strategic-context|strategic context]] as the wiki currently defines them (mission, scorecard, objectives, vision, strategy, principles) — it's a layer *underneath* strategic context, determining who ultimately gets to set and change it. A product leader coaching an org toward the [[product-operating-model|product model]] is implicitly betting on the durability of the leadership team's mission commitment; governance structure is what that bet actually rests on.

### Update — from "a big mistake" to active coaching (Cagan, Sep 2026)
Cagan returns to governance in [[2026-09-11-cagan-strong-opinions-loosely-held|*Strong Opinions, Loosely Held*]] as #8 of his ten biggest regrets, and the stance has **hardened from confession to prescription**. The Jul 2026 framing (above) was largely diagnostic — he'd "written about this problem" but "really didn't think there was much we could do about it," so he "viewed these product teams as unfortunate victims." The Sep 2026 article closes that gap: *"Today I coach product leaders to take a more proactive role in working with the CEO to ensure that the company has a governance structure and corporate culture durable enough to succeed on the product vision."* He re-cites Eric Ries's *Incorruptible* as the book that "tackles this problem head on." So the coach's move is no longer just *"know your governance structure"* — it's *"help your CEO build one that can survive success."* Note the boundary still holds: this is Cagan **endorsing and relaying** Ries's work, not originating a governance framework (see the anti-pattern below).

## Anti-patterns & misunderstandings
- **Treating this as purely a founder/board problem, irrelevant to product people.** Cagan's explicit point to the contrary: understanding your company's governance affects your day-to-day life as a product person, because it determines whether the culture that attracted you can be protected.
- **Confusing this with ordinary board oversight or normal accountability.** The pattern Cagan names is specifically about predatory replacement over short-term financial optics, not legitimate governance or performance accountability.
- **Assuming this is Cagan's area of expertise.** He flags it isn't — he's relaying and endorsing Ries's argument, not originating a governance framework himself. Treat claims here as a strong endorsement of external work, not SVPG canon in the way the four risks or the 20 principles are.
