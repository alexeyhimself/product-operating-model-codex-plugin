# Vision Pivot

> A **vision pivot** abandons the [[product-vision|product vision]] itself and puts the company on a path to a different business; a **discovery pivot** changes the solution while keeping the vision intact. Cagan's point is that teams constantly mistake the second for the first — and give up on a good vision far too early.

## Why it matters
"Pivot" is, in Cagan's words, "probably one of the most overused and abused terms in today's product teams" — used loosely as a synonym for *change* (a pivot of the tech stack from Java to Ruby, a pivot from Scrum to Kanban, a "pivot" of a team onto a new assignment, "because pivots are something cool teams do"). Most of that loose usage is harmless. The harmful case is specific and costly: **teams giving up on their product vision too early**, because they have labelled a normal solution change as a fundamental one.

Cagan is blunt about the failure rate: "most of the time, especially with startups, I see them failing not because their vision was bad, but because they gave up on their vision too soon."

## How it works

### Vision pivots — rare, expensive, and mostly unwarranted
The [[product-vision|product vision]] is the 2–5 year picture of what you're trying to achieve. It is "typically why people join a company or team, and it's a compelling problem that we all believe is worth solving." Cagan's framing of the default stance: "We know it's hard. If it was easy, it would not take us several years to solve, and it probably would have been solved already by lots of others." So the organization's job is to make the vision real — "we don't work for a few weeks or even a few months and then just declare it's not happening."

The operational mantra he borrows is Bezos's: **"Be stubborn on vision, but flexible on details."**

But the organization must also "be able to distinguish vision from illusion." A vision pivot is warranted when **the vision itself is the problem** — and in Cagan's experience that means one specific finding: *you are solving something customers don't care enough about.* **There is no real market.** That is a cheap thing to check — see [[2008-05-13-cagan-market-discovery-vs-product-discovery|market discovery vs. product discovery]] — and, he notes, "this is not usually the issue."

### Discovery pivots — frequent, cheap, and expected
While pursuing the vision, [[product-discovery|discovery]] tries a great many ideas. "We expect that many of these ideas won't work, and the ones that do will require several iterations." So teams must not be "wedded to particular solutions or features or approaches" — be open to different monetization models, different growth mechanics, different features driving activity.

The test of whether something is *only* a detail: "Your vision is not to offer a subscription model vs. a transaction model. That's just one particular approach to monetization." So long as you find a way to make the vision come true, you're fine.

This is why discovery competence is a *vision-protection* skill: "Product discovery is how we get as many iterations towards our vision as possible," with the objective of reaching [[market-fit|product/market fit]] "before we run out of money (or management runs out of patience)."

### The one-line distinction
> A **discovery pivot** moves you closer to realizing your product vision.
> A **vision pivot** puts you on a path to a different business with a different vision.

## Anti-patterns & misunderstandings
- **Calling a discovery pivot a vision pivot.** The central error. A team hits several failed solution attempts, concludes the vision is wrong, and walks away from a market that was real and a problem worth solving. What they needed was more discovery iterations, not a new business.
- **Survivorship bias from famous pivot stories.** "We all love to talk about the very visible vision pivots that created products and companies like Flickr, YouTube, Twitter, Facebook, and Groupon. However, what's talked about a lot less is the much more frequent discovery pivots that are less visible yet lead to the consistent improvement behind successful products." The visible cases are the rare ones; the invisible ones are the norm.
- **Using "pivot" as a morale device.** Relabelling a team's reassignment as a pivot is the harmless-but-hollow usage Cagan flags — it dilutes a term that needs to stay precise to be useful.
- **Being stubborn on the details instead.** The inverse failure: holding a particular monetization model, feature set or architecture as though it were the vision, and pivoting the *vision* to preserve the detail.
- **Pivoting the vision without ever validating the market.** If the trigger for a vision pivot is "no real market," that claim should be tested, not assumed — the techniques are fast and well established.

## In your context
_Agent prompt: when someone in the org says "we're pivoting," establish which kind before anything else. Ask what specifically is being abandoned — the customer problem and market (vision), or the current approach to solving it (discovery). If they claim the vision is wrong, ask what market evidence supports that, and how many discovery iterations they actually ran first. Consult the org's separately-supplied [[strategic-context|strategic-context baseline]] for the stated vision and how long it has been in play._
