# Transformation Politics

> Every transformation is a people change at scale, and every people change is political. Cagan (channeling Christian Idiodi): **"every problem is a people problem."** In every successful transformation, leaders were aware of and intentional about the political challenges; they did not avoid them ([[2024-11-21-cagan-transformation-politics|canon]]).

**Cagan's 2026 admission that this page's subject was a long blind spot.** In [[2026-09-11-cagan-strong-opinions-loosely-held|*Strong Opinions, Loosely Held*]] (#6) Cagan names ignoring politics as one of his ten biggest regrets: *"I tried to ignore the importance of politics, hoping that strong product work would be enough to carry the day. That was naive."* He notes people rarely call it politics — they say "stakeholder management, executive briefings, or organizational alignment" — but the underlying power structures are "a consequence of limited resources, competing objectives, insecurities and personal ambitions," part of the fabric of nearly every company (worst at larger ones). His tell of how far the correction has gone: *"Most of my content in the past three years has been either about dealing with politics, or understanding the impact of AI"* — which is exactly why this page and its sibling [[cross-functional-partnering]] exist.

## Why it matters
A transformation to the product model **redistributes decision rights** — from stakeholders (who used to fill the roadmap) to product teams (who now choose solutions). It changes who is consulted, who is accountable, and who is judged for results. Without explicit political work, transformations stall at [[model-maturity|Level 2 — checkbox transformation]] because the new structures don't have the trust they need to operate.

## Eight political dimensions (Cagan's framing)
Each comes from [[2024-11-21-cagan-transformation-politics|Transformation Politics]] directly:

### 1. Product Leaders
The product leader is the key. Where the leader hasn't worked in the model, a [[types-of-product-coaching|product leadership coach]] is usually essential to fill the gap. The leader must establish working relationships with executives and stakeholders — starting with **humility and a genuine desire to learn** their concerns and constraints. The product leader is "the face of the broader product organization" — be careful which PMs you expose to stakeholders, and when. **"The product leader will be judged by her weakest product manager."**

### 2. Product Managers
"Few things will undermine confidence and trust faster than people with a product manager title that are viewed by stakeholders or executives as clueless about how the company actually works." Hence the need for **effective PM onboarding** on business viability — marketing, sales, finance, monetization, legal, compliance, security, privacy. **Protect the integrity of the "product manager" title** — and any title with the word "product" in it. Resist title proliferation (assistant/associate PM, multiple flavors of PO).

### 3. Change Management
Gradualism rarely works. Once a transformation starts, the **clock starts ticking** — orgs run out of patience before small changes can show impact. The more reliable path is **demonstrating the impact of a much larger set of changes in a contained scope** (= [[pilot-teams|pilot teams]]). Show what good really looks like, on real work.

### 4. Pilot Teams
The **political mechanism** of transformation. See [[pilot-teams|pilot teams framework]] for the design checklist. Strong product leaders hand-pick members, pick problems carefully, ensure training and coaching, and aim for **business outcomes** stakeholders will respect.

### 5. Product Discovery
**Trust the stakeholders on the problem.** Stakeholders have lived with the problems for years and have very good understanding of what needs solving. Pilot teams that spend weeks or months in problem discovery while stakeholders watch are politically corrosive. **Race to a successful solution.** If the team's problem-framing turns out to be wrong, solution testing will reveal it within days.

The general rule: if a product team works the same problem for **more than two weeks**, they need immediate coaching to decide whether to continue ([[discovery-health|canon]]).

### 6. Product Strategy
This is the **politically hardest** of the three transformation dimensions — moving control over what to work on from stakeholders to product leaders. The wiki's operational rule: **hold this dimension for last**, after Solve and Build have produced trust. Cagan: "you will likely have better results holding off on changing how you decide which problems to solve until after you have largely won the hearts and minds of the stakeholders and executives." (This refines [[2022-05-31-moore-transformation-defined|Moore's earlier 'pursue in parallel' guidance]] — both are canon, see [[transformation|Transformation page]].)

### 7. Keeping the Lights On
Every product team also has a responsibility to handle the never-ending list of minor items stakeholders need to run the business. If a team — pilot or not — appears to ignore this, stakeholders will insist on resources for it one way or another. **Budget for it in pilot teams**, or the political support evaporates.

### 8. Internal Evangelism
Large-company transformation takes **1–3 years**. Very few orgs are prepared to wait that long for results. Maintain a **drumbeat of real business outcomes**, monthly minimum:
- **Real business results**, not activity. ("Don't confuse business results with activities.")
- **Both successes and failures.** Sugar-coating destroys credibility.
- **Frequency matters.** Monthly is the floor; more is better.

## What this is *not*
- **Not "playing politics" in the bad sense.** Cagan is explicit about this. Politics here means *being intentional about the people-change* a transformation requires.
- **Not flattering stakeholders.** Trusting their problem framing is not flattery; it's respect for their domain expertise and a faster path to results.
- **Not waterfall.** Holding the *Decide* change for last doesn't mean staging Solve+Build in advance. It means sequencing **what you reset trust on** when.

## Anti-patterns
- **Avoiding the politics.** "We just need to do the work; the politics will sort itself out." It won't.
- **Picking the politically loudest stakeholder as the pilot's customer.** The point is *visible business outcome*, not appeasing the loudest voice. Pick the problem that, if solved, demonstrates the model.
- **Long problem-discovery against stakeholder will.** Politically toxic; usually unnecessary; rarely worth the trust cost.
- **Sweeping organizational change before pilot proof.** New titles, new orgs, new processes adopted enterprise-wide before a pilot has produced a single business outcome.
- **Quarterly transformation reports of *activity*.** "We trained X people, ran Y workshops, established Z forums." None of these are business outcomes.

## The podcast-form deepening — Product Therapy Ep 14 (Cagan)
The [[2024-11-21-cagan-transformation-politics|Nov 2024 article]] above is the written canon; [[2025-02-06-idiodi-cagan-coaching-transformation-politics|Product Therapy Ep 14 (Feb 2025)]] is the podcast-form co-primary that adds practitioner detail:

- **The "results window."** *"The moment you say 'we're going to start a transformation,' the clock starts ticking. Pretty much every organization I've ever seen will lose interest, lose motivation, or get distracted"* if real results don't land inside that window. Corollary: **bigger changes for a much smaller piece of the org beats small changes across the whole org.**
- **The Jonathan Moore / Trainline pilot in Cagan's voice** — Moore picked his best PM, designer and engineers, put them on a new native mobile app (the "prize at the end of the road"), and told the team explicitly: *"we need to show the rest of our own organization what good looks like."* The pilot-team framing is not just about learning; it's a **political prize** the org can point at.
- **The multi-BU rule.** Transform one business unit at a time; substitute the BU-GM for the CEO in every political dynamic; an acquired BU is *"all bets off"* and non-transitive from a nailed transformation elsewhere.
- **The CEO-mandates-sweeping-change-now scenario.** When a CEO refuses a pilot, invoke the [[technology-adoption-life-cycle]] — laggards are always a small %, but forcing the *middle* to move faster than ready loses would-be contributors. *"You hurt all at once, discover all at once."*
- **The #1 transformation-failure cause (restated crisply).** *"The product leaders think all they could do is retitle their product owners to product managers."* Stakeholders keep going to the two people who actually know the industry and deliver, not to the retitled PMs. See also [[feature-teams-vs-product-teams]].
- **The politics of picking problems (new addition).** Trust the stakeholder that the problem is real — they've been dealing with it for 10 years — and discover the *solution*, not the problem. Making the team re-verify problems is *"a way to really undermine trust right at the beginning."* Earn a seat at the problem-picking table by first delivering outcomes on the stakeholder's problems.
- **The champion / evangelist duty.** Someone (CEO or CPO) puts their reputation on the line, keeps beating the drum, and makes sure every success is broadcast. Not usually the pilot team's manager — but deeply involved in picking it, skip-leveling into it, and coaching the managers of the pilot team members.
- **Idiodi's closing frame:** *"You got to win hearts and minds before you seek to lead."* The pack-leader metaphor — *"nobody joins a pack and automatically becomes the pack leader."*

## The change-management framing — Product Therapy Ep 18 (Hickman)
[[2025-04-17-idiodi-hickman-coaching-change-management|Ep 18 (Hickman)]] adds a distinct pillar to the political-dynamics enumeration: **the middle-management resistance layer.** Senior execs get the why (competitive threat, valuation gap). Teams get the why (they want empowerment). But **middle management built their career operating in the old way — the change *threatens* what has made them successful.** They aren't resisting maliciously; most simply *don't know what "great" looks like* to reinforce the change. The fix: **the training → coaching shift.** Training is a one-time event; coaching is ongoing support. Middle managers pushing back may not disagree with the transformation — they just don't know how to reinforce it in their teams. Hickman's *"never coach a change-management department — coach the leaders"* rule is the corollary. See [[transformation]] for the fuller change-management treatment.

## Trust-building tactics from Ep 24 (Cullen · Fredell · Datasite)
[[2025-07-24-idiodi-cullen-fredell-coaching-transformation-datasite|Ep 24 (Datasite)]] surfaces the practitioner tactics that made trust-building work during a real transformation:
- **Public learning.** Idiodi (then at Datasite) spent significant time with Cullen (Head of Sales) *publicly* — *"I had no right to tell the organization what problems to solve — I had no competence in the industry. But people had to see me learn."* The sales-leader meeting in New York where the leaders asked no product questions, only about family and life, then reported back: *"this guy is cool, it's okay."* Trust as *fundamental*: forget the process; do I trust your intent enough to give you a chance to fail?
- **The culture bank.** Cullen's metaphor — *"you have to have something in the culture bank if you're going to make a withdrawal from it."* The Datasite One launch failure was a huge withdrawal; the trust deposits had been made ahead of time. See [[transformation]] for the fuller treatment.
- **The extended trio.** Idiodi and Cullen jointly extended the standard PM+designer+engineer trio to pull in sales and service management when needed — kept the sales team engaged as supporters, not skeptics, during a long build with no product to show for months. See [[cross-functional-partnering]].
- **Convert the CRO through customer visits.** Idiodi took the CRO to two customer meetings. First: skeptical. Second: CRO hits his head on the table realizing this is *validating* what the first customer said, going deeper. From then on the CRO championed a product tour and called every sales office to welcome the product team. Single conversion → pivot.
