# Org Design Models

> How a company draws its reporting lines around products: the **GM (general manager / business-unit / single-threaded-leader) model**, the **Functional model**, or a **Hybrid** of the two. A different axis from [[team-topology|team topology]] — org structure is *who reports to whom*; topology is *what each product team is responsible for and how members collaborate*. Articulated by [[marty-cagan|Cagan]] in [[2024-07-30-cagan-the-product-model-and-org-design|The Product Model and Org Design]] (Jul 2024).

## Why it matters
Company leaders reach for a reorg as the fix when a transformation stalls — it feels actionable and visible. Cagan's TL;DR is blunt: in most cases, **a reorg is not necessary**, and doing one anyway is usually "rearranging the deck chairs on the Titanic." Knowing the two named models (plus the hybrid) — and that the right choice is a function of *business strategy*, not of whether the org has embraced the product model — keeps a coach from letting "should we reorg?" substitute for the harder real questions: product-leader quality, team competencies, and product culture.

## How it works
- **GM model** (also called BU — business unit — or STL — single-threaded-leader): each general manager owns a product or line of business end to end. Optimizes for *speed* and spinning up a relatively large number of new products/lines of business. **Amazon** is Cagan's worked example.
- **Functional model**: engineers report to engineering managers, designers to design managers, PMs to PM managers — organized by discipline, not by product line. Optimizes for a *holistic, deeply-integrated user experience* across a smaller number of complex products. **Apple** is Cagan's worked example.
- **Hybrid**: the most common model in practice — a portfolio of loosely related products (favoring GM) that still needs the leverage of a shared platform (favoring functional). Common in companies that have grown by acquisition.
- **The driver heuristic:** business strategy → product vision/strategy → org design, in that order. A portfolio of loosely related products with their own visions and strategies favors GM. A single, tightly integrated, holistic product experience favors functional.
- **Company size correlates, loosely:** small companies skew functional; large companies (often via acquisition) skew GM or hybrid — Cagan's own estimate, from having "seen as many product organizations as anyone," is that these two models plus the hybrid cover roughly 95% of what exists.
- **Coaching implication for coaching-as-manager:** the functional model is a natural fit for "manager as coach," since functional managers share the discipline they're coaching. In a GM model without functional managers under the GM, coaching responsibility needs to be explicitly assigned to someone other than the direct manager — otherwise it silently disappears.
- **Every org design trades off differently** — same principle [[team-topology|team topology]] establishes one level down: no design is free of friction, it just relocates where the friction lands.

## Anti-patterns & misunderstandings
- **Conflating org design with team topology.** Org structure (reporting lines) and topology (product-team responsibility and collaboration) can — and often do — change independently of each other. A reorg doesn't automatically fix a topology problem, and vice versa.
- **"We need to reorg to transform."** Whether an org embraces the product model — feature teams vs. empowered product teams — is **orthogonal** to which org-design model it uses. Either org design can run either team model. Reorganizing without addressing product-leader quality, team skills, or product culture changes nothing structural about outcomes.
- **Assuming one model is intrinsically "more product."** Amazon (GM) and Apple (functional) are both exceptionally successful, both apply product-model principles fully — they simply have different business strategies driving different structural choices.
