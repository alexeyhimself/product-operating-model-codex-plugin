# Coaching Up

**Coaching up** is the IC's craft of developing their own manager — exposing information the leader might not see so their behavior can change, without a career-progression motive. Sibling concept to [[coaching-mindset]] (manager coaches IC) and to [[stakeholder-relationship-health]] (lateral peer partnership). Primary source: [[2025-09-04-idiodi-hickman-coaching-up|Product Therapy Ep 27]] with [[lea-hickman|Lea Hickman]].

## Coaching up vs managing up — the intent distinction

**Managing up** is about influencing an outcome, how you're perceived, career progression.

**Coaching up** is about **exposing information the leader might not see, so their behavior can change** — with *no career-progression motive.* Hickman: *"It has nothing to do with career progression, nothing to do with making yourself seen in a better light. It's about helping someone get exposed to information they might not be open to."*

## Why it matters

Most leaders **haven't experienced good coaching themselves**, so they don't know how to give it (see [[coaching-mindset]]). *"You can no longer be a good manager without being a good coach"* ([[bill-campbell|Bill Campbell]]). Rockstars are the most receptive to being coached — same reason the world's best athletes have multiple coaches. **Doing the job ≠ getting better at the job** (Usain Bolt still has a coach; Steph Curry has five or six).

The unspoken corporate rule: the moment you become a manager, phrases like *"I don't know / I need help / I made a mistake / I was wrong / I'm sorry"* disappear — they were normal a day before. Coaching up gives leaders permission to be human again.

## If your manager doesn't coach you

1. **Ask directly for it.** Being *"hard to coach because you're a rock star"* is real. Idiodi: *"Why does Usain Bolt need a coach? He's the fastest man on the planet."*
2. **Model out the framework you want.** Bring your leader the structure: *"you see things I don't, you have more perspective — where do you think I can get better? If we came up with a plan, what could that look like?"*
3. **Celebrate when they try.** Even if the first attempt is clumsy — *"I got tremendous value from your feedback"* plants the seed that this is positive for their career and healthy for them to do.

## Patterns of difficult managers

### The always-right manager — the jiu-jitsu move

Convert challenge into **asking them for help**: *"Help me break this down — I need more insight."* Asking for help opens them up because they feel they're helping you, while your questions create the space to expose gaps. Diagnose the root: usually insecurity, imposter syndrome, or a prior environment that measured them on being knowledgeable about everything.

Idiodi's rule: *"There's no price for being right in product work. There's just zero price."* Shift from proving-right to shared learning. *"Confidence does not equal clarity; managers may be operating from fear, not certainty."*

### The disengaged / checked-out manager

Hickman's technique:
1. **Positive intent first** — *"was there a fire? Were you pulled into something?"* Give benefit of the doubt.
2. **Name the pattern explicitly** — *"when you kicked off you said this was the most important priority; I'm confused you had to leave."*
3. **Offer a structured way to engage** — *"would it help if I went to the meeting for you? Would it help if I sent you the summary?"*

If nothing changes across sessions, **skip-level escalation is the right move** — worst case is when everyone knows and no one talks. Cagan told Hickman herself once: *"I'm surprised you have waited so long."*

## The User Manual — Idiodi's core tool

Every electronic in your house has a user manual you consult when it breaks. Yet most people have never studied the one boss they work with year after year.

**A user manual is a personal playbook: how they work, how they make decisions, how they like to communicate, how they receive feedback.** Idiodi's content list:
- Their goals — and how their manager measures *them*, so you can help them look good.
- Their bonus structure and incentives.
- Their family situation.
- Their stakeholder battles.
- Their strengths and weaknesses.
- Their development plans and OKRs.
- Their communication style — *"some leaders read no emails; unless you say it in a one-on-one it will never stick. It's a $10 lunch away from getting by, cheaper than fighting all these things."*

**Reverse it too — Idiodi-as-manager writes his own user manual and gives it to every direct report** proactively. States his weaknesses explicitly: *"I suck at responding to emails — text me if you need me."* *"Nobody hired me for my weakness. But you're going to see it. Why not put it out there?"*

**Validate the user manual you wrote about someone else** — *"I took time in my meeting — is your preference really email versus one-on-ones? Do you really get feedback this way?"*

Guard against the **v1-vs-v5 problem**: the user manual you wrote when they were version 1 stops fitting when they're version 5 — and when you're version 5 too.

## When NOT to coach up

Cagan (via Hickman): *"The most difficult thing to change in any organization is the culture."* If you've tried the techniques and the environment doesn't respond, the honest read is to consider whether you'll get what you need there. Idiodi's summary: *"at that point it's no longer about managing up, it's about managing risk."*

## The two core skills to become a better employee

- **Ask questions.**
- **Actually listen.**

Hickman: *"If you listen, people will tell you exactly who they are — but you have to listen."*

## Idiodi's closing challenge to ICs

*"There's no promise to you when you join a company that you should have a great relationship with your manager. Many people harshly judge their work environment by their poor relationship with their manager when they were not intentional about trying to shape that relationship. What the organization owes you is clarity about where we're going. What you own is shaping the relationship."*

## In your context

Ask about the specific manager the IC is trying to coach up — what have they already tried; where is the manager on the always-right / checked-out / doesn't-know-they-should-coach spectrum; do they have a user manual (or the seed of one); has the manager themselves ever received good coaching. This is where an org's [[strategic-context|strategic-context baseline]] on management culture matters.
