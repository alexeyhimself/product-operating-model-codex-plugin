# Empowered Product Teams

> A durable, cross-functional team **given problems to solve (not features to build)** and held accountable for outcomes — "the most fundamental of all product concepts" (Cagan).

## Why it matters
Everything else in the model rests on this unit. Without empowered teams you have a feature factory: teams that receive solutions to build, are measured on output, and own neither the problem nor the result. The four [[principles-of-product-teams|product-team principles]] each describe one facet of an empowered team.

## How it works
A cross-functional team (product manager, product designer, engineers — the [[the-product-team-trio|trio]], plus the full eng team) is assigned a [[team-objectives|team objective]] (a significant problem + measures of success) — via [[okrs|OKRs]], which Cagan calls "first and foremost an *empowerment* technique" ([[2020-02-24-cagan-team-objectives-overview|canon]]). The team owns both [[product-discovery|discovery]] (finding a solution worth building, against the [[the-four-big-risks|four risks]]) and [[product-delivery|delivery]] (building and shipping it), and is judged on the [[outcomes-over-output|outcome]]. It runs on [[collaboration]], not hand-offs, and depends on a culture of [[trust-over-control]].

### The trust-gap diagnosis ([[empowered|EMPOWERED]] Ch 4)
The book's founding question: *why*, given how many books argue for empowered teams, do most companies still not? The CEO answer boils down to **trust** — leaders don't believe they have the level of people needed to truly empower them. When Cagan pushes on why they don't hire people they *would* trust, the responses typically split into *"can't find, can't afford, or can't attract the level of people that Google, Amazon, Apple, and Netflix hire."*

Cagan's response is the book's thesis: *"having worked with many people at each of these companies, I point out how ordinary the vast majority of the people on these teams actually are. Maybe the important difference lies elsewhere?"* — plus the observation that many people move from ordinary companies to those exemplars and *"their performance dramatically improved in the process."* The subtitle — *Ordinary People, Extraordinary Products* — is the hypothesis in five words: strong product companies **leverage ordinary talent differently**, they don't hire different humans. Backs the "empowerment is not backing off" note further down and connects to [[product-leadership]] (better leadership, not less).

### The three team types ([[empowered|EMPOWERED]] Ch 1 + footnote 3)
Ch 1 grounds the wiki's team-type contrast in book form and adds a **third** type sharper than the feature-team framing:

- **Empowered product team** — cross-functional (PM + designer + engineers); given problems; empowered to find the solution; accountable to outcomes. The PM owns [[the-four-big-risks|value + viability]], the designer owns usability, the tech lead owns feasibility; *"together, they own the problem and are responsible and accountable for the results."*
- **Feature team** — cross-functional in headcount only; given features to build (output); PM mainly doing project management. *"These feature teams sometimes claim they're doing some product discovery, but they rarely are. They've already been told what the solution should be."*
- **Delivery team** ("Scrum team" / "dev team") — Ch 1 footnote 3 — *"doesn't even pretend to be a true product team. They are not cross-functional, and they are not empowered. There is a product owner (responsible for administering the product backlog) and some number of engineers. They are purely about output (code and ship). If you're running a process like SAFe, then this is unfortunately you, and truthfully, I have no idea why you would want to read this book."* Sharper than the feature-team framing — a delivery team isn't even cross-functional. See [[feature-teams-vs-product-teams]] for the full picture.

### Two axes (per [[2021-04-27-cagan-product-vs-project-teams|Cagan]])
- **Empowerment** (product vs. feature) — given problems, not features ([[feature-teams-vs-product-teams]]).
- **Ownership / durability** (product vs. project) — durable team (1–2 years minimum), not a pool-allocated project team.

Both must be true. Either deficit produces a feature factory.

### Why this works (per [[2022-09-20-moore-changing-how-you-solve-problems|Moore]])
- Engineers are **the single most important source of innovation** — empowered teams are designed to tap that ([[empowered-engineers]]).
- Discovery shrinks the cycle dramatically: 3–5 iterations in weeks (empowered) vs. 1–2 years (feature) — see [[time-to-money]].
- Only **10–20%** of features on typical roadmaps generate positive ROI; empowered teams pursue outcomes, not feature counts.

### In practice (case study)
The [[spotify-discover-weekly|Spotify Discover Weekly]] case is the canonical illustration of what empowerment actually requires of leaders: co-founder CEO **Daniel Ek was openly skeptical** of the idea and said so — but he **gave the team a problem, not a solution**, and let the work proceed. Empowerment = leaders own the strategy and strategic context (and the hard calls); the team owns discovering the solution, "even if the ideas weren't [the CEO's] own." A concrete marker of the real thing: any empowered Spotify team can roll an experiment to **up to 5% of users without asking permission**.

## Empowerment is not a free-for-all (Jones, 2024)

The most persistent misreading of the word, and [[chris-jones|Jones]] addresses it directly in [[2024-08-04-jones-empowered-vs-feature-teams-peter-yang|*Real Talk about Empowered vs. Feature Product Teams*]]:

> *"Empowered is a cool word, but it's a very feel-good word… people might overhear it as 'I'm empowered, I'm going to work on what I want to work on, I'm going to use my best judgment' — when in fact that's really not what we mean."*

**What is actually meant:** leadership takes an *active* role in defining the problems and allocating them onto teams — teams may have a say, but leadership is accountable, because that is how the work rolls up and stays coherent. The team's empowerment is over **the solution**.

**The over-empowered failure mode.** A team that picks its own problems as well as its own solutions produces work that is disconnected from every other team's; nothing has the leverage to move a needle. Jones's diagnosis is unsparing: this is usually **leadership abdicating**, feeling good about "getting out of the way." [[christian-idiodi|Idiodi]] makes the same correction independently in [[2023-05-30-idiodi-building-strong-product-cultures-product-unfiltered]] — the problems come from leadership, and leadership gets them from the [[product-strategy|strategy]]; then the team decides how. It is neither top-down nor bottom-up.

**The two symmetric leadership failures**, both named by Jones:
- The leader who **reverts to command and control under stress** — something goes wrong, hands go back on the wheel.
- The leader who **steps so far back** they are not close to what the team is working on at all.

> *"What we need is the leader that really knows when to lean in and how to lean in and when to step back."*

And leaders may have to steer a team somewhere it does not want to go — that is a responsibility, not a violation, because the leader holds the view across all the teams.

**The bottom-up rung.** For a PM who cannot stop being handed solutions, Jones's move is to attach an outcome to whatever arrives: *"great, you want us to ship this feature — what does success look like?"* Success is not shipping on time; it is 20% adoption in the first month and 90% of those retained. Even that much creates a level of empowerment. See [[coaching-up]].

## Anti-patterns & misunderstandings
- **Feature team / delivery team:** receives a roadmap of features, measured on shipping them. The opposite of empowered.
- **Project team / "pool model":** engineers reassigned per project; team disbands after launch; no ownership of the outcome ([[2021-04-27-cagan-product-vs-project-teams]]).
- "Empowered" misread as *unaccountable* — empowerment is inseparable from accountability for results.
- Splitting discovery and delivery across different groups, breaking [[sense-of-ownership]].
- **Titles without competencies.** Cagan ([[2023-03-15-cagan-product-model-competencies]]): many adopt the new titles without learning the new competencies — "much more severe than you might imagine."
