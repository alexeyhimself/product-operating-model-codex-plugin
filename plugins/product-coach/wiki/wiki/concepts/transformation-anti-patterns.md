# Transformation Anti-Patterns

**Transformation anti-patterns** are the recognizable failure patterns that appear over and over when organizations try to move to the [[product-operating-model|product operating model]]. Coaches recognize them because they're the reasons transformations stall, backslide, or collapse — not because of ambition or tools, but because of misapplied moves that feel right in the moment.

Primary source: [[2026-03-19-idiodi-castenfors-coaching-transformation-anti-patterns|Product Therapy Ep 38]] with [[marcus-castenfors|Marcus Castenfors]] (author of *10 Anti-Patterns When Moving to the Product Model*). Supporting: [[2026-02-19-idiodi-hickman-coaching-transformations|Ep 37]] with [[lea-hickman|Hickman]] on the messy middle.

## The loop of transformation failure

[[marcus-castenfors|Castenfors]]'s named six-step loop:

1. *Let's give teams the opportunity to innovate.*
2. *Patience is a virtue.*
3. *Are we there yet?*
4. *Houston, we have a problem — no results.*
5. *Abort mission — back to micromanagement.*
6. *Oh crap, micromanagement stifles innovation. → Loop restarts.*

Root cause of the loop: **coaches failed to teach the new capabilities; leaders stepped too far away from the teams; teams failed to be transparent about what they're working on and results.** The antidote is co-coaching of team AND leadership simultaneously (see [[pilot-teams]] with [[gabrielle-bufrem|Bufrem]] Ep 34).

## Losing momentum

The anti-pattern that stalks every transformation. *"You have doubters and you have believers. Doubters are lurking, wanting to see if this is actually going to work. The best thing you can do is show results — results that this new way of working is actually better than the old."* Meet doubters where they are — sales, marketing, ops all need to feel *what's in it for them*.

## Transformation-in-a-bubble

Transforming product-and-tech alone, without bringing sales, marketing, customer service, HR, legal, and finance along. *"Product is what you sell, what you market, what you service. If you forget about what's surrounding product and tech, you're going to fail."* Case: a company trained many teams, created new strategic context — then sales woke up one day asking *"where's my road map? What happened to my feature?"*

## Autonomy too soon

*"'We're empowered, we can do whatever we want.' What happens? Chaos."* Teams are given problems to solve, accountable to outcomes — but without the coaching, mentoring, and skill development to actually solve them. Leaders then abort back to micromanagement, starting the [[#the-loop-of-transformation-failure|loop of failure]]. **Idiodi's true test of empowerment** (a coherent set of preconditions):

1. Team has the right level and range of skills.
2. Team is given a problem, not a feature to build.
3. Team is measured on solving that problem.
4. Autonomy = leaders give context and tools to solve, then get out of the way.
5. Accountability comes with autonomy — not *"we're empowered so we work on anything."*

## The air sandwich

[[marcus-castenfors|Castenfors]]'s named concept for the gap between vision and daily work. *"Picture a sandwich filled with air. At the top: clear vision and direction from leadership. At the bottom: clarity on what we do day-to-day — what's in our backlog for the next sprint. In the middle: a big question mark. That's the air."* The product model is the way to *connect* the dots — teams need to understand how their problem-to-solve connects to something bigger.

## 17 North Stars — clarity anti-pattern

*"I heard from one organization that they had 17 North Stars. How can you make a decision on what's most important when you have 17 North Stars? It is not a North Star at that point."* Simple, storytelling-worthy strategic context that wins hearts and minds. Companion to [[#the-air-sandwich|the air sandwich]].

## OKRs as the silver bullet

*"'All right, we're just going to use OKRs. That's how we move to the product operating model.'"* Naivete of a new tool in isolation without changing the ecosystem around it. See [[okrs]] and [[team-objectives]].

## Product-management theater — installation not adoption

Hickman's frame from Ep 37. Renaming Product Owners to Product Managers, calling it done. *"A great product leader once told me that's a problem of installation, not adoption. What we're trying to drive is adoption — grow the skills, develop them, coach them, make sure we're not just changing titles."* See [[product-management-theater]].

**Note on this catalog vs. SVPG's own written taxonomy.** This page's catalog comes from Product Therapy coaching conversations (Castenfors, Hickman, Bufrem). [[transformation]] carries a parallel, earlier catalog from SVPG's own written canon — [[2024-02-27-hickman-transformation-theater|Hickman's "Transformation Theater"]] article names seven forms (Transformation, Title, Innovation, Discovery, Agile, Strategy, and Stakeholder Collaboration Theater). The two catalogs overlap in spirit — "installation not adoption" above is this page's version of what the article calls **Title Theater** — but are kept on separate pages deliberately: one is coaching-podcast voice, the other is SVPG written canon. See [[transformation#transformation-theater-the-wider-taxonomy|the full taxonomy]] rather than duplicating it here.

## Product leadership played as management

Hickman: leader takes an inventory of what all the teams are working on, wraps a story around it, calls it a product vision. *"That's bottoms-up. It's just a list and an inventory of all the things the teams are doing."* Not what leadership means — a declarative view about where you want to go and the hard calls that make it real is what leadership means.

## Underestimating protect-value work

[[marcus-castenfors|Castenfors]]'s three-buckets frame — new value / existing value / protect value. Protect-value (compliance, tech debt, bugs, operational things) is often hidden below the surface. If pilot teams are swamped with it, expectation mismatch is guaranteed. **Idiodi's first rule of tech debt: *talk about tech debt.*** Quantify what it costs the business.

## Dependencies — root of all evil

*"A team all excited about a prototype from discovery, tested on customers — then they go into delivery, start coding, and 'oh crap, we're dependent on this system and this team and this and that.'"* Slowness like tar. Delivery infrastructure is *not* optional — it's one of the three transformation dimensions (changing how you build / changing how you solve problems / changing which problems you solve).

## No vehicle for your vision

Team topology, capability, and delivery infrastructure = the *car* to reach the destination. Vision without vehicle = frustration.

## Transformation valley of despair

The middle of the arc where results haven't landed and doubters are gaining. Counters: **discovery demos** (build momentum by showing what teams learned even without shipping); **leaders moving away from their comfort zone.**

## Pilot theater / pilot-team factory / sacred golden child

[[gabrielle-bufrem|Bufrem]]'s pilot-specific anti-patterns from Ep 34 (see [[pilot-teams]]):

- **Pilot theater** — the pilot works but nothing broader changes; infinite pilot cycles.
- **Pilot-team factory** — cookie-cutter "let's have hundreds of pilot teams" kills the learning the pattern exists to create.
- **Sacred golden child** — leadership starts throwing every hard problem to the pilot team instead of scaling the way of working.

## Cagan's two lists — where the pitfalls actually come from (ProductTank Oslo, 2022)

Two years before *TRANSFORMED*, Cagan gave the fullest spoken taxonomy of transformation failure ([[2022-04-13-cagan-common-transformation-pitfalls-producttank-oslo|ProductTank Oslo]]). His organising cut is **not** by dimension but by **who causes it** — and his framing is that the first list is the harder one because most of it sits outside a product person's control. *"Most of this talk, honestly, is for CEOs."*

**External to product & technology — ten:**
1. **Addiction to command and control.** The source of waterfall and of roadmaps: leaders believing their job is to decide and pass down instructions. Netflix's *lead with context, not control* is the contrast Cagan names. Dead on arrival without an honest conversation with the CEO and C-suite.
2. **Appointing a Chief Digital Officer.** A bad sign, and Cagan says he sees it especially in Europe. Product, design and engineering will make their (substantial, but tractable) changes; the *other* stakeholders will not, and when they escalate, a CEO who has delegated this to the CDO has nothing to say. *"If this isn't driven by the top of your company it almost certainly won't happen."*
3. **Hiring McKinsey / Bain / BCG.** Board cover, millions spent, nothing useful produced. *"Probably the best example of the emperor has no clothes I've ever seen."*
4. **Sales-driven product.** Not sales' fault — they are on commission and feeding families. The symptom of a weak product organisation, and the fix is [[reference-customer-program|reference customers]] (*INSPIRED* Ch 39, named from the stage).
5. **Marketing-driven product.** Rarer now, but the same failure: customers and internal executives *do not know what is now possible*. Why focus groups don't work in tech products.
6. **CFO project-based funding.** Business cases requiring numbers nobody can know. Cagan's intervention: ask the CFO to go count how many of last year's funded business cases actually met their objectives.
7. **Predictability addiction.** *"They just want to know when… predictability is easy to get but very dangerous."*
8. **Reporting to a CIO rather than a CTO.** Cagan's sharpest structural point, and one he flags as more common in Europe: **a CIO runs a cost centre, a CTO runs a profit centre.** *"You really couldn't get much different than that."* Tech debt kills CIO-led companies more often than CTO-led ones, because a CTO treats the codebase as what powers the company.
9. **Outsourced engineering.** *"Would you outsource the CEO?"* — because innovation comes from engineers, and an outsourced engineer is not paid to suggest that the thing you asked for is a bad idea.
10. **Overwhelming tech debt.**

**Internal to product & technology — ten** (easier, because they are in your control): the desire for **process** · **inadequate product managers**, and the product-owner *title* (a role in a process is not a job) · engineers who "just want to code" (you need at least one on each team who cares what gets built) · **tools that smuggle in a process** — roadmap and OKR tools that encode someone else's way of working · too much [[keep-the-lights-on]] work (Cagan's watermark: past ~20–30% of capacity, add an engineer) · too many initiatives · too much tech debt · **too much pressure** · **more work** · **outcomes are hard**.

The last three are the honest ones, and Cagan does not soften them: an empowered team is *more* pressure and *more* stress than a feature team — by design, because ownership is the point — and some people, once they understand what is involved, rationally opt out. See [[sense-of-ownership]] and [[missionaries-vs-mercenaries]].

## The golden rule

[[marcus-castenfors|Castenfors]]'s closing frame — **meet them where they are + show results + name the anti-patterns as they appear so people don't slip into them unconsciously.**

## In your context

Every org will have its own live subset. When coaching, look for: which loop-of-failure step is playing out right now; is there an air sandwich (test with a skip-level *"what's the strategy?"* — the [[strategic-context#the-grandfather-test|grandfather test]]); is the transformation in a bubble; is autonomy running ahead of capability; is the pilot getting sanctified instead of scaled.
