# Imposter Syndrome

> **Imposter syndrome** = the nagging fear that you don't deserve to be in the job you're in, and that someone is going to find out. In product work, a **small dose is healthy** — it prevents winging things and pushes you to do your homework. A **large dose is paralyzing** — it stops you taking the risks the job requires and traps you in avoidance. From [[chris-jones|Chris Jones]] and [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] on *Product Therapy* Ep 6: *"I've come around to believing that everybody has it to one degree or another — some more, some less; for some it becomes a real positive force, for others it just gets in the way."*

## Why it matters
Imposter syndrome is the most common invisible ceiling on a product person's growth. It's not a moral failing and it's not rare — Jones and Idiodi both share stories of paralyzing episodes at 23 (Idiodi: $26M lost across 17 failed experiments) and mid-career (Jones: destroying leadership confidence in a single strategy meeting by mumbling through a follow-up question he didn't understand). The healthy version is *the impulse that makes you do the homework* — [[product-sense|product sense]] is earned partly *through* imposter syndrome. The paralyzing version is *the avoidance* — a PM who won't do discovery because they're afraid of what they'll find; a strategy lead who won't take a position because they're afraid of being wrong; a leader who won't publicly say *"I don't know"* because they're afraid of being seen as incompetent.

## How it works — two signals worth distinguishing
Jones's diagnostic (Ep 6):
- **Signal 1 — is it telling you something real?** *"You haven't thought this through, you haven't done your homework, you don't believe yourself yet."* This is the healthy dose; the response is do the homework.
- **Signal 2 — is it irrational?** *"Overestimating the worthiness of the others around you, underestimating your own."* This is the paralyzing dose; the response is coaching, not more homework.

Common surface symptoms: **Sunday scaries** (dreading Monday); the **unnecessarily-long preparation** loop (Idiodi's three months of over-planning as a proxy for not acting); the **fake-through-a-question** trap (Jones's mumbled strategy answer); the **wing-it-and-hope-nobody-notices** pattern in leaders who never earned depth in a specific domain.

## How to coach through it — Ep 6 moves
- **Model vulnerability first** (Idiodi + Jones): *"you're humanizing that other people also have this — most people who feel it think they're alone."* A leader who admits their own imposter moments deflates the "I'm the only one" story.
- **Coach into the fear, not around it.** Jones's story: his manager gave him a *"write a definitive point of view in prose on two strategy topics — there is no right answer, but you have to defend it and handle objections."* Coach the specific competency the fear is protecting, and the fear shrinks.
- **The "sign up for six before you do one" hack.** Jones's onboarding at SVPG: rather than *"try one workshop, see how it goes,"* he was booked for six back-to-back. Counterintuitively comforting — takes the pressure off any single attempt, converts the fear of failing *this* one into practice reps.
- **Reciprocity upward — coach the boss.** Idiodi's CEO exercise: a 16-year founder-CEO went through a coaching drill where a report several levels down actually coached him. Emotional take-away: *"in 16 years, nobody has ever asked me what I needed."* The most senior people carry the biggest imposter syndrome and the least permission to admit it — coaching that upward is a real service.
- **Reduce the anticipation, not the reality.** Jones's closing insight: *"the anxiety of anticipating something is so much greater than the reality."* Get engaged with the task quickly — the imposter feeling collapses fastest once you're in motion.

## Where it clusters in product
- **Non-technical founders** starting a tech company.
- **PMs new to platform, data, or AI-heavy products** where technical depth feels non-negotiable.
- **Women in product leadership**, especially at levels where they're often the only one in the room (empirical pattern; not a claim these are the only affected people).
- **Newly-promoted managers** — the day-of-promotion trap where *"the only difference between you last week and this week was that you announced it on LinkedIn — but now you're supposed to have all the answers."*
- **PMs joining without a strong manager to coach them.** See [[coaching-the-pm]].

## Anti-patterns & misunderstandings
- **Treating imposter syndrome as a personal defect.** It is nearly universal; the coaching move is normalization + practice, not fixing.
- **Confusing over-preparation with rigor.** Endless planning is often imposter syndrome in disguise — the tell is that no action follows the plan.
- **Silencing it entirely.** A small dose of imposter syndrome is diagnostic — it's telling you where you still need to do the work.
- **Coaching it in a group meeting.** Like [[coaching-agency|agency]], this is 1:1 work; nobody admits it in a group.
- **Leaving senior leaders alone with it.** The higher you go, the less permission you have to say *"I don't know"* — and the more the imposter feeling compounds. Someone has to reciprocate the coaching upward.

## Jones's plainest version (PMDojo, 2024)

Alongside the *Product Therapy* Ep 6 material above, [[chris-jones|Jones]] gives an unguarded personal account in [[2024-04-05-jones-product-power-play-pmdojo]]:

> *"Especially early in my career, a lot of what drove me was **fear** more than anything else — a lot of anxiety, a lot of imposter syndrome. As I've gotten older, and especially as I've been a manager at varying levels, I've just learned that everybody has that to some degree."*

His advice to his younger self is not a technique but a permission: **do not be afraid to reveal a lack of knowledge or a point of view**, even in a job where you are supposed to have one — just be clear when you ask that you do not have it *yet*. *"People are generally, for the most part, more flattered than anything else that you're asking them for their input."*

And the closing framing, which is the useful one for a coach:

> *"There's a little bit of liberation in there. Everybody has it. Even your manager has it. **Even — especially — the most confident-seeming person in your organization.** They've just come up with a bold way of coping with it."*

The device he pairs with it is **"what would you do?" rather than "what should I do?"** — you still get the input, you stay allowed to be vulnerable and undecided, and the decision stays yours. See [[coaching-up]] and [[decision-making]].
