# Innovation over Predictability

> Optimize the culture for **innovation**, not for delivering a predictable feature schedule — because "100% predictability = 0% innovation."

## The belief behind it
Organizations fixated on shipping as many features as predictably as possible crowd out innovation. Cagan quotes Henrik Kniberg: "100% predictability = 0% innovation." And: "Companies that focus on predictability will tell you it's the stakeholder's job to worry about whether features are valuable... But strong product companies know... it goes to the nature of technology-powered products — stakeholders and customers don't know what's possible. It is the engineers who know what is just now possible" (*TRANSFORMED*). **High-integrity commitments** (real dates when truly needed) should be the **exception, not the rule** — see [[2024-03-21-huryn-product-model-first-principles-part-2]].

**Cagan's 2026 self-critique — he under-weighted how deep the pull is.** In [[2026-09-11-cagan-strong-opinions-loosely-held|*Strong Opinions, Loosely Held*]] Cagan lists this among his ten biggest regrets: *"One of my biggest mistakes was that I simply didn't appreciate just how powerful and deeply rooted the desire for predictability was — from both product people and company leaders — and how at odds this is with not just humility, but with innovation and outcomes."* His mechanism names the two big artifacts directly: **roadmaps** (what features, when) and **PRDs** (the requirements) are *"an enabler for thinking we know more than we really do."* And he locates predictability at the **root** of two failure modes the wiki treats elsewhere: the persistent focus on **output** (see [[outcomes-over-output]]) and the **lack of trust** those output-obsessed companies suffer. His conclusion restates the principle in his own words: *"Predictability has its place, but not at the expense of outcomes or trust."*

## What it looks like when followed
The org accepts uncertainty as the price of innovation; engineers' knowledge of "what's now possible" shapes what gets built; date commitments are rare, deliberate high-integrity commitments rather than the default operating mode.

## What violating it looks like
The feature factory dressed as discipline: every team committed to a packed quarterly roadmap with dates; innovation squeezed out; "predictability" treated as the highest virtue and stakeholders left to worry about value.

## "Your ROI estimates are nonsense" — and the stakeholders know it
The most direct attack in the canon on the artifact that carries the predictability demand, from [[2026-07-22-cagan-ai-is-helping-bad-companies-fail-faster|Cagan, Jul 2026]]:

> *"I was taught this principle as: **you need to know what you can't know, and you need to admit what you don't know**. And what are the things you cannot know? I would argue **your ROI estimates are nonsense** — because it depends entirely on the solution your team actually comes up with. If the solution they come up with is mediocre, most likely you'll have a very hard time selling it and your revenue numbers will be all fantasy. On the other hand, if they solve it really well, your numbers will prove very pessimistic."*

The point is not that the estimators are careless; it is that **the number is downstream of work that has not happened yet**. Three things follow:

- **The estimates are known fiction on both sides of the table.** *"When the CEO says they want ROI projections to decide what to invest in, **their stakeholders lie to them. I've been in that game. I've lied that way too.**"* And the reason nobody says so: *"the CEO will say 'of course not' — but you're not getting funding either."*
- **The fix belongs to the leader, not the team.** *"This is where the leaders need to say: **don't even make up those numbers. Go run experiments and show me the evidence.**"* If the demand stays, the fiction stays.
- **The respectable precedent is the VC.** *"A VC is basically doing what the leader of a company does, just for a portfolio… They give a little bit of money — seed money — for you to try out an idea, and if that idea goes well they love the idea of giving you real money. They are **not** believing an ROI claim from some person pitching them."*

**Why the estimates fail so badly rather than a little.** *"If the product isn't very good, it's not like 50% of what you hoped for buys it — maybe **5%** buys it. Most products make virtually nothing, except the good products, which make a boatload."* A wildly non-linear payoff curve is exactly the shape a point estimate cannot represent — which is the case for [[placing-bets|portfolio thinking]] rather than per-item forecasting.

**What to offer instead of a fabricated number.** A [[high-integrity-commitments|high-integrity commitment]] once discovery has produced evidence — and before that, the staged version Cagan describes: *"is this a problem worth solving? … If you then do some product discovery and see you can come up with a good solution, you can say: this team, they've got something here. **Let's underwrite this team.**"*

## Tensions & trade-offs
- Predictability isn't worthless — some commitments (partners, contracts, regulation) genuinely need dates; the principle is that these are **exceptions**, made as high-integrity commitments, not the norm.
- Built on [[placing-bets]] (you can't guarantee outcomes on a schedule) and protected by [[learning-over-failure]].
