# Decision Making

> How individuals, teams, and leaders in the product operating model make decisions — team-level and PM-level, distinct from the executive-partnering *Decisions* element on [[partnering-with-executives]].

## When to use it
Every time a product team, a PM, a designer, a tech lead, or a product leader needs to make a call — from *"should I take this meeting"* to *"which of these two solutions do we build"* to *"should we pivot the product strategy."* The frame below adapts across scale, from the smallest sprint call to the largest bet-the-company move.

## The framework
[[martina-lauchengco|Lauchengco]] anchors this teaching on [[2025-05-01-idiodi-lauchengco-coaching-decision-making|Product Therapy Ep 19]] from her organizational-behavior background + Costanoa Ventures operating-partner lens. The framework has six elements:

### 1. High-integrity decisions
The SVPG standard: **reasonable, data-informed, rational, defensible — regardless of the outcome.** The team + leaders can *respect the rationale even when they disagree*. A decision's value is *relative to the point in time it was made*, based on the information available then — you'll get some things wrong in retrospect, and that does not mean you used the wrong framework.

**Reassurance for empowered teams that fear making empowered decisions:** *"you're empowered to make the most reasonable decision at that point in time relative to the information you have. Some will look bad in retrospect. That does not mean you used the wrong framework."*

### 2. Context: is the decision or the *how* more important?
- **Startup / small team** = making a decision (any decision) beats being perfect. Clock ticking; imperfect action beats analysis paralysis.
- **Big org** = HOW you make it is everything. *"If it's politically unsupported, it doesn't matter if it was the best decision in the world."* Lauchengco's Andreessen-Horowitz story: *"I don't like it, what else you got?"* — Horowitz just said yes; data didn't matter.

### 3. Collaboration ≠ consensus (SVPG canon)
- Not voting (majority rules).
- Not one dictator (trumping vote).
- **Team-sport deference to expertise** on the relevant axis — engineering on feasibility; design on usability; PM on value + viability; market-side voices on positioning.
- Amazon's *"disagree and commit"* is the norm — consider fully, then move.

### 4. Data-informed vs data-driven — the crucial distinction
- Data reflects the **past**; product decisions impact the **future**.
- Data should not *make* the decision; a human should.
- **AI flattens to averages; the exceptions are where the signal is.** Lauchengco: *"the 2s tell you what's broken; the 10s just pat you on the back. We as decision-makers have to know the 2s matter more."*

### 5. Gut feel is informed intuition
- Trust it when it's built on years of data + product sense + business sense — that's [[founder-style-leadership]].
- Distrust it when it's ungrounded — the founder who says *"I've been in the business a long time, I know what customers want"* but hasn't touched the technology.
- Lauchengco's bias for early-stage: trust *market-context* intuition more than product-centric intuition (see [[product-in-startups]]).

### 6. Idiodi's coaching test questions
When someone brings you a decision for evaluation, ask:
1. Is it good for the customer?
2. Is it good for the business?
3. Does it go against your morals / values / integrity?
4. Is it solving a real problem?

**Idiodi's crispest single question:** *"what must be true for this decision to succeed?"* — beats *"what has worked somewhere else."*

## Tools within the framework
- **Amazon's one-way vs two-way doors** as the reversibility test. Two-way = default to speed; one-way = default to depth of analysis.
- **Cagan's relative-prioritization framing** (via Lauchengco): no absolute; goals are relatively weighted; the revenue-vs-retention example — companies chase revenue when *retained customers ARE revenue*, and the whole downstream cascade of decisions changes.
- **Strategy provides guardrails; prioritization becomes implicit.** *"Strategy should be broad enough that I can decide whether to accept this meeting invitation from Christian relative to my priorities."* Bad prioritization frameworks (RICE, ICE by themselves) are often an excuse for not making the strategy call.

## Inputs & outputs
- **Inputs:** the decision to be made; the goals + relative weights; what data / evidence is available (and what isn't); which stakeholders have voice vs vote; time pressure.
- **Outputs:** a decision; a reasoning trail others can respect (even when they disagree); commitment (per "disagree and commit"); a check on whether this was one-way or two-way.

## Pitfalls
- **Waiting for perfect information.** In most cases the information you need to move never fully arrives.
- **Chasing rational frameworks in irrational-actor environments.** Lauchengco's own Andreessen-Horowitz reminder: even the best-argued analysis loses to founder rejection.
- **Data-driven-ness.** Data flattens; averages hide the exceptions that carry the signal; the past does not fully predict the future.
- **Delegating to AI.** LLMs are *research assistants in your pocket* (great for gathering, summarizing, analyzing). Never delegate the decision itself. See §5 above.
- **Agentic AI over-delegation.** Micro-decisions inside a proven strategy (which segment gets which campaign) are delegatable. Strategic decisions (positioning, 6-month market direction) are not.
- **Substituting prioritization frameworks for strategy calls.** RICE/ICE without a strategy is a way to defer the real call.
- **Senior-leader blind spots.** *"The more senior you are, the more your blind spots have impact and the less visible they are to you."* Get a coach; you're already underutilising them.
- **The easier-obvious-choice reflex.** Am I making the easier choice here? = the first blind-spot check.

## Example
A PM inherits a stakeholder request to build a feature they think is wrong for the customer. High-integrity decision-making would:
1. Test which context they're in — is the stakeholder likely to accept a *"here's my reasoning, I want to make a different call"* response? (In some orgs yes; in some no — the *how* matters more than the decision.)
2. Consider whether to run a test (data-informed) vs decide with existing information (judgment).
3. Ask *"what must be true for this feature to succeed?"* — often this exposes assumptions the stakeholder hasn't tested.
4. If they proceed against the stakeholder: name the decision explicitly, share the reasoning, commit to the outcome. Do not silently deprioritize.
