# Time Management (for Product People)

> How product people (and their coaches) protect the time that actually matters — the [[product-discovery|discovery]] time that never feels urgent — from the tide of urgent-but-less-important work that flows in every day. From [[chris-jones|Chris Jones]] and [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] on *Product Therapy* Ep 11. The single-line thesis: **urgent will always win over important unless you make time for the important on purpose.**

## Why it matters
Two symptoms bring this framework to the surface constantly:
- *"I'm working 12-hour days and I still can't get to Discovery — I get to my four hours after work, from 6 to 10 pm."* (Real quote; Ep 11.)
- Product people whose calendars are packed with meetings all week and who genuinely couldn't tell you what they *decided* or *shipped* that week.

Neither is a personal-productivity problem in isolation — both are symptoms of **operating-model and culture** issues: an org that runs on meetings because it doesn't trust the team, hasn't given the team context, and hasn't given the team discovery. See [[trust-over-control]] and [[principles-over-process]].

## The Eisenhower matrix (Ep 11)
Jones's foundational teaching from a Covey Group workshop early in his career:

|                   | **Urgent**       | **Not Urgent**            |
| ----------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------- |
| **Important**     | Q1: fires        | **Q2: the real job**      |
| **Not Important** | Q3: interruptions| Q4: waste                 |

The claim: *urgent always wins over important* unless you intentionally protect Q2. Worse, there is a **seduction in the urgent** — it produces visible closure, someone gets off your back, and you get to feel busy — even though you moved nothing that matters. Q2 is where product [[product-discovery|discovery]] lives, and Q2 is what disappears first.

## Culture problem or coaching problem?
Ep 11 makes an important distinction: excessive urgency is either a **coaching problem** (this person doesn't know how to protect Q2) or an **operating-model problem** (the whole company runs this way). The tells for the operating-model version: elevated process, standing meetings, checkpoint rituals, IT-style stakeholder management, teams not empowered to decide, teams without enough strategic context. Fix the operating model or the individual coaching won't stick.

Idiodi's three cultural drivers of a *"meetings culture"* worth naming:
1. **Low trust** — I don't know what you're working on, so I need a meeting to check in.
2. **Little/no context** — everything feels important, so I attend everything.
3. **No discovery** — we're discovering during delivery, in meetings.

Fix any of the three and the meeting count drops on its own.

## The pie-chart exercise (Ep 11 coaching move)
A single, high-leverage 1:1 coaching move:

1. Ask the person to draw a pie chart of **how they actually spent last week** — five or six slices, look-at-your-calendar precision, no spreadsheet.
2. Insist on the *actual* pie chart, not the *wished-for* pie chart.
3. Talk through the gap.

Jones's canonical example: a PM who thought he was *"spending all this time on Discovery"* but whose pie chart showed the *"customer time"* slice was 100% in-deal and 0% out-of-deal. That conversation was the coaching moment — cap the in-deal slice at 20%, and now something has to go into the out-of-deal slice.

## The 4 types of meetings (Chris Jones)
- **Communicate status** — ask whether it can be a written update. The best offline is more efficient than the meeting.
- **Make a decision** — use the Amazon 6-page written-narrative discipline (silent read, then discuss); most objections are already handled in the doc.
- **Solve a problem** — get a small number of smart people together; this is the one meeting type you should not eliminate.
- **Build relationship** — the fourth type is a **remote-work-only necessity** — the spontaneous corridor conversation is gone, and if you don't protect a channel for relationship time, it doesn't happen.

## Idiodi's 7 meeting rules (culture play, Ep 11)
- **Kill the one-hour default.** Change every meeting on the calendar to 20 minutes. Add time only where the calibration proves you need it. (Parkinson's Law — work expands to fill the time.)
- **Start at odd times** (8:47 am, 1:55 pm). Breaks the back-to-back cascade; forces attendees to actually notice the meeting.
- **Require a written narrative before you call one.** Amazon's 6-page memo culture: *"if you have to write six pages before you call a meeting, you will have fewer meetings."*
- **Cap attendees around 8.** Bain research: each additional attendee past ~8 reduces decision effectiveness by ~10%. Social loafing kicks in.
- **Agenda + purpose, or decline.** *"There is a magical button in your calendar many people have not discovered. It is called decline."*
- **Meetings ≠ work.** Talking became work — mostly because it filled the vacuum from broken trust, missing context, or no discovery.
- **Fix the three cultural drivers.** The meetings culture is downstream — the fixes are upstream: trust, context, discovery. See Idiodi's three drivers above.

## Discovery is your day job, not your side hustle
Ep 11's cleanest reframe: for a PM or a designer, **discovery is the day job**; for engineers, discovery is the side hustle (their day job is delivery). The ~4-hour-a-day discovery guideline gets restated here — that does not mean *"user testing four hours a day."* Discovery is the conversations with the tech lead, the stakeholders, the writing that becomes the story, the prototyping that becomes the acceptance test. Not all of it is in front of customers; all of it is upstream of delivery.

**The zero-sum misread.** New adopters of discovery often hear it as *"now I have to do 4 hours of discovery on top of my full delivery job."* That misunderstands what good discovery does — it *shrinks* delivery work (fewer things built, iterated cheaply in prototypes, decided before they hit engineering). Discovery done well is a net reduction in total effort, not an addition.

## Proactive vs reactive time
Idiodi's diagnostic frame: **do you feel more like a firefighter or a physician?** — a firefighter reacts, a physician diagnoses. When most of your week is meetings + email + chat, you're reactive; when most of it is discovery, thinking, and deciding, you're proactive. The pie chart makes the ratio visible.

## Anti-patterns & misunderstandings
- **Meetings-as-status-badge.** *"How many meetings do you have today?"* as a proxy for importance. Reversed causality — the leaders you most want to work for have the fewest meetings.
- **Discovery as sequential to delivery.** Discovery-during-delivery is what causes the meeting flood.
- **Fixing individual time management in a broken operating model.** The individual coaching can't outrun the operating-model gravity; fix them in the right order.
- **Confusing collaboration meetings with status meetings.** *"There's a difference between meetings and work."* Working sessions that produce artifacts or decisions are not the problem; status meetings that don't produce either are.
- **Airplane-mode as a hack, not a discipline.** Idiodi's closing coaching tip is real — but airplane-mode-when-you-need-to-think is a bandage on a scheduling problem. Schedule the thinking time; don't hide from the noise.
