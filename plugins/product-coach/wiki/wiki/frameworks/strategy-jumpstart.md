# Strategy Jumpstart

The **Strategy Jumpstart** is the SVPG method [[lea-hickman|Lea Hickman]] uses to help organizations *create or refine their product strategy in the room* — putting SVPG's master-class material into practice for one company's specific business and product. Not a strategy offsite; not annual planning; a *product* strategy exercise, one level below the corporate/business strategy.

Primary source: [[2026-07-16-idiodi-hickman-strategy-jumpstarts|Product Therapy Ep 42]] with Lea Hickman (the deep dive). First-named: [[2026-02-19-idiodi-hickman-coaching-transformations|Ep 37]].

## What it IS

Hickman: *"An opportunity for an organization to work with an SVPG partner to either create or refine their overall product strategy. Getting the right people in the room, gathering all the insights they have — telemetry, financial numbers, industry insights, user insights — bringing them together and finding areas of connection. Then making really hard decisions about where we're going to focus and quantifying what success looks like."*

Assumes the **corporate/business strategy is already laid out** (what we sell, what markets, what profitability targets). The Jumpstart works one level below — the [[product-strategy|product strategy]].

## Who's in the room

- **Head of product** (CPO).
- **Head of engineering / CTO** (technology decisions).
- **Head of design** (portfolio-fit decisions).
- **Key go-to-market stakeholders** — product marketing, sales — because *"a key part of your product strategy is not only building it right and solving a problem for the customer, but also being able to bring that solution to market."*

**No more than 8–10 people.** *"People who can sit around a conference table."*

**All decision-makers.** *"What you don't want is people who then have to take the output and go get approval. You want the decision-makers in the room."*

**Product teams don't attend by default.** Only if the strategy area is one where leadership doesn't have the required depth. But *product teams are the primary source of the insights that feed the room* — their experiments, learnings, and qualitative discovery are present through their artifacts even when they aren't.

## The homework — before the Jumpstart

Gather all the insights: **data (telemetry, financials), industry, user, competitive.** Then Hickman works through the insights ahead of the meeting — which is also **diagnostic**:

*"How much muscle memory or skill does the collective leadership team have around identifying areas of connection and delivering artifacts that expose those insights? So the pre-work also becomes a learning opportunity. The output of a product vision or strategy is only as good as the inputs — and the insights are those inputs."*

Bias tends to expose itself in the pre-work; a good outside coach spots it before the room does.

## Why focus is the hardest part

*"Not all ideas and not all problems to solve yield the same result for your customer and the business. Peanut-butter product strategy is the same as not having a strategy."* See [[product-strategy]] for the SVPG canonical treatment.

## In-the-room moves

### False constraints

*"If you could draw the line — let's say you had limited resources. Assume you don't have 500 engineers or 50 product teams. What if you only had 10? Where would you focus?"* Force a real prioritization by imposing a constraint the org doesn't actually have.

### Question the sacred

*"A particular team might already be thinking about the solution because they have a team focused on fixing it — and instead of letting them focus on a higher-order bit, they want to keep the sacred team off to the side. It's about questioning things that have never been questioned. As an outsider, it's easier — sometimes it's naive, sometimes it's just being open without fear of political repercussions."*

### Tumbling the rocks

*"You can't do this via an artifact — you need to be in the room. We always talk about **tumbling the rocks** — we need to tumble those rocks together, because there's going to be friction, and that's OK. It's OK to have that constructive friction."*

### Guarding against weaponized metrics

*"The implied agreement going in is take our bias, put it to the side. Use an open mind. This isn't about your pet projects or your organization — this is about how we identify the biggest problems to solve within this period."*

## The outputs — what good alignment looks like coming out

**A prioritized list of problems to solve** — and for each, **a quantifiable measure of success.**

Directional measures (*"increase adoption"*) are easy — *quantifying* takes work. *"I might think 75% is success but the CFO knows success is 85%. Aligning on that magnitude is critical. It's when we start identifying — 'OK, to get to 85% we'll have to let go of some things not on the prioritized list.' That's where the rubber hits the road."*

## Communicating after the Jumpstart

**Broadly and often.** *"Every time you have the meeting, every time you bring people together, put it in a place where everyone has access — not just the problem to solve, but how we're measuring success and how you track that measurement as time goes on. The repetitive evangelism of the product strategy is almost as important as the choices."*

**One priority list across the org.** *"The head of engineering, head of product, head of design, head of sales — we're all aligned on the product strategy. We don't have our own definition of what that is."* Especially where technical challenges exist — one priority list, held by the CTO and CPO together.

**Idiodi's individual-alignment check.** *"Nobody raises their hand and says 'that's a dumb strategy.' Once you have alignment, go through your next one-on-one — 'tell me what the priorities are.' See how well your communication has made its way down."*

## Communicating the "you're not on the priority list"

*"The communication piece seems the most difficult for senior leaders — there's concern that if a team is not represented in the top priorities, they'll believe the team is not valuable to the business."* Coaching frame: *"just because you're not on the priority list doesn't mean you're not important — if you weren't important you wouldn't be funded. It just means that's not how the organization is measuring success this period."*

## Cadence — holding focus as priorities shift

*"The only thing that should change your strategy is data that warrants a change. Have flexibility for existential crises or genuinely new data — a new competitor, a new startup gets funded, a new technology change. When that happens you may be warranted to revisit."*

Otherwise: hold the line.

## In your context

The org's business strategy, current insights, and last quarter's results are the inputs. The specific room composition, sacred teams, and bias patterns to guard against live in that org's own context. When coaching a company through a first Jumpstart, walk the sequence: gather insights → pre-diagnose bias → 8–10 decision-makers → tumble the rocks together → prioritized problems + quantified success measures out → communicate broadly + check individually → hold the line unless new data warrants change.
