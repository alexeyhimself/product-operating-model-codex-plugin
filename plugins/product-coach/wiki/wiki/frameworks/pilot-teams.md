# Pilot Teams

> The canonical SVPG technique for transforming to the product model: instead of a company-wide rollout, pick a small number of teams, set them up the new way, and let them produce a visible business outcome. **Risk-mitigation tool, political tool, and proof-of-concept all at once** ([[2016-11-03-jones-pilot-teams|Jones]] · [[2024-10-29-cagan-transformation-as-a-project|Cagan]] · [[2024-11-21-cagan-transformation-politics|Cagan]]).

## Lineage
[[2016-11-03-jones-pilot-teams|Chris Jones's 2016 article]] is the **origin** of pilot teams as SVPG canon and the piece both later Cagan articles cite. Jones's four selection criteria — **People** (right skills, right mindset, *enthusiastic* about the change), **Location** (physically co-located, in 2016 terms; the durable point is *close cross-functional collaboration*), **Charter** (scope framed in **business outcomes** not features), **Autonomy** (dependencies minimized so the pilot tests the model, not the surrounding org) — plus the "one-to-two-quarter run, **insulated from legacy processes**" cadence, are the *method*. [[2024-11-21-cagan-transformation-politics|Cagan's 2024 political-selection checklist]] (ambition-ceiling / manager-support / tech-debt / skills) is a **refinement** on the People-and-Charter criteria for high-stakes transformations — not a replacement. Use Jones for the mechanics; add Cagan when the political weather demands it. [[2025-07-14-cagan-the-politics-of-pilot-teams|Cagan's dedicated 2025 written article]] is now the wiki's **primary root** for the staffing and problem-scoping mechanics below — re-rooted from the looser, chronologically-earlier Ep 14 podcast treatment, consistent with the wiki's standing article-over-podcast convention. Ep 14 stays as supporting for its own distinct contribution: the four-check political-selection vocabulary and the Trainline/Moore worked example.

## When to use it
- Your org is starting (or restarting) a transformation to the product model.
- Leadership has aligned on the destination but not on *how*; pilots are the safest way to learn the implications.
- You need to **win hearts and minds** — pilots demonstrate "what good looks like" to skeptical stakeholders and executives.
- You suspect the org is at risk of running [[transformation-as-a-project|transformation as a project]] — pilots are the antidote.

## Steps
1. **Pick the people deliberately.** Hand-pick the [[the-product-team-trio|trio]]: a PM with real value/viability skill, a [[product-designer|designer]] with usability + holistic-CX ownership, a Tech Lead with the autonomy and disposition to be a [[empowered-engineers|missionary]]. Cagan: strong product leaders hand-pick the members.
2. **Pick the problem deliberately.** Not so simple that the product model isn't needed (no one will be impressed). Not so dependent on un-done tech-debt replatforming that the team can't move. The problem must be **meaningful to the business** (a business outcome), not just nice for customers.
3. **Pick the outcome and measure.** What business KPI will move? How will you measure it? *Before* the pilot starts.
4. **Size the team small.** Smaller teams are easier and faster on every axis — role clarity, coaching, communication, collaboration, decision-making, cross-team dependencies. Large pilot rosters signal cost-insensitivity to stakeholders.
5. **Train and coach.** Members need training on the model before the pilot starts, and coaching available throughout. Without coaching, pilots regress to feature-team behavior under stress.
6. **Trust stakeholders on the problem.** Stakeholders have lived with these problems for years. Pilot teams that spend months on problem discovery before solving are politically corrosive. Trust the stakeholder framing; race to discover a successful **solution** ([[2024-11-21-cagan-transformation-politics|canon]]).
7. **Establish a drumbeat of outcomes.** Monthly minimum. Real business results, not activity reports. Share failures as well as successes.
8. **Then scale** — second pilot, third pilot, more teams. Don't pretend one pilot is the transformation; it's the **first** demonstration, not the destination.

## Inputs & outputs
- **In:** leadership alignment on transformation; a discrete business problem; trio + leadership ready to be coached; training/coaching budget.
- **Out:** a visible business outcome attributable to the pilot; concrete learnings about what the model means for *your* org (the abstract becomes tangible); proof-of-concept the rest of the org has watched.

## Pitfalls
- **Picking a too-easy problem.** Stakeholders shrug; the pilot proves nothing.
- **Picking a too-hard problem.** Pilot fails for reasons unrelated to the model (e.g. replatforming dependency). Sets the transformation back.
- **No measurable business outcome.** "Improved morale" or "shipped faster" without a real KPI move is hard to defend to skeptics.
- **No coaching available.** Pilot teams regress under stress. A [[product-coach|coach]] (internal or external) needs to be reachable for the first months.
- **Large team that looks expensive.** Stakeholders perceive the model as adding overhead. Keep it small.
- **Long problem-discovery phase.** Cagan: trust the stakeholder framing of the problem; race to a solution. If the problem framing is wrong, you'll see it in solution testing within days, not months.
- **Treating the pilot as the whole transformation.** The pilot is a learning device. After it produces outcomes, the next question is what changes for the rest of the org — not "are we done?"
- **No senior sponsor.** Pilots without executive cover get squeezed by the larger org's funding, hiring, and review processes.

## How it relates to transformation politics
Pilot teams are the **political mechanism** of transformation. They:
- Let skeptical executives see *what good looks like* without committing the whole org.
- Give the product leader a place to grow trust before exposing more product managers (the [[product-leadership|product leader is judged by her weakest product manager]]).
- Concentrate the risk of early failure on a contained team rather than across the company.
- Produce the outcomes that fund continued investment in the transformation.

## The political-selection criteria (Product Therapy Ep 14 · Cagan)
[[2025-02-06-idiodi-cagan-coaching-transformation-politics|Ep 14 (Cagan)]] adds a crisp four-check list for pilot-team selection beyond the "problem worth solving" test:

1. **Ambition ceiling** — ambitious enough that people can't say *"we could have done that in our old way of working"*; not so hard that success is impossible. (*"Triple the business in a quarter"* is the fail-side; incremental optimization is the fail-side too.)
2. **Manager-support check** — the pilot team members' *managers* must be supportive. Some managers are; some are less so; check before you pick people out from under them.
3. **Tech-debt check** — if the pilot depends on a tech-debt issue that won't be addressed for a year, you've set the team up to fail. Check the infrastructure the pilot needs.
4. **Skills check** — do you have people with the necessary skills, or will you have to hire them first (which changes the timeline dramatically)?

**The Jonathan Moore / Trainline story in Cagan's voice** — the canonical worked example. Moore picked his best PM, designer and engineers, put them on a new native mobile app (Trainline didn't even have one — a genuine leadership gap), and told the team explicitly: *"we need to show the rest of our own organization what good looks like."* Moore knew from prior industry-leading mobile-app work that this team could succeed at this problem. The pilot became the "prize at the end of the road" that motivated the rest of the transformation. See [[trainline-transformation]] for the fuller story.

## The Datasite One near-fail (Product Therapy Ep 24 · Cullen · Fredell)
[[2025-07-24-idiodi-cullen-fredell-coaching-transformation-datasite|Ep 24 (Cullen + Fredell)]] surfaces what happens when a first pilot *misses* its ambition ceiling — the Datasite One M&A platform's first customer deal in Ireland went badly ("nowhere effing near ready"). The recovery: 3-day company offsite → October launch. The lesson is not that pilots should be less ambitious — it's that the **culture bank** (see [[transformation]]) has to be full enough to survive a pilot failure. First pilots that "just fail without recovery" are usually pilots where the trust deposits weren't in place ahead of time.

## Scaling beyond the pilot — each business unit is its own transformation ([[2025-07-14-cagan-the-politics-of-pilot-teams|Cagan, 2025]])
If the first pilot succeeds, the natural next request is a company-wide rollout. Cagan's 2025 article adds a point not previously on this page: in a large company, **treat each business unit as essentially its own transformation** — and run pilot teams for each. This is especially true when the business units are the product of earlier acquisitions, where trust, culture, and credibility do not automatically transfer across the acquisition boundary. One successful pilot in one part of the company does not automatically buy trust in another.
