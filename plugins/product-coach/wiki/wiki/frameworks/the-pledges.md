# The Pledges

> **SVPG canon.** Three Cagan articles (Sep 2022 – Jun 2023) that state, in the team's own voice, what an [[empowered-product-teams|empowered product team]] commits to each outward constituency — and what it needs back. Together they are the closest thing SVPG has published to a **social contract for empowerment**.

## Why this exists

Most writing about moving to the product model describes changes *inside* the product organization. The pledges address the part that is usually left implicit: **empowerment is a renegotiation with people outside the team**, and those people experience the change too. A customer used to naming features to a salesperson, a stakeholder used to commissioning work, an executive used to asking for dates — each is being asked to give something up. The pledges name what they get in return.

Cagan's framing of the customer's position generalises to all three: they are open to a change in dynamics **only if they believe they're more likely to get their needs met, not less** ([[2022-09-12-cagan-pledge-to-customers]]). That conditional is the negotiating position, and it is why a pledge is stated as a commitment rather than as a policy announcement.

## The three pledges

### 1. To customers — *[Sep 2022](https://www.svpg.com/pledge-to-customers/)*
Four sections: **promises**, **product discovery**, **product delivery**, **happy referenceable customers**.

The load-bearing part is the four conditions on any promise of a date or deliverable:
1. The team engages **directly** with users and customers — proxies on either side are "not sufficient," and direct access is a prerequisite to any commitment.
2. **Only the team that will deliver may promise.** Not executives, sales, marketing, customer success, or delivery managers.
3. No promise before enough discovery to know what's required — normally one or more prototypes against the specific risks. These are [[high-integrity-commitments]].
4. A **commercial** product company must find a solution general enough to serve other customers; if it genuinely can't be, direct the customer to a custom-solution provider. See [[commercial-vs-internal-products]].

The delivery obligations are unusually concrete for SVPG: test the capability works, test it introduces no **regressions**, be sensitive to the customer's cost of adopting it (re-certification, re-learning, re-training), and when mistakes happen, correct quickly *and* analyse how to avoid repeating.

### 2. To stakeholders — *[Jun 2023](https://www.svpg.com/pledge-to-stakeholders/)*
A stakeholder is "someone not explicitly a member of a product team, yet represents a key constituency, area of the business, or special expertise."

The mechanism is a single habit: **whenever a solution might affect a stakeholder, show them a prototype before anything is built.** If the PM understood the constraints, it takes minutes and a thumbs-up; if not, the team iterates the prototype rather than shipped code. See [[prototypes]].

Two commitments sit underneath it — the PM engages directly with each relevant stakeholder to learn their constraints, and the team pledges **not to build, and certainly not to ship, any solution that doesn't meet the critical needs of the business**. Plus transparency: stakeholders are welcome in user testing, welcome to see prototypes, welcome to see usage data and live-data test results.

### 3. To executives — *[Jun 2023](https://www.svpg.com/pledge-to-executives/)*
Seven reciprocal bargains: **decisions · outcomes · disagreements · promises · focus · surprises · trust.**

Each names what the team commits *and* what it depends on executives for — context and financial/regulatory parameters for decisions; degrees of freedom for outcomes; permission to explore alternatives for disagreements; restraint in demanding dates for promises; holding [[focus]] at least quarterly.

The **surprises** clause is the most operationally useful: two symmetric wastes are named — a team builds production-quality work only to learn it's unacceptable, *and* an executive who raised nothing at the prototype review declares a serious problem after ship. Both trigger a post-mortem.

## The thing most teams get wrong

Cagan explicitly rejects the naive reading of empowerment. Product leaders "would like to think this is as easy as saying 'please just back off and give the product teams space to do their work.'" It isn't — executives have real needs in order to run the company responsibly, and teams need [[strategic-context|strategic context]] that comes *from* executives. The pledge to executives asks for **more** engagement, not less: "product teams need frequent, high-quality engagement." What changes is the *nature* of the interaction, not its volume.

## Wiki synthesis — how to use these

- **As a diagnostic, not a template.** Reading the three pledges against how a team actually behaves surfaces the gap faster than a maturity rubric. Most orgs fail the same two clauses: promises made by someone other than the delivering team, and solutions built before any stakeholder saw a prototype. See [[stakeholder-relationship-health]].
- **The precondition is on leadership.** The pledge to stakeholders is emphatic that "the onus is on the product leadership to ensure that every product team has a competent product manager that has put in the work to understand the various constraints of the business" — and that expecting stakeholders to trust a PM without that foundation is "unrealistic … and unwise." When a team complains stakeholders won't let go, check the precondition before the relationship. Related: [[coaching-vs-contracting]].
- **Order matters.** Customers → stakeholders → executives is a deliberate progression outward. A team that hasn't earned direct customer access has nothing to pledge to an executive yet.

## In your context

_Agent prompt: does the org have anything like an explicit, outward-facing commitment from its product teams — or is the new way of working being adopted silently, leaving customers and stakeholders to infer what changed? Check the four promise conditions specifically: who is currently allowed to commit a date, and has any stakeholder seen a prototype before build in the last quarter? Do not draft the org's pledges for them — the act of authoring is where the commitment comes from._
