# Product Career Development

> How a product person shapes their own career — the practical companion to [[hiring-and-onboarding]] on the *"what do I do next?"* question that PMs, PMMs, engineers, designers, and product leaders all keep asking. From [[jon-moore|Jon Moore]] and [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] on *Product Therapy* Ep 9. The core reframe: **approach your career like a product** — with a vision, a strategy, and a competency plan — because *you don't get promoted to learn the job; you get promoted to do it.*

## Why it matters
Product is a discipline most people fall into rather than train for. Moore's own path was journalism → BBC → a two-metre desk move into what was then called a *"digital project manager"* role. Idiodi's was premed → sales → winning an innovation competition at 23 with a spare-time idea. **Both hosts explicitly deprecate the University product-management program as sufficient preparation.** The industry has proliferated bachelor's, master's, and certificate programs that teach product mechanics; Idiodi has audited several and found none that reliably produces good product people. What actually produces them: **great coaches, or great environments where you can practice** (see [[product-coaching]]). Career development is what puts you in reach of one, the other, or both.

## Approach your career like a product
Ep 9's central move — extend the [[product-vision|vision]] / [[product-strategy|strategy]] / [[team-objectives|objectives]] discipline to *yourself*.

- **Career vision.** Where do you want to be in 3–7 years? What role, what kind of company, what kind of impact? This is the leap-of-faith view of yourself.
- **Career strategy.** The sequence of markets and roles that gets you from here to there. Which kind of team next? Which specific manager? Which industry? Which stage of company?
- **Competencies you need per stage.** What does the next role require that you don't yet have? Practice the next role in your current role before the promotion arrives.

Idiodi: *"if you don't plan for anything, then anything will happen — and that's okay, that's actually your plan; but if it is your plan, don't be upset about the layoff or the missed promotion."*

## The human-skills reframe (Kate Leto)
Idiodi flags the *"soft skills"* label as diminishing and reframes them as **human skills** — a term he credits to [Kate Leto](https://www.kateleto.com/articles/human-skills-wheel), which originates in a US military officer's manual (hard skills for equipment, human skills for people).

The human skills that most consistently make the biggest career difference in product (Idiodi's rank order):

- **Emotional intelligence** — self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship management. Hardest to evaluate but highest-leverage.
- **Collaborative problem-solving** — knowing what you don't have, when you need help, and how to bring the right people together to solve it. Distinguished from *"good problem solver working alone."*
- **Critical thinking** — a lot of smart people in product don't actually think about the problem; they execute a template.
- **Trust-building** ([[trust-over-control|see]]) — the operating currency of the discipline.
- **Agency** ([[coaching-agency|see]]) — sense of self-efficacy, courage to try.
- **Time management** ([[time-management|see]]) — protects Q2.
- **Perseverance** — nobody hires you for your weaknesses; you're hired for your strengths, but you'll be tested through hard stretches.

These are the skills that let you *use* the technical skills of discovery, strategy, and delivery. Without them, the technical skills stall.

## Life experience is an asset, not a delay
Moore's counter-narrative for those coming to product late or laterally: **life experience is one of the great sources of diversity of thought** — teams whose people have all followed the same product-program pipeline routinely miss what a former nurse, former salesperson, or former teacher would spot instantly. The Google associate-PM program's implicit rule (per Ep 9): *"you must be good at two of these disciplines — engineering, finance, marketing, sales — before we teach you product."* That is a feature, not a bug.

## Mentorship as leverage
Where your company has no one senior in the product model who could coach you, **mentorship outside the company** is the recognized lifeline. Moore's mentorship arc with Cagan — from an unsent-then-sent email at the back of a room, to a Skype call before his first CPO role (*"if you don't believe in yourself, John, no one else is going to"*) — is the wiki's canonical case for this. Cold-outreach mentorship works surprisingly often; the tax is small (the mentor's minutes) and the leverage is large (a role you wouldn't otherwise take).

## The under-rated career move: don't rush team-level jobs
Moore's most contrarian claim: *"don't rush your exit from team-level jobs, because that's where the gold dust is."* You draw on team-level experience for the rest of your career; a leader whose team-level résumé is one PM year at a startup followed by four years of leadership titles has almost no product judgment to draw on. Cagan's echo: the leaders you most want as coaches are the ones who *"have been there and done that"* at multiple stages, across multiple company types.

## Stay relevant by creating value
Moore's answer to *"how do I stay relevant?"*: **create value, and create a lot of it.** Product is a commercial role — the discipline exists to drive outcomes (usually revenue) for customers and the business. The London PM community's "Reckoning" (Moore's word) is the industry's belated correction to PMs who focused on process, delivery, and design theatre without ever moving a business metric. The career-development version of *"outcomes over output"*: your promotable evidence is not the artifacts you produced, it is the outcomes you moved.

## Anti-patterns & misunderstandings
- **Wait to be promoted, then learn the job.** The reverse of the working rule; if you can't do the job before the title, you probably won't do it after.
- **Optimize for salary bumps, not for coaches.** Especially early in a career, *who* you work for compounds more than *what* you're paid. Cagan explicitly names Google, Amazon, and Apple as apprenticeships worth accepting a down-levelling for.
- **Career-planning-as-title-planning.** The vision is the impact you want, not the title. Titles are downstream of the impact.
- **Confusing certification for competence.** Product certificates give a shared vocabulary; they don't produce good product people.
- **Under-investing in human skills because they feel non-technical.** The single most predictive skill difference between promotable and non-promotable senior PMs is emotional intelligence, not technique.
- **Skipping mentorship because your company has no one senior in the model.** That is precisely when you need it most; ask.
- **Treating team-level jobs as steps to escape.** They are where the judgment gets built.

## In your context
_Agent prompt: for the person you're coaching, what is their career vision? Which of the seven human skills is the highest-leverage next area? Which team-level experience do they still not have that they should? Who could mentor them from outside the company?_
