# John Deere — Smart Industrial Transformation

> A company founded in 1837 that makes tractors, in which — five or six years before the telling — *"everything was absolutely legacy"*, becomes a company where **an engineer watching a customer through a camera invents machine-learning-targeted herbicide spraying**. [[chris-jones|Chris Jones]]'s worked example of the product model in a company with no plausible claim to being a technology company.

> **Provenance note.** Unlike [[trainline-transformation|Trainline]], [[datasite-transformation|Datasite]] and the other *TRANSFORMED* stories, this is **not a book chapter**. It is told by Jones from the stage in [[2024-04-03-jones-moving-to-the-product-operating-model-product-people|*Moving to a Product Operating Model*]] (Product People, Apr 2024) as his single illustrative example. The account below is what he said; it is not a documented end-to-end transformation narrative, and the wiki does not claim more detail than the talk supports.

## Starting state
Jones's characterisation of Deere around 2018–2019: *"everything about this company was absolutely legacy"* — what they built (an old-school physical asset), how they built it, what manufacturing looked like, how it was sold and distributed, how they reached customers. Technology energy and innovation were not part of the conversation. On the product side specifically:

- **No true product management discipline.** What existed were people Jones describes in the CSPO sense — *"backlog administrators"* carrying the product-owner title. The same pattern [[2021-06-10-cagan-the-cspo-pathology|Cagan names as the CSPO pathology]] and that [[trainline-transformation|Trainline]] found in 2015.
- A conventional manufacturer's distance between engineering and the customer.

## What changed
Three moves, in Jones's account:

1. **A vision, held by a few people, of the "smart industrial" future** — the farm of the future rather than the tractor of the present.
2. **The product management competency was built, not assumed.** They trained people, hired people, and organised technology teams so that cross-functional conversation actually happened.
3. **A hard conduit to the customer, and this is the part that matters.** Deere invested specifically in *observing customers in situ*:
   - **Product managers visited farmers roughly weekly** — any given PM in direct conversation with a customer once or twice a week.
   - **A two-way camera system** was set up so teams at headquarters could watch, simultaneously, the driver in the tractor cab *and* what the tractor was doing in the field — continuously beamed back. **Engineers could watch.**

## Outcome — the innovation
Herbicide, conventionally, is sprayed evenly across a whole field. An **engineer** watching the camera feed had the insight: put a camera on the tractor, apply machine learning to identify where the actual weeds are, change the sprayer, and spray only there.

It shipped into the product line. At the time of the talk, Deere was approaching the first full calendar year in which **95% of the crop coming out of a treated field was never touched by herbicide**.

The business change underneath it is the second half of the story: Deere reconceived **what value means to its customers** — not tractors, but agricultural automation and efficiency — and **how value is distributed**, collecting significant revenue as recurring software subscriptions rather than only as hard-asset sales.

## What it illustrates
- concepts: [[empowered-engineers]] — *"prior to this, there was no way an engineer would have even seen or cared what was going on here."* The innovation is unattributable to a roadmap, a stakeholder request, or a PM; it required an engineer with customer context and permission. This is the single cleanest [[2020-04-20-cagan-the-most-important-thing|"most important thing"]] demonstration in the video corpus.
- concepts: [[product-discovery]] and [[continuous-interviewing]] — the weekly-cadence customer contact, and *in situ* observation over interviews. Note the parallel to [[2024-11-02-jones-from-features-to-outcomes-khodor|Jones's "three customers a week, not batched into a phase"]] tactic.
- concepts: [[intelligent-products]] — an ML capability embedded in a physical product, sold as recurring value.
- concepts: [[transformation]] and [[where-the-model-applies]] — the answer to *"does this apply to us, we're not a tech company?"* Jones's framing: pretty much every company is now a tech-enabled company, and Deere is the proof by counter-stereotype.
- concepts: [[time-to-money]] — the shift from selling assets to delivering (and monetising) ongoing value.

## Transferable lessons
1. **The camera is the lesson, not the machine learning.** The ML was available to any competitor. What Deere changed was *who could see the customer* — and it put engineers in that group deliberately, at cost, in a form (live, continuous, two-way) that required no scheduling.
2. **"Everything is legacy" is a starting state, not a disqualification.** Age, physical product, and an entrenched distribution model did not prevent the model taking hold; the absence of a product management competency was the actual gap, and it was closed by training and hiring.
3. **Jones's own measure of a transformation applies cleanly here:** *what can you do now that you could not have conceived of before?* Deere's answer is a specific product line that no one asked for.

## In your context
_Ask of the org's [[strategic-context|baseline]]: which of your engineers has watched a customer use the product this month — not a recording, not a summary, the actual thing? If the answer is none, ask what the equivalent of Deere's tractor camera would cost you, and compare that to what you spend on gathering requirements._
