# The Guardian — *eyewitness* and the iPad Launch

> [[marty-cagan|Cagan]]'s standing answer to *"what does a successful transformation actually get you?"* A 200-year-old newspaper that the internet had nearly killed builds a five-person product team; Apple gives that team **seven weeks** and an unreleased device; Steve Jobs puts the result on stage. **The point of the story is not the app — it is that they could do it at all.**

> **Provenance note.** Told at length by Cagan in [[2022-04-13-cagan-common-transformation-pitfalls-producttank-oslo|*Common Transformation Pitfalls*]] (ProductTank Oslo, Apr 2022). Judy Gibbons, the board chair who drove the transformation, is profiled in [[empowered|*EMPOWERED*]]. The Guardian is also referenced in passing on [[transformation]] and [[keys-to-successful-transformation]].

## Starting state
- **Founded 1821.** Survived two world wars; nearly killed by the internet, which stripped the classified-revenue base out of the media business — eBay, Craigslist, dating sites, job sites each taking a slice.
- Funded by a trust; the board's life-saving decision was to bring in **Judy Gibbons** — a former Apple and Microsoft executive — and make her **chair**, not merely a member.
- Technology capability at the outset: essentially none. *"A little group that would take an article written by a journalist and wrap a little HTML around it and put it on a website."*
- ~600 journalists on the payroll — the constraint that made this existential rather than strategic.

## What changed
A **small, good** technology group was created — Cagan is emphatic that it was not a big group. From it came the Guardian iPhone news app, built by **five people: three engineers, one product manager, one designer.** Apple considered it the best news app on the platform and promoted it heavily on the store, which mattered enormously to a company with no marketing budget.

## Outcome — the seven weeks
Jonathan Moore, the PM on that five-person team, is called to Cupertino with no explanation. Apple is about to announce the iPad; **Steve Jobs has personally encouraged them to invite the Guardian** to build for it, because he thinks they built the best iPhone news app. No promises — but if Apple likes what is submitted, it will be shown to Jobs, and if *he* likes it, he will feature it on stage.

Seven weeks. New device, new SDK, new gestures, and no hardware to test on for the first fortnight.

- **The [[high-integrity-commitments|high-integrity commitment]] came first.** Apple needed to know whether they were submitting at all. The team analysed the SDK, did quick prototyping, and only then committed. Cagan names this on stage as a high-integrity commitment.
- **The insight was an asset nobody was using.** Moore noticed the new device displayed images beautifully — and that the Guardian had a photo editor sourcing world-class images that a print newspaper reproduced badly and therefore barely used. He went to the photo editor and asked: *what if we told the news through photos?*
- **The product was an operation, not just an app.** *eyewitness* ran a daily production chain: the photo editor selects a photograph from the world's news; editorial commissions and edits a short story to sit behind it; the photographer's technical details are collected for the image; a sponsor (Canon) is attached, so the thing makes money.
- **Discovery was real.** Heavy prototyping and testing, on users *and* on the editorial team, before the build.
- **They asked the board before submitting** — an emergency board meeting, given the visibility. The board's only feedback: *how did you get a 200-year-old company to do an entire new app in seven weeks?*

Jobs introduced the iPad, swiped past the New York Times and USA Today, and stopped at *eyewitness*: **"this is what this device is all about."** For the first year of iPad sales, close to every purchaser installed it.

## What it illustrates
- concepts: [[transformation]] — **the litmus test.** Cagan's framing throughout: *"what could you do now that you couldn't do before?"* Most companies he knows, *including Google and Amazon*, would struggle to do this in seven weeks. The story exists to make the test concrete.
- concepts: [[empowered-product-teams]] — a team given a problem (make something awesome for this device) and held accountable for the result, where "shipped to Apple" would have counted for nothing.
- frameworks: [[high-integrity-commitments]] — the commit-after-enough-discovery pattern, in its natural habitat: an external partner with an immovable date.
- concepts: [[the-four-big-risks]], [[product-discovery]], [[prototypes]] — value and feasibility attacked before the build, with the editorial team as a viability stakeholder tested alongside users.
- diagnostics: [[disruption-response]], [[model-maturity]] — the Guardian is the positive case of a company whose *capability* let it take an opportunity that appeared with no notice.
- concepts: [[empowered-engineers]] — three of the five.

## Transferable lessons
1. **Capability is optionality.** Nobody could have planned for the iPad invitation. What the Guardian had bought with its transformation was the ability to say yes.
2. **Small is the point, not a caveat.** Five people. Cagan flags this deliberately — *"don't need big teams for this stuff."*
3. **The insight was an underused internal asset plus a new medium.** Not a customer request, not a competitor's feature: a photo archive that print had always wasted, meeting a device that finally displayed it well.
4. **Board-level sponsorship was upstream of everything.** Cagan calls making Gibbons *chair* "the first thing done right" — the same top-down-cover point he makes about CEOs throughout the rest of the talk.

## In your context
_Ask of the org's [[strategic-context|baseline]]: if a major platform partner offered you a marquee launch slot with seven weeks' notice, what would happen? The answer is a fair proxy for how far a transformation has actually got._
