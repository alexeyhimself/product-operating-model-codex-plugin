# Product Manager

> In an [[empowered-product-teams|empowered product team]], the PM is accountable for ensuring the solution is **valuable** and **viable** — that customers will buy/use it, and that it works for the business — while design owns usability and engineering owns feasibility (the [[the-four-big-risks|four big risks]]). The PM is a member of the [[the-product-team-trio|trio]], not its boss. Per *[[transformed|TRANSFORMED]]* (Ch. 10), value and viability are the PM's explicit responsibility.

## Accountabilities
- **Value and viability risk** — the PM personally owns the question of whether customers will buy/use the product and whether it works across the business's constraints (finance, sales, marketing, legal, partnerships). The other risks belong to the trio.
- **Deep knowledge the team relies on** — the PM is the team's acknowledged expert on the customer, the data, the industry/domain, the business, and the product itself. This is the foundation that earns the PM a seat in solving the problem.
- **Bringing the business context into discovery** — translating company strategy, [[team-objectives|objectives]], and constraints into the problems the team tackles, and feeding [[powered-by-insights|insights]] back up.
- **Product-owner mechanics** — the administrative/backlog responsibilities (a small subset of the role, not the role itself).

**Why value subsumes the rest — and why the accountability can't be split.** [[2022-02-07-cagan-value-and-viability|Value and Viability]] (Feb 2022) is the clearest statement of what this ownership actually means. Cagan's argument: value "is in no small part a consequence of viability, usability and feasibility," so saying the PM owns value means "we're counting on the product manager to ensure that *all* of these risks have been addressed."

He frames it through an adage he attributes tentatively to Ben Horowitz — "when the product succeeds, it's because everyone on the product team did what they needed to do; but when the product fails, it's the product manager" — and answers the fairness objection directly: PMs never get *sole* credit for success either ("good luck trying to create a successful product without the help of designers or engineers"), and standing up for mistakes is the foundation of the [[sense-of-ownership|sense of ownership]] empowered teams depend on. *(Passing attribution only — the §2 non-SVPG boundary still applies; `good-product-manager-bad-product-manager` remains a documented non-target.)*

⚠️ **The exclusion clause — the wiki's most compact PM-in-name-only test.** "Note that if you're a product manager on a feature team, then you're *not* responsible for value or viability (the stakeholder requesting the project or feature is), so this doesn't apply to you." See [[feature-teams-vs-product-teams]] and [[product-management-theater]].

**And the causal point most orgs miss:** the feature-team model is often the *consequence* of an accountability vacuum, not of bad intentions. If the PM won't or can't own value and viability, leadership "ends up looking to others to take on that responsibility" — a stakeholder, a line manager, a named GM or business owner — and "now you're a feature team there to implement what that stakeholder or GM considers valuable." The corrective for the most common dodge: "When a product manager says, 'not my fault' that sales can't sell, they are not only mistaken, but they are missing the point of their role."

> **What the PM is *not*:** a project manager, a backlog administrator, a "CEO of the product," or a feature-request router. [[2023-12-12-cagan-product-managers-vs-product-leaders|Cagan's terminology ruling]] makes the individual-contributor point explicit at the title level: **product manager = IC; product leader = the people manager of product people.** The two terms get conflated often enough, especially outside the US, that it is worth naming directly. The CSPO/product-owner responsibilities are "just a very small subset of the responsibilities of the true product manager" (Cagan). The Product Owner is a **process role** (Scrum ceremony); the PM is a *product* job — see [[process-people]], [[2021-10-28-cagan-process-people|Process People (2021)]] and [[2021-06-10-cagan-the-cspo-pathology|The CSPO Pathology (2021)]] for why asking one to cover the other is the "CSPO pathology." Cagan's regional pattern: the PO problem is concentrated in Europe.

### Product Owner (Scrum) — a role term, not a separate competency
Resolved 2026-09-06 (ingest-plan Batch 10): `[[product-owner]]` is a **role term**, not a person or a distinct wiki competency, so it does not get its own page — it belongs here, as a section on the PM competency it is a subset of.

**Product Owner is a Scrum ceremony role, not a job.** [[2016-12-13-cagan-product-manager-vs-product-owner-revisited|Cagan's canonical treatment]]: the CSPO/product-owner responsibilities — backlog grooming, sprint-ceremony administration — are "just a very small subset of the responsibilities of the true product manager." Per [[process-people]], Product Owner sits in the **process-people** category (alongside Scrum Master, Six Sigma Black Belt) rather than the **maker** category a real PM belongs to. Asking a PO to *cover* the PM job — rather than the reverse, a PM who also handles PO mechanics — is the **CSPO pathology** ([[2021-06-10-cagan-the-cspo-pathology|The CSPO Pathology, 2021]]): training and title without the product/process/people foundation a real PM needs. Cagan's regional pattern: the PO problem concentrates in Europe, mirrored by the US's product-ops variant of the same over-reliance-on-process instinct.

**Under AI, product owners are named as one of the most vulnerable roles** — see [[2023-06-09-cagan-preparing-for-the-future|Preparing For The Future]]'s three-way distinction (empowered-team PM vs. feature-team PM vs. Agile product owner) on [[the-four-big-risks]] and the "Creator, not facilitator" section above: a role built entirely around backlog administration and process mechanics has the least of the value/viability judgment work AI struggles to replace.

**Historical root of "PM ≠ project manager"** ([[2007-10-10-cagan-product-management-vs-project-management|canon, 2007]]): the conflation is a holdover from shipped-software companies (Microsoft's "Program Manager" title covered both). It broke once internet companies moved to frequent, parallel releases on a shared codebase — the **"release train"** model — which needs its own dedicated, cross-cutting project management (sometimes called the train's **"conductor"**), distinct from any one PM's scope. See [[delivery-manager]] for the modern form of that role.

## Product management vs. product *managers*
[[2023-11-28-cagan-alternatives-to-product-managers|Cagan]]: *product management* — responsibility for value and viability — never goes away, but *product managers* have never been the only way to cover it. Three alternatives, in ascending order of how well they tend to work:
1. **Founder/stakeholder-driven** — the founder or stakeholders own value + viability, and the team just builds the roadmap (i.e., they're a [[feature-teams-vs-product-teams|feature team]]). Cagan: "the root cause of most failed efforts." The most common alternative, and the worst one.
2. **Another trio member steps up** — the designer or Tech Lead covers PM responsibilities on top of their own ("triple threats"). Admirable but not scalable — it's two full-time jobs at once.
3. **Product-leader-driven** — skilled [[product-leadership|product leaders]] cover value + viability directly, alongside vision/strategy/topology/coaching. Apple's model: no individual-contributor product managers on their core consumer devices, but an unusually deep bench of product leaders. Trade-off: leaders become decision bottlenecks ("Apple is not known for moving quickly"), and the model **depends on product leaders with 10–25 years of Apple-caliber depth** — copying the structure without that talent just recreates alternative #1 with extra steps. See [[apple]] and [[apple-role-of-product]].

**The named hybrid between #2 and #3:** [[2023-12-12-cagan-product-managers-vs-product-leaders|group product manager (GPM)]] — a player-coach title Cagan calls his favorite path for someone moving from PM into product leadership, and the closest formal title to what Apple's product leaders do at scale (own value/viability directly *and* carry the people-manager job).

**Coaching use:** when a CEO proposes cutting the PM role to save cost, this is the framework to walk them through — value and viability don't disappear, they just land somewhere, and alternatives #1 and #2 both fail more often than they succeed.

## Creator, not facilitator (and the reckoning)
Cagan's sharpest spoken framing ([[2024-03-10-cagan-product-management-theater-lennys|Lenny's]]): a real PM is a **creator, not a facilitator** — "my job is to say why" is not a job (the *why* comes from [[product-strategy|product strategy]]). [[2016-10-01-cagan-behind-every-great-product|Behind Every Great Product (2016 essay)]] — the **root** of this framing, restated live in the [[2018-06-07-cagan-behind-every-great-product-mtpcon-london-2016|2018 MTP London talk]] — names the **three ways teams run a PM**: (a) escalate every decision to the manager; (b) call a meeting for every decision (design-by-committee); (c) actually do the job. Only (c) is product management. The essay also lays out the **PM-as-CEO analogy** (with the difference that no one reports to the PM) and the **four qualities** — smart · creative · persistent · plus grit — that separate strong PMs from adequate ones. Cagan's six named case-study PMs from the essay — Lauchengco (Word for Mac), Kate Arnold (Netflix), Jane Manning (AdWords), Alex Pressland (BBC), Camille Hearst (iTunes), [[lea-hickman|Lea Hickman]] (Adobe Creative Cloud) — are the wiki's canonical worked examples of *doing the job*.

**"Product creator," in writing.** [[2025-05-28-cagan-the-era-of-the-product-creator|The Era of the Product Creator]] (May 2025) gives this spoken "creator, not facilitator" framing a written, more precise definition: *"anyone actively shaping the product and tackling the product risks [value, usability, feasibility, viability] is a product creator. Their title doesn't really matter."* The term deliberately extends beyond the PM title — strong designers, engineers, founders, and business stakeholders can all play it — and feeds directly into Cagan's Feb-2026 model-as-coach prescription; see [[model-as-product-coach]]. Cagan later tempered the article's optimism about how *broadly* this actually happened in practice — see [[2026-08-10-cagan-a-fresh-definition-of-the-product-role|A Fresh Definition of The Product Role]] below.

**A non-product-insider's version of the same argument.** [[2026-08-10-cagan-a-fresh-definition-of-the-product-role|A Fresh Definition of The Product Role]] (Aug 2026) has Cagan translate analyst Benedict Evans's implicit definition of the product role into product-model language: recognizing the *general problem* behind a specific pain point ([[product-discovery|problem discovery]]), discovering an effective *solution* (the value risk), and understanding the business deeply enough that the solution also works for it (the viability risk). Evans, quoted by Cagan: *"People that are really good at using the tool are not the same people as those that are really good at creating the tool."* Cagan uses this to revisit his own May-2025 optimism: the broadening of who becomes a strong product creator "has been maybe a little bit true, but not anywhere near what I had hoped for" — the bottleneck was never the tools, it's whether someone thinks like a product person at all.

**The new litmus test** ([[2026-04-14-cagan-ai-product-coaching-women-in-product|AI Product Coaching]]): "we're all builders" — if you don't love prototyping and testing prototypes, product isn't the role for you. PMs now do [[build-to-learn-vs-build-to-earn|build-to-learn]] work themselves rather than delegating all of it, on a foundation of [[product-sense|product sense]]. This is the heart of the [[product-management-theater|reckoning]] Cagan warns is underway: backlog-administrator PMs are the most vulnerable roles, and with AI **viability becomes *more* important**, not less (probabilistic vs deterministic software; legal, ethical, and mission-critical questions land squarely on the PM). **Root, re-established.** The three-way distinction behind this claim — empowered-team PM (value + viability) vs. feature-team PM (program/project manager) vs. Agile product owner (administrative) — and the prediction that the latter two are AI's most vulnerable roles actually originates in [[2023-06-09-cagan-preparing-for-the-future|Preparing For The Future]] (Jun 2023), 10 months ahead of [[2024-04-16-cagan-ai-product-management|AI Product Management]] (Apr 2024), which stays primary for the fuller viability-under-AI treatment and the structured four-risk breakdown (see [[the-four-big-risks]]); [[2025-09-29-cagan-future-of-product-management-producttank-sydney|Sydney]] restates it further 27 months later.

## What the job is not (and is) in build-to-learn
Cagan's [[2026-04-28-cagan-build-to-learn-faq|Build To Learn FAQ]] answers a question this page's "creator, not facilitator" framing raises but doesn't spell out: if the PM isn't a facilitator, what exactly *are* they, day to day? Four explicit no's, then the yes:
- **Not "the decider."** Every trio member makes dozens of judgment calls daily using their own expertise. Cagan's analogy: a surgical team defers to whoever is best suited for a given decision, collaborating when it touches other areas — there's no single "decider" role.
- **Not "the protector of the team."** Shielding the team from everyone who shows up with ideas or requests isn't the job — most such ideas won't solve the problem anyway. The PM's job is to help the team find a solution that actually works, not to be the designated "no."
- **Not "the one who explains the why."** That's [[product-strategy|product strategy]]'s job — a few minutes of context-setting, and anyone on the team could do it. Not nearly enough to justify the role on its own.
- **Not "the manager."** Restated plainly: the PM is an individual contributor, "not the boss of anyone" — the same point the "CEO of the product" metaphor above is careful *not* to mean.
- **The yes:** the PM's specific contribution is owning **value** and **viability** — shaping the solution so customers will buy/use it and so it meets the business's constraints — bringing the same kind of deep knowledge (customer, data, industry, business) that engineers bring on technology and designers bring on users.

**Founders:** don't hire a PM too early — pre-PMF, value and viability are the founder's job; the heuristic is ~20–25 engineers, and the first product hires should own go-to-market and pricing too ([[2024-03-21-cagan-product-management-rules-in-early-stage-june|June]]).

## The three kinds of product manager — and their opposite AI trajectories
Cagan has described three ways companies build products *"for more than 25 years"*, each with its own kind of product manager. His claim on [[2026-07-22-cagan-olsen-how-ai-impacts-product-management|Lean Product Meetup, July 2026]] (stated first, more compactly, on [[2026-04-23-cagan-golden-era-for-product-management-udacity|the Udacity interview]] in April) is that **AI is hitting the three in opposite directions** — which is why generic "is product management dying?" commentary is unreadable. Establish which one someone holds before answering anything.

| | Who owns value & viability | Cagan's read, mid-2026 |
|---|---|---|
| **Agile product owner** — dominant in Europe | nobody on the team | *"That's a role in a delivery process, and that delivery process is pretty obsolete today. So that is not the job you want… they're in trouble. They have to upskill as fast as possible."* |
| **Feature-team PM** — dominant in the US | stakeholders, via the roadmap | Cross-functional but not empowered. *"We call them product managers, but they're really project managers."* |
| **Empowered-team PM** | the PM | *"The good ones are getting multiple offers constantly."* |

**The market signal, in one sentence:** *"We've got this weird setup where **salaries are basically crashing on the first, there's a lot of layoff stuff in the second, and in the third they're escalating.**"*

**The automation trap for the middle group.** Feature-team PMs are the ones most visibly enthusiastic about agent harnesses and workflow automation, and Cagan's read is unsentimental: *"they're all thrilled. I'm like, great, you have now automated your **project-management** job. Does the company need you anymore? Probably not — because you're not doing the product management, and now you're not even doing the project management."* Automating the job you were never supposed to be doing removes your last remaining justification. The route out is the one on [[pilot-teams]]: *"go to your manager and propose letting you run an experiment — typically that's called a pilot team — for a quarter."* And the prior choice, stated bluntly: *"you can spend your time learning Claude Code, **or** you could actually develop your product sense — that's literally the choice a lot of people are making."*

**Only two of the four risks, and why that is the trap.** *"The truth is most feature teams only work on **usability and feasibility**, because they're given the solutions in the roadmap. But in an empowered team, it's now your job."* See [[the-four-big-risks]], [[feature-teams-vs-product-teams]].

**What the PM brings when anyone can build the prototype.** With build-to-learn democratised, *"it's a lot less relevant who creates the prototype — it's more about the different **lenses of evaluating** that prototype."* Engineers bring feasibility, designers bring experience, and the PM brings the customer understanding — *"the sense of what it will take for them to love and to choose and to buy and to use your product"* — plus the business dimensions: *"how it's marketed, how it's sold, how it's serviced, how it's funded, how it's monetized, what's required in terms of legal, privacy, compliance… tax."* Hence Cagan crediting [[christian-idiodi|Idiodi]] by name: *"the new superpower used to be technology. **The new superpower in the age of AI is business savvy** — and on the other side the technology knowledge is less critical."*

**"Triple threats."** *"It's never had to be three people. Some people can cover product management and product design — awesome. Some cover engineering tech lead and product manager together — great. I know a few people that do all three really well; those are called triple threats."* The requirement is the lenses, not the headcount.

**A burnout figure to negotiate with.** Cagan is *"genuinely worried"* about burnout, most of all for feature-team PMs, *"because project management is non-stop meetings and then you're trying to fit [product work] on top of that."* His number: *"you need somehow about **four hours a day** to do the build-to-learn stuff. If you're in meetings all day long then you've got a problem — you're probably trying to be project manager **and** product manager."* If the calendar cannot yield four hours, the conversation is about what to stop, not about working harder.

## Competencies — weak vs strong
Cagan assesses PM competence along three pillars — **product knowledge, process skills, people skills** ([[pm-competency]] is the assessment rubric; [[coaching-the-pm]] is how each is developed). Product knowledge is table stakes (~2–3 months to ramp); people skills separate competent from exceptional.

### Product knowledge (the foundation — without it, nothing else matters)
| Competency | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| User & customer | Knows users second-hand, through tickets/requests | Company-acknowledged expert on target users; has met many real ones (Cagan: ≥15 visits as onboarding) |
| Data | Delegates questions to analysts; can't read the tools | Operates the analytics tools *and* understands what the data is telling them |
| Industry & domain | Vague on the landscape; surprised by competitors | Knows the domain, competitors, and which trends matter for this product |
| Business & company | Unaware of finance, GTM, legal constraints | Understands revenue/cost KPIs, the funnel, legal/partnership constraints; stakeholders feel understood |
| Product operational | Can give a basic demo, no more | Expert user of their own product; could demo, onboard, and handle support; dogfoods |

**This table holds unchanged into the AI era.** [[2025-03-24-cagan-baxley-product-design-and-ai|Cagan & Baxley]] (Mar 2025) restate the same five knowledge areas under the explicit name **"product sense,"** paired for the first time with the designer's parallel **"design sense"** (see [[product-designer]]). Nothing here changed — the AI-era article confirms the framework travels forward unaltered rather than revising it.

#### Customer-centricity — the trait under the first row ([[2019-10-24-cagan-coaching-customer-centricity|Cagan, 2019]])

The "User & customer" row above is a *competency*; the coaching-series article behind it names the **trait** that competency depends on, and is the root SVPG source for the visit cadence.

- **The cadence.** Cagan's own coach required **30 customer visits** (15 US, 15 Europe) before he was allowed to make a single decision. His recommendation: **≥15 visits to start, then a minimum of three 1-hour customer interactions every week, ongoing** — with the [[2019-02-08-cagan-coaching-tools-the-one-on-one|weekly 1:1]] used to ask what was learned. The purpose of having the PM retell those stories around the company is explicitly reputational: to establish her as the person with deep, personal knowledge of the customer.
- **Keep the word "customer" narrow.** A common PM failure is to treat stakeholders, customer service, the CEO, or advertising partners as "customers." Cagan reads this as a remnant of technology-serves-the-business and says it **dilutes the real customer**. Internal users of customer-enabling products and platform developers matter and may be necessary to deliver value — but *"none have the weight or importance of the true customers."*
- **Claim vs. demonstrate.** Ask a CEO or PM whether they care about customers and you get indignation. The tell is behavioral: how they handle an outage or a confusing change, and how often they actually sit face-to-face with users.
- **The executive-override warning.** In genuinely customer-centric companies, if a team does not prioritise a customer problem as highly as the executives do, the executives *will* step in — *"if you make them choose between empowered teams and taking care of customers, you probably won't like what they decide."* A useful corrective to a naive reading of [[empowered-product-teams|empowerment]].
- **Still not order-taking.** The job is *to innovate on behalf of customers*, explicitly contrasted with focus groups — see [[2017-09-25-cagan-customer-inspired-technology-enabled|customer-inspired, technology-enabled]].
- **Timeline:** sincere, consistent customer-centricity takes **a year or more** to develop in a new PM, with mistakes in judgement along the way.

### Process skills & techniques
| Competency | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Product discovery | Jumps to build; tests nothing up front | Tackles the [[the-four-big-risks|four risks]] *before* engineers build; uses qual + quant; solves collaboratively; focuses on [[outcomes-over-output|outcome]] |
| Product optimization | Ships and moves on | Runs ongoing A/B optimization on live products where traffic warrants |
| Product delivery | Ignores delivery as "engineering's job" | Understands delivery techniques and the cost trade-offs of release decisions |
| Development process | Only knows the ritual (standups, Jira) | Understands discovery + delivery end-to-end and their own product-owner role within it |

### People skills (what separates competent from truly successful)
| Competency | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Team collaboration | Order-taker model; designer/eng brought in late | True collaboration built on trust and respect; involves the trio early, with direct customer access |
| Stakeholder management | Stakeholders feel unheard; surprises them | Mutual trust; understands each stakeholder's constraints; previews anything of concern before building |
| Evangelism | Can't get the team or company behind the work | Shares vision/strategy persuasively; motivates team and stakeholders |
| Leadership | Relies on the title (which grants nothing) | Earns influence over people who don't report to them; looked to under pressure |

**Credibility is earned, and requires two-way transparency (Hickman, 2017).** [[lea-hickman|Hickman]] at [[2017-11-24-hickman-transforming-to-a-product-culture-mind-the-product|Mind the Product London 2017]]: PM autonomy is *earned* through execution + accountability + integrity — *"you have to do what you say you're going to do, to build a reputation for integrity that other people can buy into."* Points forward to [[high-integrity-commitments|high-integrity commitments]] as the mature formulation of the same rule: keep the commitment or renegotiate honestly. Transparency runs both ways: *"be just as comfortable delivering bad news as positive results — otherwise you only tell half the story and people won't believe you appreciate the whole situation."* This is the counterweight to evangelism above: a PM who over-sells the wins and under-reports the risks loses credibility faster than one who never brings a win at all.

## In the exemplars (case studies)
- **Google — a high bar.** PMs are expected to have business acumen, a technical foundation, and the ability to drive hard problems to outcomes, plus an **entrepreneur mindset** (many leave to found startups — treated as a signal Google hired right; acquired-company CEOs often become the PM). The **[[product-leadership|Group Product Manager]]** player-coach is a Google staple, and strong [[engineers|Tech Leads]] are why many Google PMs "haven't touched a ticket in years" ([[google-product-model]]).
- **Apple — the true product role vs "fill the white space."** Historically product-definition sat with Jobs and "Product Marketing Managers" took products to market ([[product-marketing]]). Cagan pushes back on defining the PM as "**do whatever needs to be done to get the product out**" — admirable in startups, but it "misses the primary point of the product role: to ensure the team is building a product that has the necessary **[[the-four-big-risks|value]]**" ([[apple-role-of-product]]).

## Common failure modes
- **The feature-team PM** — a backlog administrator / project manager taking orders from stakeholders, not owning value and viability. The default in the project model; see [[feature-team-vs-empowered-team]].
- **CSPO-only** — trained on product-owner mechanics, then confused about why they're failing; they never built the product/process/people foundation.
- **The "I'm a great designer/engineer" PM** — meddles in usability/feasibility instead of owning value/viability and trusting the trio.
- **No customer contact** — reasoning about users from requests and opinions rather than first-hand knowledge.
- **People-skill plateau** — competent on knowledge/process but unwilling to develop collaboration/stakeholder skills; Cagan: if there's no sincere interest in improving, it's time to find a more suitable role.

## Why value and viability are what degrade first (Jones)

A structural explanation this page can use, from [[2024-11-02-jones-from-features-to-outcomes-khodor|Jones]]: the PM's two risks are **value** and **viability** — and *"these two are particularly interesting, because in feature teams you don't usually have to worry about those things, at least not within the product team."*

On a feature team, whoever asked for the work owns value and viability. If adoption fails, that is not the team's fault. **In the product model it is** — which is why the model needs someone who deeply cares about both, and why the role hollows out in an org that has not made the switch. [[christian-idiodi|Idiodi]] makes the same point from the other end in [[2023-12-21-idiodi-essence-of-product-management-lennys]]: *"if teams are given roadmaps of projects and features to build, you actually really don't need a product manager, because they're going to **assume** value."*

Idiodi's viability breakdown is the fullest in this batch: is it sellable, can the Salesforce move it, is it compliant, does it work with our partnership obligations, can we market it — plus the observation that B2B and B2C differ mainly in the **weight of the constraint set**, not in the job.

## The "Bob test" (Idiodi)

Idiodi's device for explaining product management to a CEO who thinks they do not need it ([[2023-12-21-idiodi-essence-of-product-management-lennys|Lenny's Podcast]]):

> Ask the CEO: *who in your company do you trust to make a decision on what markets we go to, what things we do?* They always have someone — *"Bob has been here a long time, he knows everybody, everybody trusts him, he understands our business."*
> Then: **"Well — Bob is a product manager."** *"Imagine if you had Bobs on every team. Imagine how much you could accomplish."*

The point is that **the role is competency-based**. The reason people dislike product managers, in Idiodi's diagnosis, is not the role but the experience of *incompetent* instances of it: the trust that lets a company delegate a decision comes from being the person who knows the customer, the data, the industry, the business and the product better than anyone. Where that competence is absent, the influence is displaced — to sales, to executives — and this is *"not a cultural knock or a leadership knock, it's really on the individual, and the discipline has to elevate itself to a place that it **earns** the right to make a decision."*

His practical instruction to a PM who does not have that standing yet: go to the loudest, most influential person in the organisation and ask them to **teach** you — and if they have no time, offer to help them instead. You borrow their trust, build the relationship, and learn what their influence is actually made of. See [[emotional-intelligence]] and [[hiring-and-onboarding]] for the leader-side version of the same technique.

**The framing he offers PMs about the job itself:** *"you wake up on behalf of someone else to solve a problem for them, and you have to do it well enough that they give you something back in return — a certificate of appreciation. Revenue, engagement, loyalty, reference."* And the calibration: *"if it's not fun, you're probably not doing it right. If it's not hard, you're probably also not doing it right."*

## Relationships
- **The trio.** The PM works as a peer with the [[product-designer|product designer]] and the [[engineers|tech lead]] (the [[the-product-team-trio|trio]]), not above them — "what each of design and engineering brings to the table is just as essential as what the PM brings." Prototyping, wireframing, and user research are the designer's job, not the PM's ([[2020-06-12-cagan-product-management-start-here|canon]]).
- **Stakeholders.** Built on mutual trust earned by understanding constraints and previewing concerns — harder than trio trust because interactions are fewer and weightier.
- **Leadership & coaching.** The PM is developed by their manager (or a [[product-coach]] / [[model-as-product-coach|model-as-coach]]) via the [[pm-competency|assessment]] → [[coaching-the-pm|coaching plan]] loop, ideally in weekly one-on-ones. Expect **~3 months of real work** to reach basic competence, *with* weekly coaching ([[2020-06-12-cagan-product-management-start-here|Cagan]]).
- **Feature-team trap.** If you have the PM title but spend most of your day on project management or backlog admin, you're on a feature team or worse — see [[feature-teams-vs-product-teams]].
- **Group product manager (GPM).** As orgs grow, the "player-coach" GPM leads a team *and* coaches one to three other PMs — an underused role for scaling PM depth ([[2017-02-10-cagan-beyond-lean-and-agile-dan-olsen|canon]]; [[inspired|INSPIRED]] Ch 17 has the fullest treatment). Ch 17 uses a marketplace example: 10 teams → 3 groups (platform · buyer-side · seller-side); one VP product + three GPMs; each GPM is PM for one team in their group and coaches the 2 other PMs. *"This role is often called a player-coach role because of this dynamic of leading your own team, in addition to being responsible for coaching and developing one to three other PMs."* Career outcomes: some GPMs move to director/VP; some to principal PM; **some stay as GPM permanently** because they love the blend of hands-on work + coaching influence.
- **Product marketing.** With a direct sales channel the PM needs a [[product-marketing|product-marketing]] partner (owning the market side of product-market fit); "responsibility for sales is on the product team" ([[2022-12-07-cagan-product-lessons-jobs-musk-20vc|Andy Grove / OKRs]]).
- **Hiring & onboarding.** New PMs are hired for **potential** and coached to competence in ~3 months — see [[hiring-and-onboarding]]. Cagan's "CEO of the product" metaphor ([[2017-02-10-cagan-beyond-lean-and-agile-dan-olsen|Beyond Lean and Agile]]) is about the *breadth of business context* a PM must hold — "like a startup CEO" — explicitly **not** boss authority.

## The PM's partnership job (Part VI)
The PM is the person who **operates the cross-functional partnerships** the model depends on — with customers (Ch 21: promises come from the team that will deliver; direct access as prerequisite; commercial-product vs custom-shop framing), sales (Ch 22: shared time in front of customers is the trust mechanism; reference-customers-not-specials is the systemic fix), product marketing (Ch 23: the eight collaboration areas — see [[product-marketing]]), finance (Ch 24: bring data, not business cases; use [[high-integrity-commitments]] sparingly), stakeholders (Ch 25: show a prototype before building anything that might impact them), and executives (Ch 26: the six-element trust model — see [[partnering-with-executives]]). The unified pattern lives on [[cross-functional-partnering]]. Coaching a PM through their weakest partnership is usually the highest-leverage move a manager can make.

## Common frictions inside product (Ch 46)
[[transformed|TRANSFORMED]] Ch 46 (*Objections from Inside Product*) is the largest chapter in Part X — because the model's own team has honest frictions that need honest answers. The wiki collects them in [[overcoming-objections|the overcoming-objections synthesis]]; the PM-relevant ones are: "how do we own outcomes we don't control?"; "can we pivot in discovery?"; "we're being set up to fail"; "which decisions are ours to make?"; "PM and designer exclude engineers from discovery"; and "where do POs and BAs fit?" (short answer: they don't — PO is a role covered by the PM, BA responsibilities move partially to PMs and partially to designers). Cross-links inline in the objections page.
