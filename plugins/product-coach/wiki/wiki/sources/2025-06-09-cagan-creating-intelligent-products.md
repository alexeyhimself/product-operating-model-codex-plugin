## Notes for the coach

### The historical anchor
Cagan's very first published article (HP, March 1986) already discussed "intelligent programming environments" — assessing the impact of modifications, diagnosing bugs, automatic testing. The era's dominant approach was **expert systems**: capturing human-expert decision rules and using inference engines to reach conclusions.

### The origin insight (the doctor anecdote)
Young-engineer Cagan tagged along on a "knowledge systems engineer" interviewing a Stanford physician about patient-monitoring decisions, to capture the physician's rule set. The rules turned out to be far too vast to enumerate — but more importantly, the physician explained his real process was making "educated guesses based on *probabilities*," then running tests to confirm or rule them out. This is why early rule-based expert systems stalled, and it's the seed of this article's central claim.

### The central thesis (verbatim-close)
"Probabilistic solutions are not edge cases. They are *integral* to creating intelligent solutions." A person or a foundation model is judged "intelligent" when its probability-based predictions are mostly right — even though sometimes wrong. Cagan argues this applies broadly: diagnosing a broken engine, forecasting sales, giving investment advice, developing a new medication. **"I view probability as central to intelligence, not an alternative."**

### The term and its scope
**"Intelligent products"** = a deliberate blend of deterministic and probabilistic approaches, aimed at creating substantially more useful and valuable solutions. Explicitly **not** a reference to AGI — irrelevant to whether/when that's achieved, and not required for the products under discussion here.

### The rebuttal this article exists to make
Cagan targets product teams — especially in B2B — who consider probabilistic solutions merely "intriguing" but not relevant to their mission-critical, regulated, or compliance-constrained businesses. His counter is the whole article: probability is already how human experts operate in exactly those high-stakes domains.

### Worked examples across the spectrum
- **Waymo Driver** — possibly "the single most impressive intelligent product" Cagan has personally experienced: over a decade of product discovery and delivery, continuous learning, a 1,500-car fleet generating 50M+ miles of real-world driving and 20M+ miles of simulated driving *per day*, responsible for both getting passengers to their destination and protecting the lives of passengers, pedestrians, cyclists, and other drivers.
- **Classical-ML intelligent products people don't register as "AI"**: Google Translate, [[spotify-discover-weekly|Spotify's Discover Weekly]] (already a wiki case study — now also an example of an intelligent product), Netflix Recommendations.
- **Generative-AI-era examples beyond text/voice/image generation**: Shopify Magic, Cursor AI, Tome.

### The ask of product people
Move beyond a deterministic-vs-probabilistic binary; treat probabilistic behavior as a first-class design ingredient, not "a bolted on feature, but as a key to intelligent products." The most valuable future AI applications will likely be exactly where human experts already reason probabilistically.
