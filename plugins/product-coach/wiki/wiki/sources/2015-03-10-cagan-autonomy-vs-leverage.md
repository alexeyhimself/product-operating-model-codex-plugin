## Notes for the coach
- **The symptom this series starts from.** "While most leaders tell me they have empowered, autonomous teams, some of the people on those teams complain to me that they don't always feel so empowered or autonomous." Cagan's diagnostic move is to ask what specifically the team can't decide — and he finds the answers fall into two cases: **(a) the team isn't trusted yet**, or **(b) the team wants to change something leaders considered part of the foundation.** Case (b) is what the article is about, and distinguishing the two is the first thing a coach should do.
- **The trade-off named.** Team autonomy vs. **leverage of a shared foundation**. Cagan is explicit that he's a fan of both: "while I love the core notion of autonomous, empowered teams, I am also a big fan of investing in the foundation." And equally explicit that there is **no single right answer**: "I do not believe that there is one answer to this question. I think it is different for each company and even for each team."
- **The easy example vs. the hard ones.** Easy: each team choosing its own source-control tool — if engineering standardised on one, the total cost outweighs any benefit. Hard, and left genuinely open: test automation approach, programming languages, UI frameworks, browser compatibility, expensive features like offline support, "the flavor of Agile they wish to use," and whether every team must support company-wide initiatives.
- **The eight considerations** — the citable framework, and the most useful part of the article:
  - **Team skill level** — Cagan's A/B/C-team framing: an experienced team of smart creatives; a well-intentioned team lacking the experience to decide well; a junior team that "may not even know what they don't know yet."
  - **Importance of speed** — leverage means not re-inventing the wheel, but "sometimes it's simply an accepted and acknowledged cost of empowerment to allow teams to potentially duplicate areas or proceed slower in the name of autonomy."
  - **Importance of integration** — largely independent products vs. a highly integrated portfolio; optimise for the team's solution or for the company.
  - **Source of innovation** — if future innovation must happen *at the foundation*, teams need freedom to revisit core components; if at the solution level, discourage foundation churn.
  - **Company size and locations** — scale and dispersion make leverage both more important and harder; centres of excellence, holistic roles, or added process are the usual responses.
  - **Company culture** — pushing toward leverage "can be perceived by the teams as chipping away at their level of autonomy," tolerable for B/C teams, "more problematic for A level teams."
  - **Maturity of technology** — "one very common problem is to try to standardize on a common foundation prematurely … Building on a house of cards."
  - **Level of accountability** — "you want the teams to stress over these trade-offs." Cagan's own tiebreaker: "If I believe the team is strong and they fully understand the consequences and risks, and yet they still feel they need to ignore or replace a key component of the foundation, then I tend to side with that team."
- **Dated detail.** The article opens by noting most companies have moved off "the pool model" of staffing — a 2015 observation; the `dedicated-product-teams` article it links is separately carded.
