## Notes for the coach
- **Framing build-to-learn work.** Start from a *problem to solve* and an *outcome to achieve* (customer problem, company problem, or both); success = the outcome was achieved.
- **The hard part, disambiguated.** Product strategy (a leadership job) picks the problem. Understanding the problem is usually fast and not the hard part — misunderstandings surface quickly once you test prototype solutions. **Solving the problem is the hard part, by far** — especially when the solution must be demonstrably better than existing alternatives. So "building to learn" = primarily solution discovery.
- **Explicit pushback on a common misconception.** Discovery is *not* about confirming the problem is real — Cagan calls that belief a trust-destroyer with leadership. Leaders/stakeholders rarely prioritize problems that aren't real; the difficulty is nearly always in the solving.
- **The implicit empowered-team agreement, stated plainly.** Leaders/stakeholders are trusted to identify worthy problems; the team is trusted to solve them in ways that work for customer and business. Neither side can know in advance that a given problem is *the most important* one — only that it's real.
- **What exactly discovery is testing for.** Whether the solution will truly solve the problem and generate the outcome — mapped explicitly onto the four risks: **value** (customer unimpressed / won't switch), **usability** (too complicated), **feasibility** (harder to build than imagined), **viability** (not compliant/secure/legal/sellable/monetizable). Reinforces (doesn't originate) the four-risk table already on [[the-four-big-risks]].
- **The PM's job is NOT (new to the wiki, four explicit no's):**
  - **Not "explaining the why"** — that's product strategy's job (a few minutes of work, and anyone on the team could do it); not enough to justify the role.
  - **Not "the decider."** Every trio member (designer, engineer) makes dozens of judgment calls daily using their own expertise. Cagan's analogy: a surgical team defers to whoever is best suited for a given decision, collaborating when it affects other areas — there's no single "decider."
  - **Not "the protector of the team."** The PM's job isn't to shield the team from people bringing ideas/requests — most such ideas won't actually solve the problem, but the PM's role is to help the team find a solution that works, not to be the designated "no."
  - **Not "the manager."** The PM is an individual contributor, not the boss of anyone on the team — corroborates the existing wiki framing on [[product-manager]] ("CEO of the product" ≠ boss authority).
- **What the PM's job IS, stated crisply.** Owning **value** and **viability** of proposed solutions — shaping the solution so customers will buy/use it, and so it meets business-stakeholder constraints. Deep knowledge of customers, data, industry, and business is what Cagan calls "product sense," parallel to how engineers bring deep tech knowledge and designers bring deep user knowledge.
- **AI accelerates discovery too, differently than delivery.** In delivery, AI is mainly automation/code-generation. In discovery, AI is more of a prototyping and decision-support tool — and developing strong product sense (to make good discovery decisions) is itself something AI can help teach.
- **The role of the PRD, in the product model (new to the wiki).** The prototype is the **primary** way learnings get communicated to engineers ("prototype as spec" — corroborates [[2025-09-12-cagan-the-purpose-of-prototypes]]). The PRD **supplements** discovery in the product model (enumerating specific use cases and non-functional requirements) — it must never be used **instead of** discovery, which is exactly the project-model failure mode. "Countless products have failed because a product manager thinks he or she knows what's 'required' only to find later that they were wrong."
- **Learning doesn't stop at launch.** Delivery is optimized to earn, but production usage data still teaches — and it's the only place the *ultimate* test (did we solve the problem and deliver the outcome) can actually be confirmed.
- **"Ready-fire-aim," named (new to the wiki).** The logic of "faster output → faster outcomes" (accelerate delivery in the project model and hope outcomes follow) is explicitly named here. Cagan's rebuttal, tying directly to [[test-ideas-responsibly]]: customers who feel constantly, erratically changed-upon feel like guinea pigs — fine for opted-in discovery cohorts, corrosive when a paying customer feels experimented on. Protect general users/customers from this; that's what discovery's many test-and-learn techniques are for.
- **Who you test each risk with, restated as a clean list.** Value + usability → users/customers. Feasibility → engineers (including on dependency teams). Viability → the relevant stakeholders (sales, marketing, legal, compliance, finance, operations, etc.). Not every risk needs testing with every constituent on every prototype — test the relevant party based on the level of risk.
- **How you know you made the right call.** Only defensible answer: did the work generate the necessary business outcome. If not, use the latest data/learnings to improve quickly.
- **Further reading pointer (both already SVPG-canon in this wiki):** *INSPIRED* and *Continuous Discovery Habits* named as the two essential books.
