## Notes for the coach
- **The argument is explicitly *not* about competence or spend.** Cagan is careful on both: "it's *not* because their people are clueless … most of the time the people there – at least the senior ones that you interact directly with – are well above average," and "it's *not* due to lack of investment on your company's part." That framing matters when coaching a leader who's already spent heavily and assumes the fix is a better engagement or a bigger budget.
- **The thesis.** "My theory is that this anti-pattern is structural. These firms are inherently unable to help, and transformations driven by them are almost certainly destined to fail."
- **The analogy he uses.** A power utility wanting to move from fossil fuels to renewables calls its fossil-fuel contacts of several decades — who of course position themselves as being "in the 'energy' business" and of course say yes. "But really what you're asking is for them to act against their own institutional interests. And even if they were willing to do that, it's unlikely they know how." Both halves of that sentence are the point: **unwilling, and also unable.**
- **The mechanism, back in tech.** Stakeholders have long leaned on business consultants for decision support. Transformation requires that capability to move **in-house and to different people** — product leaders rather than stakeholders. "Stakeholders hire management consultants for help with decisions, and those consultants will not be incentivized to provide advice that diminishes the power of the people who hired them."
- **The parallel case: IT outsourcing.** If a large share of engineering is outsourced and transforming requires bringing that competency in-house, "do you really expect the outsourcing firm to advocate for that? This is a direct threat to their business." Read alongside [[2024-08-13-cagan-kerievsky-the-product-model-in-outsourcing]], which treats the outsourcing question at length and less absolutely — worth noting the two as complementary rather than identical positions.
- **The practical carve-out — don't lose it.** Cagan closes: "there are often very good people in the management consultancies and the outsourcing firms, and just because you shouldn't hire their firms to help you transform, that doesn't mean you can't hire away some of their people." With the caveat that you need someone with real product-model experience to put them to good use.
- **Accountability line.** "[Understanding this is on you]" — the transformation cannot be delegated to a vendor. This is the same conclusion [[2024-10-29-cagan-transformation-as-a-project]] reaches from a different direction.
