# Product Therapy Ep 29 — Coaching Product Strategy

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[marty-cagan|Marty Cagan]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 29 · SVPG, October 2, 2025 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=acryXl9EVEg). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the wiki's most complete podcast-form Cagan statement on product strategy to date.** Materially deepens [[product-strategy]] beyond the [[transformed|TRANSFORMED]] Ch 22 grounding and beyond the earlier SVPG *Product Strategy* article series (Focus / Insights / Actions / Management, all already carded). This ep is the canonical podcast source for the *"you don't have a prioritization problem, you have a strategy problem"* frame, the two-components (what+why) definition, the written-narrative artifact, and the AI-in-strategy distinctions.

Key material:

(a) **What product strategy IS.** *"The job of the product strategy is to choose which hard problems to solve — hard problems for the customers or hard problems for your company."* Two goals: **take us toward the [[product-vision|vision]]** and **pay the bills along the way**. Framed as **bets** — deciding a problem is important doesn't mean you'll solve it this quarter.

(b) **Distinguishing product strategy from business strategy and go-to-market.** *Business strategy* — sell internationally or just domestically? PLG vs direct sales vs channel? Content distributor or content creator (Netflix's classic business-strategy shift)? *Go-to-market strategy* — how the product reaches customers. *Product strategy* — which problems to solve in service of the vision AND the business strategy. Business strategy drives go-to-market and product strategy; product strategy also drives go-to-market. In good companies you have all three. Getting to [[market-fit|product-market fit]] means figuring all three out.

(c) **The prioritization symptom.** *"The most obvious sign a company doesn't have a product strategy is they talk about the desperate need for prioritization. They complain that product managers aren't prioritizing. That's just a sign they don't have that product strategy, because fundamentally the product strategy is about making those choices."* Two-line coaching test: *if you have a strategy, prioritization is implicit.*

(d) **Do you need a product vision?** Small companies often don't — the founder is constantly sharing it verbally, and things change too fast to nail the artifact. Bigger companies (>~10 product teams) genuinely need it — the vision keeps everyone rowing in the same direction in a way that the strategy alone cannot: *"product strategy says 'your team, solve this problem; that team, solve that problem' — that doesn't mean we're going in the same direction. That's what vision is for."*

(e) **What characterizes a strong product strategy — the two components.** *"The output of a product strategy is two things really. It's the problems to solve this year, this quarter at a minimum … but the other part is just as important, which is the *why*."* WHAT — the choices, made explicit (what you will do, and especially what you *won't*). WHY — the strategic context: the data, the reasoning, the argument, the hypothesis. Amazon's six-page narratives are the exemplar. Written form serves two roles: (i) it's data/logic-informed so anyone can read and decide if they agree; (ii) transparency neutralizes politics — no leader can dismiss it as *"just Marty's choice vs Joe's choice."*

(f) **Focus — the hardest part.** *"If the product strategy doesn't make some hard choices about what you will and especially what you won't do, that's not very helpful. And it's not enough just to declare something as 'I believe this to be true' — a lot of product strategies are just wishful thinking."* Coaching an executive test-run: pick the *one bet* you'd stake this year on if you had to pick one, and try it. Focus should not be more than **2–3**. Focus does NOT mean stop doing [[keep-the-lights-on|keep-the-lights-on]] work or stop tech debt — that would be suicide (business unit leaders literally can't run the business). Track the percentage; it's significant but not the majority of team time.

(g) **Idiodi's coaching sequence for stakeholder-driven "prioritization problems."** The leadership offsite where 70 items collapse into "7 key pillars" or "7 big rocks" — each pillar is a run-on sentence like *"make the platform stable, more secure, more revenue, best-in-class."* That is *not* a strategy, that's a lack-of-focus problem masquerading as prioritization. In empowered teams, if you have a real strategy, prioritization is implicit. When teams say *"we need to prioritize"* — coach: *"you actually need a strategy."*

(h) **The house-building analogy** (Idiodi). If you're building the foundation and someone says "let's build the roof" — a roof is important to a house, but you don't have walls yet. Work in progress on a job site: you can't crowd the site with roof materials while pouring the foundation. Strategy can be that logical.

(i) **Earning the right to strategize.** The product leader owns the product strategy. But politically, that power has been sitting with the stakeholders. To earn the shift: (1) first convert stakeholder feature-lists to [[outcome-based-roadmap|outcome-based road maps]] (translate "build this" → "solve this problem, measured this way, we have a hypothesis about the solution"); (2) once teams deliver a few results, stakeholders view them as partners, not the IT team. **Then** the product leader can start bringing bigger problems to solve.

(j) **Empowered teams do solution discovery primarily; problem discovery insights flow BACK UP.** Coach empowered teams NOT to hoard problem discovery — even though they're capable. Reason: stakeholders have been living with their problems for years and view *"we're going away for two months to decide what really matters"* as an insult. So teams stay focused on *solution discovery*, but when they *run up against real problems* in the process (which they always do), they **bring those problems to the product leader** as inputs to next quarter's strategy. The product leader's primary job is choosing which problems to solve; the team's primary job is solving them.

(k) **Who solves and who strategizes.** Idiodi's frame: *"You want to influence the decision, not the ego. There's no price for being right in product work."*

(l) **Annual planning vs real strategy.** Most annual planning is dealing with the absence of the strategy that should already exist. It's a dog-and-pony show of pitches for funding. VCs are smarter — they fund discovery, then fund delivery based on results. Corporate annual planning funds *projects* directly, at 100x the price. SpaceX-vs-Boeing-Starliner: discovery vs annual-planning-as-project-model. There's still value in the executive team getting on the same page about business strategy, go-to-market, learnings — but that's the minor part of most companies' annual planning.

(m) **Where strategies fail** — assuming you have a real one, the most common failure is **execution.** The problems were the right ones; a team couldn't solve one (skills or data). Cagan's technique for critical problems: **assign to multiple teams**, so different skill sets can attack it and at least one is likely to break through. Discovery may also reveal that a solution won't achieve the outcome even if delivered — that's a valid strategy signal.

(n) **The Datasite example — product strategy influencing business strategy.** Business strategy input said doubled-down on one product line. Product strategy execution led to **divesting underperforming business units** to fund the growth line — a healthy example of the influence going both ways. (Cross-references [[datasite-transformation]] Ch case study and Ep 24 with Cullen + Fredell.)

(o) **AI and product strategy — two questions.** (i) *Should AI be part of the product strategy?* In many cases yes — but the harder embedded question is *what's the role of probabilistic solutions* in what you build, and where you need more guardrails. (ii) *Can AI tools help craft product strategy?* Of course, and everyone is. *"Give me a good product strategy"* → not likely. *"Improve this product strategy"* → almost always. Warning: *"the primary tool for strategy is the brain — product strategy is all about thinking clearly. Can tools bring clarity to thought? Yes. Can tools also be a substitute for thinking? Unfortunately, yes."*

(p) **Living artifact — cadence.** *"I believe that a product strategy is a living thing. At a minimum, it should be updated quarterly. In reality, I think it's living — it's just constantly updating as you learn."* Every quarter's results shape next quarter's bets. Visions rarely change; strategies change a lot.

(q) **Strategic pivots.** COVID was a real strategic pivot — many companies had a prior strategy predicated on in-person stores that became a non-starter. Vision usually survives; strategy pivots.
