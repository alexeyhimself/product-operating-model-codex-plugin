# Product Discovery In Regulated Environments

> **Citation:** [[chris-jones|Chris Jones]] · *Product Discovery In Regulated Environments* · SVPG, Jan 29, 2018 — [svpg.com](https://www.svpg.com/product-discovery-regulated-environments/). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the SVPG canonical answer to *"we can't do discovery here — we're regulated."*** Complements [[kaiser-permanente-get-care-now|Kaiser Permanente's Get Care Now case]] with a general-purpose playbook. Written for financial services, healthcare, and any company with strong internal governance / legal-approval processes.

Key material:
- **The core PM error.** Accepting "the lawyers said no" as an excuse to skip discovery.
- **What discovery actually protects the company from.** The other kind of risk lawyers don't own — building products that fail to deliver value in the market.
- **The PM's homework.** Read and understand the relevant regulations yourself. Many regulatory constraints are more *perceived* than *real* once the PM has dug in.
- **Not all discovery is equally risky.** A consumer bank can't run a landing page test (users think they're signing up for something not yet real) but can run qualitative user tests, opt-in in-person interviews with prototypes, live-data-prototype tests. Rarely a problem for lawyers.
- **The playbook fix.** If legal insists on approving every decision, agree an **informal playbook** — describing different discovery circumstances and corresponding levels of required approval. Cheap tests need little to no review; truly risky ones get the attention.
- **The relationship discipline.** Maintain an ongoing relationship with all stakeholders who could veto product decisions — meet regularly, not just when you need something. Foundation for quicker approvals + educates them on discovery.

## Notes for the coach
When a PM in healthcare, banking, insurance, government, or aviation says *"we can't do discovery,"* cite Jones's four-move sequence: (1) read the regs yourself; (2) map discovery techniques to constraint categories; (3) propose an informal playbook of approval tiers; (4) always-on stakeholder relationships.
