# Product Therapy Ep 39 — Coaching in the Age of AI

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[marty-cagan|Marty Cagan]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 39 · SVPG, April 17, 2026 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZoQIHhP7xfo). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the podcast statement of Cagan's *"model as coach"* thesis.** Complements the earlier SVPG article grounding on [[model-as-product-coach]] and the earlier Ep 20 ([[2025-05-15-idiodi-cagan-coaching-ai-impact-on-product-teams|AI Impact on Product Teams]]). This is Cagan's most direct podcast-form case for using foundation models to scale product coaching, including *how* to configure them, *what* still needs a human coach, and how AI is *exposing* product management theater rather than replacing product craft.

Key material:

(a) **Why this is a 20-year shift for SVPG.** *"We've always known the best companies work a different way. But how do those companies teach their people to work this way? It's not taught in universities or certification programs. I learned from my manager — for the first 10 years of my career, every single day I had at least one person assigned to help me get better at my job. That was called coaching."* Two things get in the way of managers coaching: *"I've never done it myself"* and *"I don't have time"* — the latter now aggravated by increasing span-of-control trends.

(b) **When AI crossed the *good enough* threshold.** *"Starting realistically — subjective — about nine months ago, I believed it had crossed that point and it was capable of being as good as a typical manager. There's occasional flashes of coaching brilliance where it looks as good as a good coach, but mostly as good as an average manager."* Cagan's own reframe: he was asking the wrong question. *"Not is this as good as a good coach? — the question is: is this good enough to help people get better at their job."*

(c) **What made models cross the threshold.** *"Two things happened. The frequency and severity of bad answers really went down — those laughable outlier answers. And at the same time, we've now got a few years of getting smarter about how to provide the right context — initially smart prompts, now strategic context."*

(d) **The role for a human manager alongside an AI coach.** *"If you're lucky enough to have a manager that is both capable AND willing to spend the time to personally coach you — count your blessings. But if you're in the 99% where you don't have that: primarily use model as coach, and bring the politics, the nuance, the areas you're still not sure about to your one-on-one. Even if your manager doesn't have deep product craft, the AI will. Your manager will at least have the company politics — 'go talk to Ed, he's the one that makes this happen with compliance.'"* Product leaders get more value from an experienced product-leadership coach (a professional or the head of product).

(e) **AI is exposing product management theater — the sharper insight.** *"About 90% of the chatter online about AI is not helpful. It's because they're using GenAI to speed up the project model. Garbage in, garbage out — and AI is highlighting that. Product management got away with all kinds of nonsense because the bottleneck was the engineers. Now the engineers are saying 'not our problem anymore.' It's pretty clear the real issue is: you were still giving us worthless stuff to build."* And competitors can copy you faster than ever, so lack of strategy and lack of discovery skill are amplified.

(f) **Idiodi's expectations-shift corollary.** *"If personalization is easier to do, people want more personalization. If automation is easier, I want less errors. This is not a downsizing moment — this is a bar-raising moment for the discipline. People will see our garbage faster; they will recognize our inability to solve real problems faster than ever, because everybody can build."*

(g) **Product creators vs product leaders — the coaching split.** Both benefit from *model as coach.* Creator work is discovery-heavy (**build to learn**); engineer work is delivery (**build to earn**). Leader work is strategy-heavy: *"the product vision, product strategy, team topology, objectives for each team, strategic context in general — that's also necessary for the AI coach in order to provide good coaching."* Cagan feels comfortable ~80% craft/20% politics for creators; the opposite for leaders — which is why leaders still need more human coaching.

(h) **Product sense as the primary starting point.** *"The primary benefit of a coach is developing product sense — the table stakes for both creators and leaders."* What that really means: learn about your customers (and, in B2B, the different kinds of users at each customer); learn your industry; learn regulation; learn competitive landscape; learn the data (levers, what good looks like, what our current state means); learn viability (usually 5–15 dimensions of the business — legal, compliant, privacy-respectful, affordable-to-build, monetizable, marketable, sellable). *"If you don't develop product sense, you're not helpful to the company. You also probably have a big target on your back for the next layoff."*

(i) **Are public LLMs enough?** The three major models (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) — out-of-the-box very good. But they've read the product literature — and *"more than half, probably 80–90% of the content on product is about the project model."* → **You have to tell the LLM which operating model you want to work in.** *"I say 'SVPG content, Teresa Torres content, Shreyas Doshi content — prioritize that. Don't feed me stuff from the 1990s.'"*

(j) **Feeding the model your strategic context.** *"In order for a product team to make good choices, they need the strategic context — the product vision, the strategy, the team topology, the team objectives. If you can point your large language model at your strategic context, it's amazing how much better the coaching is — because now instead of talking generically about jobs or marketplaces, it can talk about **your company, your vision, your strategy.** Questions become 'how is my team helping the product strategy? What team does my team depend on? I'm working on this problem — what problems does that depend on?'"*

(k) **Automating PRD generation is a fast track to bad products.** Cagan's example of the 90% of AI-chatter that's *not* helpful: *"'I can tell the model to analyze customer feedback, put the most-requested features on a roadmap, prioritize the roadmap, then generate a PRD, then generate acceptance criteria, hand it to developers — I'm done.' Do you realize you've just demonstrated you are totally useless, easily replaceable? You took a terrible process to generate bad products and now you're doing more of them faster."*

(l) **Onboarding compression.** *"3 months to a real first-class PM used to be the average with a good coach one hour a week. Today that can happen significantly faster — instead of an hour of coaching a week, I have this in my pocket. My guess is closer to a month. Real product craft skills accessible to literally anyone with an internet connection."*

(m) **Guardrails for the non-deterministic nature.** *"Even when it's not a great answer, it's usually not a bad answer. It may take you 30 minutes of asking about this rather than 5 minutes if it was a perfect answer — still way better than the alternative."* And distinguish AI-as-coach (developing product sense) from AI-generating-PRDs (automation): *"If you think that's what your job is, then you just automated your job."*

(n) **Overdependence signs.** *"People just frankly not thinking and saying 'OK, well this is what I got.' It is human nature to want a recipe book, a playbook, a process. The problem with processes is it's too often used as a substitute for thinking. I want to teach people to think — use the coach to challenge your thinking, improve your thinking, call you out when you're not thinking."*

(o) **How to instruct the coach.** *"Emphasize you want it to play the role of coach. Say 'I'm not using you for affirmation — I'm using you to help me get to good outcomes. That means pointing out things that may be very difficult.'"* Cagan has played with 50 phrasings, sometimes extreme, to get the model to hold that role. Interfaces: natural-language chat (comfortable for coaching); over-the-shoulder / agentic monitoring (the developer pattern — could work but feels Big Brother-ish); more will emerge.

(p) **AI is surprisingly good at office politics.** *"For a while people said 'yeah, it knows customer discovery but it doesn't understand politics.' I've realized the opposite — the models are actually quite good at coaching politics. There's a lot of content out there on the topic — Christian and I have contributed a good chunk of it."* Cagan's example: coaching a product leader through a strong-account-ownership head of sales — describe the head of sales's concerns, ask for advice on how to deal with that — *"it is remarkably good at giving you tips for earning that trust."*

(q) **The Idiodi persona trick.** *"AI is not political — it's not going to use you against you. There's a baked-in psychological safety. I even set up personas — I create a persona for my manager, tell it everything I know about my manager, and any time I'm going to head-of-sales I say 'remember, Marty doesn't like when things are presented this way.'"*

(r) **The rise of the triple-threat product builder.** All roles are *building.* Product managers, designers, engineers alike — the primary distinction is what they build FOR: PM/designer *build to learn* (prototypes for discovery); engineers *build to earn* (production). Any of us should build prototypes. Interviews at top companies now: *"what's your favorite prototyping tool? Here's a problem — prototype a solution and show me how you'll test it. How will you know their chief compliance officer is OK? How will sales be able to sell it? How will customers truly buy it?"* Triple-threats — people great at all three functions — become more common because coaches (AI + human) help each role learn adjacent skills.

(s) **Where AI coaches still fall short — leaders vs creators.** *"For creators — 80% craft / 20% politics. For leaders — opposite. Now we're not just talking about principles, we're talking about Joe, who is a real pain, and we need a way to deal with Joe. Especially product leaders who don't have the experience/foundation they should — those are the ones that need more people."*
