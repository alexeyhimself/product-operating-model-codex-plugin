# Learning Fast vs. Failing Fast

> **Citation:** [[chris-jones|Chris Jones]] · *Learning Fast vs. Failing Fast* · SVPG, Dec 13, 2017 — [svpg.com](https://www.svpg.com/failing-fast-vs-learning-fast/). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the article-form root for the [[learning-over-failure]] principle**, and the wiki's sharpest statement of *why the "fail fast" slogan is actively harmful* rather than merely tired.

Key material:
- **The slogan's intent is good, its effect is not.** "Fail fast" / "embrace failure" exist to shake organizations out of conservative, optimize-the-known patterns — a real problem. But the phrasing "robs us of the opportunity to call out the behavior and mistakes that truly need to be corrected." Worst case: "lumping together the concepts of risk taking and preventable mistakes leads to sloppiness."
- **Reclaim the word.** Failure should mean cut corners, bad decisions and preventable mistakes — *not* discovery outcomes. Jones's two paired examples: a prototype that falls flat in user testing is **not** failure, but shipping a foreseeable security flaw is; an A/B test showing no retention lift is **not** failure, but shipping a product that missed a critical facet of the customer's workflow is.
- **Replace right/wrong with learning.** Jones credits the framing to Tom Chi: move a culture off "success and failure" and onto continually surfacing the insights gained along the way, celebrating what was learned regardless of whether the idea worked. *(Tom Chi is cited in passing here; per `CLAUDE.md` §2 this is not an endorsement of his work and earns no separate card or entity page.)*
- **Why discovery is where this lives.** Discovery techniques are "all optimized to get to insights as quickly and inexpensively as possible" — far more so than delivery techniques. Delivery creates the product you are confident shipping; discovery generates the insights that tell you what that product should be.
- **The line that makes it a diagnostic.** "Shipping a product without doing sufficient product discovery is almost always a failure." Closing charge: "save 'failure' for the really bad stuff, like not learning."

## Notes for the coach
Reach for this when a team has adopted "fail fast" as a slogan and is using it to excuse preventable mistakes — or, inversely, when a team is so afraid of the word "failure" that it cannot name a genuine error. Jones gives you a clean two-column test: was this a cheap test of an uncertain idea (learning), or an avoidable mistake shipped to customers (failure)? The asymmetry is the point — discovery outcomes are never failures; skipping discovery is.
