# Product Therapy Ep 32 — Coaching Roadmaps

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[lea-hickman|Lea Hickman]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 32 · SVPG, November 13, 2025 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1bkwyhVECcI). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the wiki's most complete podcast-form teaching on the outcome-based roadmap, its anti-patterns, the transitional-roadmap technique, and the *HIC:* notation on a live roadmap.** Deepens [[outcome-based-roadmap]] beyond the SVPG article grounding and complements Ep 29 ([[2025-10-02-idiodi-cagan-coaching-product-strategy|Coaching Product Strategy]]) — where Cagan established that the way to earn stakeholder trust is to convert feature-lists to outcome-based roadmaps, this ep is Hickman's *how you actually do that.*

Key material:

(a) **What a good roadmap is — and isn't.** Idiodi's frame: not "anti-roadmap" — most traditional roadmaps (feature-by-date) are simply *wasteful* because most items on them will fail to move the outcome. *"If a roadmap were just a list of ideas, it is harmless — these are ideas. The second you put a date beside an idea, it turns from an idea into a commitment, a promise, an expectation."* Even best-intended roadmaps have destroyed companies because one stakeholder's missed expectation eroded trust.

(b) **Hickman's four problems with traditional roadmaps.** (i) Most things on them fail. (ii) They're based on assumptions with no discovery. (iii) They come from leaders/execs disconnected from the work (Idiodi's plumber/pool analogy — you trust the builder's date, not your spouse's). (iv) Value is *assumed* the moment the item ships — nobody asks "was this the right thing?"

(c) **Outcome-based roadmap — Hickman's canonical shape.** *"An outcome-based roadmap ladders up to the business results we're trying to achieve."* Structure per row: **the outcome/objective the team is working on → how success is quantified (key results) → a hypothesis of the capabilities that might achieve it → labeled as feature *candidates*, not features.** *"Features are candidates. We're going to test them. We're going to test many ideas before we actually commit."* The team's alignment to viable/valuable/usable/feasible discovery stays intact.

(d) **Timeframes, not dates.** Hickman's operational stance: *"I would never commit to a date. I'd commit to a timeframe."* Acceptable granularity: *"late Q2"* or *"second half of Q2."* When a leader pushes for a specific date, ask *why* — usually it's a go-to-market/announcement need that can be mitigated (announce the value proposition on the date, deliver on availability separately). *"It's more valuable to deliver the right product than to deliver by a specific date."*

(e) **Executive push-back on the loss of a date — "change what you control."** Hickman's coaching move: *"A roadmap is a perceived tool of control. It gives me insight and control over what all these valuable resources are working on. The problem is it's false control — I've worked with teams that delivered everything on the roadmap and had zero impact on the business. The company went out of business."* → *"Change what you control. Don't worry about controlling the features. Control the strategic context — the business objective, the product vision, the product strategy. Hold the teams accountable for the definition of success they come up with, but give them the capability to build the right product to deliver on those results."*

(f) **The four most common roadmap anti-patterns** (Hickman + Idiodi):
1. **Stakeholder-driven roadmap** — collect requests from every stakeholder, prioritize the list. No discovery, no validation. Lose-lose: team disempowered, stakeholders eventually disappointed too.
2. **Executive/competitor-driven roadmap** — every time a competitor ships something, the CEO Slacks *"when are we shipping this?"* Team has no control.
3. **Quarterly fill-the-bucket / capacity-planning roadmap** — engineering has 1,000 story points; find tickets to match capacity. Roadmap-as-capacity-planning-tool, not problem-solving-tool.
4. **Feature-factory roadmap** — list of outputs with no connection to strategy or problems. Test: ask why an item is on it; the answer is *"because the boss said so"* or *"because it's on the roadmap."*

(g) **Kerberg's rule** (attributed by Idiodi): *"100% predictability equals 0% innovation."* Predictability and innovation are traded against each other; leaders should learn to want predictable **outcomes**, not predictable outputs.

(h) **Who owns the roadmap.** In an outcome-based world, **the product manager** drives and manages it. But most teams don't create their own roadmap — they get handed one, which shapes what kind of team they are: *"if I give you the solution, you become a delivery team. If I give you the problem, I empower you."* The roadmap becomes a significant driver of how the team operates.

(i) **The transitional roadmap — how to migrate.** For teams stuck with a feature-based roadmap today: don't tear it up. **Reframe each item as (problem to solve, measure of success, feature candidate)** and use it as the presentation frame. Even when leadership expected features, the change in the *conversation* is what starts the migration: *"this is the problem we're going after; this is how we'll measure success; we have a hypothesis these features will achieve it, but we're going to test."* Bonus: takes zero permission from anyone above you.

(j) **OKRs and roadmaps can coexist — if outcome-based.** Idiodi: *"Of course, if it's a communication focused on outcomes it aligns well. But if you have a list of features to build AND you tell me to increase revenue, I'll never go increase revenue because one is important and the other is urgent."*

(k) **High-integrity commitments — the "HIC:" notation on a roadmap.** Hickman calls them out with a **`HIC:` prefix** so everyone knows this specific row is a firm commitment, not an outcome bet. Rule: HICs should be **few and far between**. Idiodi's two prerequisites: (i) some form of discovery (mostly feasibility) has been done before the commitment; (ii) the commitment comes from **the builders**, not from the leaders. Leaders can set a date (like Apple's September iPhone launch); builders decide *what will be in* the release that ships on that date.

(l) **Idiodi's rule of improv (Yes, and) for saying no to stakeholders.** Never say no. *"Yes, and tell me more why you think that's a good idea. What problem is it trying to solve?"* If a stakeholder brings you an idea worth $1M and you're not working on anything more valuable, you should *listen* — you don't have evidence you're working on something better. *"You are giving some practical way to shift stakeholders from asking you for output to advocating for outcomes."*

(m) **Team-level rules of thumb** (Hickman + Idiodi):
- *"Never tell people what you're working on without telling them why you're doing it."*
- *"Annotate your roadmaps with outcomes."*
- Ask of any item: *"what evidence do I have that this is valuable, usable, feasible, and viable?"*
- Ask of any feature request: *"what problem is it trying to solve? How will we measure success?"* — if you can't answer those two, don't do it.

(n) **The iPhone 17 example (Idiodi).** *"Do you know what's going to happen this September? iPhone 17. But what's going to be *in* it? We don't know. That's the HIC: the date is known, the contents are what teams control — what they've discovered and validated they can deliver within that timeframe."*
