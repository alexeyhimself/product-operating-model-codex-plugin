## Notes for the coach
- **Read the disclaimers first — they are load-bearing.** Cagan explicitly refuses the Silicon-Valley anti-MBA position (he quotes Musk's "avoid hiring MBAs" and says "I'm *not* one of those people"). His actual claim: MBA admits "often have the right raw materials to be coached and developed into exceptionally strong product managers," but need "critically important coaching and 'unlearning'." A Google friend's term for it: "de-program." **Do not cite this article as anti-MBA** — it's an argument about curriculum and about what coaching must therefore cover.
- **Scope is much wider than PMs.** Cagan names VCs and investors, board members, CEOs, CFOs, GMs/business-unit leaders and "countless other stakeholders." That's what makes it a *transformation* article rather than a hiring one: "these issues may need to be unlearned by the key senior leadership team."
- **The four problem areas** — the citable structure:
  1. **Equating management with leadership** — the assumption that a manager is a leader, or "the boss." The coaching correction for new PMs: "you are actually *not the boss of anyone*. As a product manager, you are an individual contributor. You are *true peers* with your designer and engineers. Moreover, you need to *earn your spot*."
  2. **Not knowing what you can't know** — the illusion of predictability, "the religion of business cases," blind faith in analytics, and "the pressure to have 'the answer'." The distinctive harm: graduates believe they're *supposed* to know these things, so not knowing reads as personal weakness rather than as the nature of innovation.
  3. **The role of technology** — technology as a cost center rather than the core enabler; engineers and designers as subordinates or as outsourceable resources. (This is the dangling `technology-as-cost-center` target's best source.)
  4. **Command-and-control leadership style** — not taught explicitly but "inferred or implied" through case studies, guest speakers, business books and "the whole mythology around business leaders." Quotes Hastings/Netflix's "lead with context, not control" as the counter — see [[2020-12-22-cagan-lead-with-context-not-control]].
- **The CPG lineage — the most original argument.** Cagan attributes much of the PM-specific damage to the old consumer-packaged-goods / brand-manager model, noting that CPG product managers today "are much more closely aligned with what we in the tech world would call *product marketing managers*." His evidence: in MBA catalogs, courses teaching product management are mostly taught by **professors of marketing**. "If you were responsible for developing a new brand of soap, or a new brand of beer, this was considered primarily a *marketing* exercise, and not an *innovation* exercise." He cites Martin Eriksson's and Ken Norton's histories rather than re-telling it. Good grounding for keeping [[product-manager]] and [[product-marketing]] cleanly separated.
- **"The perfect storm of bad product" — the convergence claim.** When the CSPO and MBA pathologies meet in one company, hundreds of stakeholders trained in command-and-control and the illusion of predictability meet "an eager market of Agile coaches that tell them exactly what they want to hear" — top-down control *and* the Agile checkbox. Cagan calls this "the perfect incubator for a disease like *SAFe*," pointing to [[2018-06-30-cagan-revenge-of-the-pmo|Revenge of the PMO]]. This is a **causal** account of SAFe adoption and belongs on [[pom-vs-agile-and-safe]].
- **Practical advice he gives.** He rarely encourages the degree for a product career; if pursued, go to a top program (the value is contacts and networking), and note that "a couple years of actual work experience from a good empowered product company before b-school" makes someone "largely immune" to the four issues. An update adds that Executive MBA programs differ mainly because attendees already have work experience.
