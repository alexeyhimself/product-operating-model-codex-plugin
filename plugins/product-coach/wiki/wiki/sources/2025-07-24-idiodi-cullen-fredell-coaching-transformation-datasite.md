# Product Therapy Ep 24 — Coaching Transformation: Inside Datasite's Radical Transformation

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]], [[doug-cullen|Doug Cullen]], and [[thomas-fredell|Thomas Fredell]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 24 · SVPG, July 24, 2025 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k8qnxHlfXwU). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the two operators' inside-out narrative of the Datasite (formerly Merrill Corporation) transformation.** Uniquely valuable because Cullen was Head of Sales and Fredell was Chief Technology and Product Officer during the transformation; Idiodi (a pre-SVPG-partner Idiodi) was a hands-on internal member of the transformation team, brought in by Fredell. TRANSFORMED Ch 40 tells this story from Cagan's outside-in vantage; Ep 24 is the co-primary from the *inside*. Do not treat these as duplicative — the podcast supplies practitioner detail the book cannot.

Key material — the story:

(a) **The starting state.** A 50-year-old salesled Minneapolis company (Merrill Corporation) with 11 operating divisions. Old legacy monolithic Oracle stack; quarterly release cadence at best; internal "cubicle farm with no natural light" and fake-book bookshelves in the onboarding room — Fredell's *"this looked exactly like the background for one of those law-firm commercials."* Sales culture was book-of-business-driven, not scaled selling (Cullen: *"one sales leader had 90 direct reports"*). Product was carved out of IT; there was no product culture at all.

(b) **The CEO's vision.** Rusty Wiley (new CEO) saw the "crown jewel" — the Datasite M&A due-diligence platform — as the foundation to build a software company with tech-company valuation. Strategy = divest translations, legal-tech services, court reporting, corporate chachki (11 divisions → 1 focused platform). Get the board to approve significant technology investment. Fredell's pitch to the board: *"a technology-based business has a much higher valuation than an equivalent revenue service business — this is a value-creation play."*

(c) **The Workiva catalyst.** Wiley had watched Workiva (Cagan-coached) disrupt Merrill's financial-print business with collaborative-across-time-and-space technology. That was the demonstration: an outsider with the product model can eat a legacy service business. It shaped the entire pitch.

(d) **Fredell's opening moves.** (i) Spent weeks in Minneapolis learning people. (ii) Realized *"I just didn't have people who had done product before, and I didn't have time to coach them up."* (iii) Terrifying decision: **wiped out the management layer** and started from scratch. (iv) Brought in [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] (product + entrepreneurial + people skills) and Jeremiah Ivan (architecture + clarity) — both from Interlinks. (v) Adopted a Spotify-inspired **squad/modular** organization structure. (vi) Committed to a **microservices** platform architecture from the start — Pivotal Cloud Foundry as the training wheels; MongoDB replacing Oracle. (vii) At the first board meeting, brought a 70-slide deck; eyes glazed over; called after by the outgoing CPO saying *"you were the right guy for the job — I told him"* → *"how close a call did I have?"*

(e) **Cullen's parallel work in sales.** Not a wholesale management-wipe on the sales side — Cullen inherited *"excellent talent that had not been empowered."* Jim Collins's *good-to-great* — right people on the bus, wrong people off — plus a shift from "big book of business" to scaled selling. Real tension: *"$1 goes to product = 1 less dollar for TNE with clients"* was personal to salespeople who genuinely believed they'd do better with the money themselves.

(f) **Idiodi's trust-building method (told by Idiodi on-air, corroborated by Cullen).** *"I had no right to tell the organization what problems to solve — I had no competence in the industry. But people had to see me learn."* Spent significant time with Cullen publicly, learning from him. The New York sales-leader meeting: they asked no product questions — asked about family, kids, life — then went back and said *"this guy is cool, it's okay."* **In a transformation, trust is fundamental: forget the process, do I trust your intent enough to give you a chance to fail?**

(g) **The Datasite One launch failure — the culture-bank story.** First deal on the new platform: a financing deal for a large Irish energy customer. *"You can't not get it right when you do an M&A deal — this isn't a normal MVP situation."* The Irish customer called the sales rep: *"I learned a whole bunch of new words about how this platform is nowhere effing near ready."* Bucket of cold water. Rather than fold: pulled the company together at the Lowe's Hotel in downtown Minneapolis for **3 straight days** in July → product launched October 9th same year. Cullen's crisp frame: **"you have to have something in the culture bank if you're going to make a withdrawal from it."** The failure forced sales + product to actually solve together, and the pattern of solving together *became the company* after that.

(h) **The extended trio.** Standard product trio (PM + designer + engineer) *extended* by Idiodi and Cullen to pull in sales and service management when needed. This kept sales team engaged as supporters, not skeptics, during a long platform build with no product to show for months.

(i) **The discovery breakthrough — Todd (CRO) at the customer meeting.** Idiodi took the CRO to a customer meeting. First meeting: CRO thought the questions were dumb ("sales knows the customer"). Second meeting: CRO *"hits his head on the table"* — realized this was *validating* what the first customer said and going deeper. *"If every product person understood this, we'd have better products."* CRO then championed a product tour, called every sales office to welcome the product team. That single conversion was the pivot.

(j) **How choices got made — from reactive projects to M&A-life-cycle vision.** Fredell's vision that emerged: **the M&A life cycle solution** — not just due diligence, but extending earlier and later (pre-diligence prep → post-deal integration → operational optimization → next deal). Company shifted from project drops ("release one, release two, big drop") to problem-solving toward the vision. Strategy: *"how do we go earlier in the M&A life cycle?"*

(k) **The technology transformation numbers.** Quarterly max release cadence → **20 deploys/day** by the time Fredell left, all seamless with no customer impact. Went from viewed as "junky old company out on Energy Park Drive" to one of the best places to work for engineers in Minneapolis (via Pivotal's marketing partnership: *"the most important company you've never heard of"*).

(l) **Closing advice.** Cullen: *good-to-great* right-people/wrong-people; force collaboration and trust-building *outside the business area* (host people at your home, Todd hosted every sales leader at his childhood Cleveland home 3 weeks after Cullen joined); embrace diversity of thought (don't build a monolith of like-minded people). Fredell: **communication that resonates with the *listener's* frame, not yours** — the contractor analogy (good contractor = clear on what will happen, does what they said, circles back on issues; bad contractor = surprises everyone).
