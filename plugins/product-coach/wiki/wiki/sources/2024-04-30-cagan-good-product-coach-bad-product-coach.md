# Good Product Coach / Bad Product Coach

> **Citation:** [[marty-cagan|Marty Cagan]] · SVPG article · April 30, 2024 — [svpg.com](https://www.svpg.com/good-product-coach/). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary.** Written explicitly in the "good X / bad X" behavioral-job-description format Cagan credits to Ben Horowitz's *Good Product Manager / Bad Product Manager* (a16z) — acknowledged, not ingested (the wiki's non-SVPG-sourcing question is still open per the ingest plan's Part 4). Eleven dimensions, each stated as good-coach-behavior vs. bad-coach-behavior:

1. **Relevant experience** — has personally *seen* and *done* product well; without this, nothing else compensates.
2. **Boundaries of credibility** — knows the edges of their own experience and calls in help rather than faking it.
3. **Ask good questions, genuinely listen** — seeks first to understand vs. waiting to deliver a favorite lecture.
4. **Dedicated to developing others** — teaches you to fish vs. doing the fishing for you (consulting, not coaching).
5. **Business savvy** — fluent in the language of business (finance); product must solve for the business *and* the customer, not just the customer.
6. **Best technique for the circumstances** — recommends what fits the situation and names each technique's weaknesses vs. forcing one favorite framework everywhere.
7. **Space and time for practice** — creates low-stakes room to try new thinking vs. pushing people to learn in high-stakes situations.
8. **Honest, constructive feedback** — timely and specific, positive and critical, built on genuine trust vs. telling clients what they want to hear.
9. **Empower and enable** — helps you see your own capability vs. validating fears and adding to the noise.
10. **Principles over process** — recognizes when someone is reaching for a process/artifact instead of thinking, and pushes back on it, rather than facilitating the avoidance.
11. **Defining success** — measured by *your* outcomes (and gives you the credit) vs. measured by completed activities.

Frames the whole article against a specific failure mode SVPG sees repeatedly: ~90% of self-described "product coaches" and "product management trainers," including in the US, are "deeply vested" in the feature-team model because that's what their clients expect and what they personally know — precisely the population SVPG's informal coach network is built to route around.
