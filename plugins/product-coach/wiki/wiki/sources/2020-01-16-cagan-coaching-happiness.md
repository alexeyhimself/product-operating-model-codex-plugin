# Cagan — Coaching: Happiness

## Role
**Primary (SVPG canon).** The most personal entry in the coaching series, and the one with the least coverage elsewhere in the wiki: the manager's responsibility for whether a PM finds the work meaningful, is recognised, and avoids burnout. ## Notes for the coach
- **The framing move.** Cagan concedes the topic sounds odd — it is not the manager's job to make people happy — then inverts it: *a manager can very easily be responsible for a PM being miserable.* "People join a company but leave a manager."
- **The six levers:** *meaningful work* (the largest factor in his experience, ahead of compensation — and the PM often can't see how her one team matters, so say it explicitly and often); *personal relationship*; *personal recognition* (people who say they don't need it usually mean they don't want it *publicly*); *work habits*; *modeling good behaviors*; *career planning*.
- **The want-to vs. have-to distinction** is the sharpest diagnostic here. Long hours because people are absorbed in meaningful work is a different situation from long hours because they're pressured or forced. Cagan is blunt: if it's the latter, *"you very likely have teams of mercenaries and not missionaries, and this entire topic is probably not something you care about."* Ties directly to [[missionaries-vs-mercenaries]].
- **Modeling beats telling.** The "do what I say, not what I do" manager who works crazy hours produces a spiral of early-in/late-out/all-hours email regardless of what they say. Share how *you* recharge.
- **Career planning may mean helping someone leave.** His example: an exceptional PM who realised the job was not what she wanted from her life, and whom he encouraged into her actual passion. The manager's power over an employee's life cuts both ways.
- Practical recognition list he offers is deliberately small and personal (concert tickets, a conference ticket, dinner for two) — and he notes he sometimes paid out of pocket, framing it as self-interested rather than altruistic: *"any good manager knows that they are only as good as their people."*
