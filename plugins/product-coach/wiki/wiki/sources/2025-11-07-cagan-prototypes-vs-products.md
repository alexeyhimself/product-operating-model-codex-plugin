# Cagan — Prototypes vs Products

> **Citation:** Marty Cagan · "Prototypes vs Products" · svpg.com, 2025-11-07 — [read the original](https://www.svpg.com/prototypes-vs-products/). Third in the "product creator series," follows [[2025-09-17-cagan-forward-deployed-engineers|Forward Deployed Engineers]]. Slim provenance card; the article is **not reproduced** here.

## Role
**Primary (SVPG canon) — supporting deepener for [[build-to-learn-vs-build-to-earn]].** Root article: [[2026-04-16-cagan-build-to-learn-vs-build-to-earn]] names and dates the distinction; this article (five months earlier) diagnoses the specific *confusion* — product managers without an engineering background mistaking a high-fidelity live-data prototype for something close to shippable.

## What it backs
- concepts: [[build-to-learn-vs-build-to-earn]] (supporting — the enterprise-class complexity list that explains *why* a prototype isn't close to a product; the named two-class tool split)

## Notes for the coach
- **Who this article is for, by Cagan's own framing.** Not customers/stakeholders (who've long been confused about prototypes vs. final products) — this is aimed at **product managers**, specifically ones without an engineering background, who've "embarrassed themselves in front of their engineers" by treating a prototype as nearly product-ready.
- **Why the confusion is newly common.** The new prototyping tools let PMs shape the product *directly* (not just influence design/engineering from the side) — a good development overall, but it surfaces a gap for PMs who never internalized how much distance separates a prototype from a commercial product.
- **The enterprise-class complexity list (new to the wiki) — business complexity:** real products often reflect dozens to hundreds of use cases and complex business logic (vs. the few use cases used when *learning* to prototype); enterprise-class solutions can mean thousands of use cases and constraints.
- **Runtime complexity (new to the wiki):** reliability ("reliability is our most important feature"), instrumentation/observability, performance at scale, internationalization (languages, currencies), integrations, zero-downtime maintenance, fault tolerance, data security, compliance, disaster recovery.
- **Important carve-out.** Internal tools and customer-enabling products usually have *lower* operational demands than customer-facing commercial products — "a shorter path to what we would consider product quality." Don't apply the full enterprise-complexity bar uniformly.
- **The two tool classes, named concretely.** Prototyping tools — Lovable, Bolt, Figma Make — for build-to-learn. Professional-build tools — Claude Code, Cursor — for build-to-earn. Skilled users of each use them very differently, "because they are each using their respective tools to solve very different problems." Corroborates (doesn't replace) the 2026-04-14 Women-in-Product talk already on [[build-to-learn-vs-build-to-earn]].
- **On tool-vendor hype.** Some prototyping-tool vendors claim capabilities "they are nowhere near able to deliver on" — sometimes marketing exaggeration, sometimes genuine cluelessness on the vendor's part. "Buyer beware."
- **The open question, explicitly left open.** Could code-gen tools eventually go from prototype to enterprise-class product in 3–5 years? Cagan: risky to say something *can't* happen; he's aware of research in this direction but hasn't seen evidence it'll land in that timeframe (cites the limits of spoken language as a specification language); and — his actual point — it doesn't need to be solved as long as discovery and delivery each have good tools.
