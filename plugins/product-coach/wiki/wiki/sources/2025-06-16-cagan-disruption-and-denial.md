## Notes for the coach
- **The Netscape parallel (Cagan's own biography).** As VP of Platform and Tools for Netscape Communications in the mid-1990s, Cagan's job was evangelizing the Internet as a new platform to developers and product companies. The single most common objection then: people didn't want to believe the Internet both enabled and required a different approach to discovery, delivery, and distribution — their old Waterfall ways were "still just fine." The second most common: "our data is too sensitive to be stored in the cloud." This experience was part of why he wrote the first edition of *[[inspired|INSPIRED]]*.
- **The generative-AI echo, verbatim-gist.** Objection 1 today: "yes, impressive, but nothing really changes — we still discover and deliver products much as we used to, and AI is essentially just another feature." Objection 2: "we aren't suitable for a probabilistic solution because [any of a dozen reasons], and we can't build on something that might hallucinate or that we can't test for every situation in advance."
- **Where the second objection is answered.** Cagan points to his own [[2025-06-09-cagan-creating-intelligent-products|Creating Intelligent Products]] article for how strong product teams mitigate hallucination-style risks with appropriate techniques, rather than dismissing probabilistic technology outright.
- **The salesforce.com footnote.** Cited as the clean historical counter-example: a company that saw the real opportunity in the new enabling technology (the cloud) well before most customers understood it, then rode the technology-adoption curve as customer understanding caught up.
- **The closing framing.** Startups will move quickly and aggressively on the new opportunity; large enterprises with much to lose will often find excuses to deny or resist — "so it goes in our industry." Either build the intelligent product yourself, or wait for a competitor to show your customers a dramatically better solution.
