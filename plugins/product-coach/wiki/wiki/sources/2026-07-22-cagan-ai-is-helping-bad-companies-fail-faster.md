# Cagan — AI Is Helping Bad Companies Fail Faster (Execute to Win)

> **Citation:** Marty Cagan with Lee Benson · *Execute to Win* / *Show Your Value* podcast · YouTube, published July 22, 2026 — [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fq9mtOYQKpI). Slim provenance card; the transcript is **not reproduced** here.

## Role
**Primary (SVPG canon) — and the spoken precursor to [[2026-07-23-cagan-the-ai-productivity-paradox|*The AI Productivity Paradox*]], published the very next day.** Cagan says on air *"my next article is on this topic."* Its distinct value over the article is a **first-person recantation**: he states plainly that his own prediction about GenAI was wrong, which makes this a [[your-evolving-thesis]] source as much as an AI-cluster one.

Key material:

(a) **The recantation — the title claim, in his own words.** *"When Gen AI first came out, I thought it would be **the great equalizer**, and now finally so many companies would be able to compete with those really good companies and close the gap. **I was 180 degrees wrong on that.** What's happened is the good companies are using it to get even better and faster. The bad companies are using it to waste money even faster."* The mechanism: *"most companies are using AI to speed up their project model, their old model. So it's just **garbage in, garbage out, faster**."*

(b) **"It was never the engineers" — Cagan's version of the wrong-bottleneck claim.** *"It's pretty obvious now even to those that are a little dense that it's never been [that] the engineers were expensive and they were slow — **but it was never the real bottleneck**."* Compare [[2026-09-17-idiodi-torres-discovery-in-the-ai-era|Torres, two months later]], arriving at the same conclusion from the discovery side: *"have we spent 20 years trying to reduce the cost of delivery only to discover the real bottleneck is decision quality all along?"*

(c) **"Measuring tokens is just output."** *"Token cost — that's just output. It's not saying anything about the outcomes."* And the closing line of the argument: *"I have seen AI generate **amazing answers to the wrong questions**."*

(d) **The learning-cost collapse, concretely.** The host describes writing off **$27M** of software. Cagan's reply: *"the good thing is, today you could make that same mistake for more like **$2,000**."*

(e) **Failing in an experiment vs failing in the market.** *"About 85% of products fail in the market"* (attributed to HBR). *"In good companies they fail constantly, but they fail not in the market — in what we call product discovery, where it's a very low-cost, low-risk, low-time experiment… If you fail in discovery, that's not failure, that's just an experiment. There's no fail, there's just learnings. If you decide to go forward and build a product and you fail, that's real failure. It's very expensive. It can cause reputational risk. It could put humans at risk."*

(f) **The four formulations of the same distinction — a lineage note worth keeping.** Cagan names them all: **Y Combinator** — *"build things that don't scale, then build things that do scale"*; **his own original teaching** — *"first you figure out how to build the right product, then you figure out how to build the product right"*; **early Google** — *"we fake it before we make it"*; and **[[jeff-patton|Jeff Patton]]'s** build-to-learn / build-to-earn, *"which I got from him like 10-plus years ago."*

(g) **"If you're not testing with real users inside of a week, you're not doing it very well."**

(h) **25–50 discovery iterations per week, pre-GenAI.** *"Even before Gen AI, we were doing 25 to 50 iterations per week. With generative AI, that's easy."* ⚠️ **Higher than the figure the wiki carries elsewhere** — *INSPIRED* says 10–20/week (quoted in [[continuous-discovery-habits|CDH]] Ch 9), and [[build-to-learn-vs-build-to-earn]] says "10–20+ prototypes per week." Record as a 2026 restatement, not a correction of the book.

(i) **The customer discovery program.** *"You recruit anywhere from 10 to 20 [customers] that care deeply more than most… they are willing to work with you in order to very quickly discover a solution that they love and works for you."* Distinguished from betas and pre-releases, which are **delivery** techniques.

(j) **ROI estimates are nonsense — and everybody knows it.** *"I was taught this principle as: **you need to know what you can't know, and you need to admit what you don't know**. And what are the things you cannot know? I would argue **your ROI estimates are nonsense**, because it depends entirely on the solution your team actually comes up with. If the solution is mediocre, your revenue numbers will be all fantasy. If they solve it really well, your numbers will prove very pessimistic."* The uncomfortable half: *"when the CEO says they want ROI projections to decide what to invest in, **their stakeholders lie to them. I've been in that game. I've lied that way too.**"* The fix is the leader's: *"**Don't even make up those numbers. Go run experiments and show me the evidence.**"*

(k) **The VC analogy.** A VC does what a leader of a company does, for a portfolio: seed money to try an idea, real money only if the experiment goes well. *"They are not believing an ROI claim from some person pitching them."*

(l) **The payoff curve is wildly non-linear.** *"If the product isn't very good, it's not like 50% of what you hoped for buys it — maybe 5% buys it. Most products make virtually nothing, except the good products, which make a boatload."*

(m) **SpaceX vs Boeing Starliner — the product model vs the project model, same contract, same money, same time.** *"Both SpaceX and Boeing bid and won the US government contract at the same time, same amount. One of them works in the product model — SpaceX — and the other works in the old model, which is called the project model."* SpaceX beat the deadlines; when Boeing's ship reached the space station *"it was so bad that NASA said you can't even bring the astronauts back on it — too likely that they will die. So who had to rescue them? SpaceX."* Cagan's reading: *"they both have talented engineers. **The difference is the leadership, the cultures and the model they use to work.**"* And on the early SpaceX explosions: *"they were blowing up rockets left and right because they were all prototypes and they were learning very quickly — as opposed to the model of very carefully documenting and doing this very formal process, and at the end finding out it wasn't even a good ship."*

(n) **Trainline, in Cagan's own voice.** UK rail deregulation created a licensing mess; Trainline existed but *"they were terrible at everything we're talking about — they didn't even have a mobile app."* KKR bought them, replaced the CEO *"with a terrific woman from eBay"*, who brought in a head of technology and a head of product; the three led the transformation to the UK's largest IPO to date. Concretely: engineering brought **in-house** from Eastern European outsourcing, obsolete tech stack replaced, missing competencies introduced, empowered teams given hard problems starting with *"create a mobile app that everybody wants to use."* Result: *"the number one rated travel app on the app store Monday to Friday"* (Uber wins weekends). Complements the Moore-rooted [[trainline-transformation]].

(o) **Necessary but not sufficient — the multi-sided-market version of viability.** *"Everybody understands that if your customers don't love it, you fail. However, what a lot of people don't realize is that's necessary but not sufficient. You have to solve the problem in a way your customers love **but also** satisfy the needs of the rail providers, the government, the legal side, the unions. **That is what actually makes it hard.**"* And the reframe of stakeholders: *"each of those stakeholders is there to protect some major asset in the company."*

(p) **Waymo as the hard case.** *"Any government can shut them down. They have to earn the right by proving safety"* — hence the public safety reporting, including that you are safer in your own car when there are more Waymos on the road. Plus **human-in-the-loop** for unusual situations, and usability work aimed at putting a first-time rider at ease.

(q) **Transformation timelines, banded by org size — more specific than the book's range.** Under ~100 people: *"literally a couple of weeks"*, because all the key people fit in a room and the CEO leads it. 100–1,000: *"closer to a year"* — results in a couple of months, but the rollout has to traverse the [[technology-adoption-life-cycle|technology adoption curve]]. Enterprise with many divisions: *"up to several years… each division kind of has to be treated as its own transformation."* And the honest tail: *"it can be anywhere from weeks to **never**, because the leaders run out of patience."* Compare *TRANSFORMED* Ch 48's *"six months to two years."*

(r) **Why so few companies adopt the model.** *"The most common reason is because **they've never actually worked that way before, the leaders**. They don't even know what that really means. They think they can ask McKinsey what to do."* Converges with [[2025-11-20-idiodi-the-real-risk-of-ai-in-product|Idiodi's]] independent version.

(s) **Humility as the root cause, with Jobs as the example.** *"A lot of these high-powered, famous, high-valuation tech-powered companies are not necessarily known for their humility. But **it actually takes humility to do great products**, because so many leaders think that they know what needs to be built. And that right there is the root of most of the failures."* And: *"even Steve Jobs understood that deeply. **He didn't know what had to be built. He just knew when it wasn't good enough.**"*

(t) **The AI product coach, with dates.** *"Today we strongly recommend to all the companies we work with that they set up the foundation models as an AI coach. You don't even need to spend any extra money for that… For about two years we've been testing this, and for most of that time the models were not good enough — they were wrong as much as they were right, and the severity of the wrong was often pretty bad. **About nine months ago that changed.** Now it's as good as a typical good manager playing the role, and better than most."*

(u) **Stakeholders implicitly own value in the project model.** *"In the old model, stakeholders control product roadmaps… but in that model **the stakeholders are implicitly taking responsibility for value**. They're the ones putting the solution on the roadmap. So you can't blame the teams when it doesn't deliver the value — although they get blamed anyway."*

## Notes for the coach
- **Lead with (a) when a leader is optimistic that AI will close their gap with the best companies.** It is Cagan contradicting his own earlier prediction, which lands differently from a third party saying it.
- Pair (b) with Torres's Ep 44 version. Two independent SVPG-orbit voices, two months apart, concluding the bottleneck was never delivery.
- **(j) is the most actionable item for a leader.** The instruction is not "improve your ROI model" — it is *stop asking for the number*. Expect resistance; (k) gives the respectable precedent (VCs), and [[high-integrity-commitments]] gives the thing to offer instead.
- **SpaceX vs Boeing (m) is strong but sensitive.** Cagan explicitly brackets the personalities (*"take away all the personality stuff"*) and argues only about the operating model; keep that framing, and keep the claim to what he says — same contract, same money, same window, different model. Do not editorialise further.
- (h) conflicts numerically with the 10–20/week figure elsewhere in the wiki. Flag both when quoting; don't average them.
- (q) supersedes nothing — *TRANSFORMED* Ch 48's "six months to two years" is still the book's line. Use the size bands when someone asks "how long for **us**."
- The host's own business (*Dinner Table*) is used as a running worked example; it is **not** an SVPG case study and should not be cited as one.
