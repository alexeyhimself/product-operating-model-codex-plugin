# Agency vs Ambition

> **Citation:** [[marty-cagan|Marty Cagan]] · SVPG article · July 2, 2025 — [svpg.com](https://www.svpg.com/agency-vs-ambition/). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — introduces ambition as a distinct, prior concept to agency**, not previously named in the wiki's existing [[coaching-agency|agency]] treatment (which is rooted in *Product Therapy* Ep 1 and covers agency alone).

- **Definitions.** *Ambition* = wanting to be the best you can be, for yourself, your team, and your company. *Agency* = taking the initiative and actions to actually impact your job, team, and company. Cagan's claim: **the prerequisite for strong product people is high agency, but the prerequisite for agency is ambition** — skills without ambition rarely get used, because the motivation to use them is missing. He states he finds lack of agency more understandable (people conditioned by command-and-control systems) than lack of ambition, which he says he struggles to understand at all.
- **The methodology-defense argument.** Answers an objection Cagan says he's heard more of recently: "even at the best product companies not every team is empowered, so why study them at all?" His response, re-quoting his own earlier line from [[2019-08-29-cagan-product-vs-feature-teams|"Product vs Feature Teams"]] — *"even in what I consider the best product companies, not every product team is empowered"* — is that the right question was never "is any company perfect" but **"what is the predominant model at a company, and is that model generating the necessary outcomes?"** Pockets of feature-team behavior exist even at the best companies, and pockets of empowerment exist even at weak ones; assessing an org means identifying its *predominant* model, not cherry-picking exceptions in either direction.
- **Studying the best is not optional, in any field.** Sports, medicine, art, music, engineering, literature, physics — every serious discipline looks to its best performers for inspiration (Olympics, World Cup, Nobel Prize as the institutional versions). Cagan says he has never before had to argue for *why* studying the best matters — only, until now, about what "best" means.
- **The Federer anecdote.** Roger Federer won just under 80% of his matches over his career — yet only 54% of the individual points he played. Excellence at the aggregate level does not require perfection at the point level; the same logic applies to product companies and their individual teams.
- **Diagnosis: lack of ambition, not the exceptions, is often the real objection.** Cagan's read is that some in the product community resist comparison to the best because they lack the desire to improve themselves, and gravitate toward voices telling them they're already fine — the "geography/market/culture/regulation is different for us" objections have shifted, in his account, from "it can't work outside Silicon Valley" (disproven by the growing case-study base) to "even the best aren't perfect, so why bother."
