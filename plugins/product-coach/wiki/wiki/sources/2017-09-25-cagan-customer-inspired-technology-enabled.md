# Customer Inspired; Technology Enabled

> **Citation:** [[marty-cagan|Marty Cagan]] · *Customer Inspired; Technology Enabled* · SVPG, Sep 25, 2017 — [svpg.com](https://www.svpg.com/customer-inspired-technology-enabled/). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the article-form root of the SVPG tagline**, three years earlier than the [[2020-05-20-cagan-customer-inspired-technology-enabled|2020 talk]] of the same name, which the wiki had been citing as the source. The talk is reinforcement; **this is the root.** It is also the single richest exemplar-story source in the corpus outside the case-studies folder.

Key material:
- **The thesis.** *"Customers are consistently not the source of true value creation and innovation… So then what is the source of true innovation? …the answer is the engineers."* Opens on Bezos: *"No customer ever asked Amazon to create the Prime membership program."*
- **The line the wiki quotes constantly, in its original home:** *"If you're just using your engineers to code, you're only getting about half their value."*
- **Five worked exemplar stories:**
  - **Amazon Alexa** — began as the Echo, a voice-controlled music speaker. *An engineer* built a prototype making it the voice controller for a streaming TV device; that prototype was the defining moment that revealed the smart-home hub.
  - **Disney MagicBand** — by 2011 DisneyWorld was becoming less fun (lines, crowds, falling return rates). Disney chartered "Next-Generation Experience" to *"reinvent the vacation experience, and keep Disney relevant,"* built a discovery lab behind Epcot, prototyped an RFID wearable, and invested **~$1B** in new park digital infrastructure. Cagan's gloss: large companies need *"the leadership and courage to make some big bets."*
  - **Google Translate** — a decade of minor quality gains, then more progress in one year than the prior ten, when engineers recognized an enabling technology (early applied machine learning). They shipped it **without announcing it**, to see whether users would notice unaided. They did.
  - **Apple iPhone** — while Blackberry, Palm, Nokia and Motorola *ran focus groups*, which told them to drop the touch screen (the Palm Treo's was poor), Apple saw the technology's potential. *"That's not how you create disruptive products."*
  - **Workiva WDesk** — the counter to "you have to be Google/Amazon/Apple/Disney." Serving finance and accounting staff at large public companies — long hours, thankless, viewed internally as overhead. Competitors *"were literally building what their customers were asking for"*; Workiva instead made early bets on cloud infrastructure and a rich modular web front-end, reasoning that leading-edge technology would also **attract and retain top engineers**. Result: NPS above Apple's, *"unheard of in the enterprise software world."*
- **The six keys to leveraging strong engineers** — the article's framework, and the reason to keep the card:
  1. **Provide engineers business context** — vision, strategy, analytics, goals, contractual and legal constraints. *"They can handle it, and they will appreciate it."*
  2. **Connect engineers with customer pain** — first-hand exposure; *"very little is actually more motivating to an engineer."*
  3. **Understand constraints vs. requirements** — the PM's job is to supply the underlying constraints (legal, financial, sales, marketing, manufacturing), not requirements, yielding *"significantly more degrees of freedom."*
  4. **Give engineers time in discovery** — *"easily one of the best ROI things you can do."*
  5. **Measure the product team as a whole** — names split OKRs (separate engineering and PM objectives) as *"exactly the wrong approach."*
  6. **Competent and confident product managers** — confident enough to be collaborative and seek engineers' opinions rather than needing to know everything.
- **Table stakes, stated bluntly.** Strong engineers passionate about the vision, *"absolutely not something you want to outsource. You need [[missionaries-vs-mercenaries|missionaries]] not mercenaries."*
- **The realistic floor.** Not every engineer wants to engage at this level, and that's fine — *"but you do need at least one engineer on every product team that is willing and able to engage at this level."*

## Notes for the coach
The go-to source when a leader says *"our engineers just build what we tell them."* The six keys are a **diagnostic checklist** — walk them in order and the failing one is usually #1 (no business context) or #4 (no discovery time), with #5 (split OKRs) the most common structural cause hiding behind both.

Note the Workiva story's second-order argument, which is easy to miss and useful with CTOs: betting on leading-edge technology was **also a recruiting and retention strategy**, which in turn sustained the innovation. That is a causal loop, not two separate benefits.
