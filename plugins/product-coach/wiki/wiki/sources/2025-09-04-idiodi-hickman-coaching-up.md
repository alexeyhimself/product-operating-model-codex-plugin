# Product Therapy Ep 27 — Coaching Up (with Lea Hickman)

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[lea-hickman|Lea Hickman]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 27 · SVPG, September 4, 2025 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_ngNcxkCZSY). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the wiki's canonical teaching on *coaching up*: how an IC develops their own manager, and how to take ownership of the reporting relationship rather than treating it as fixed.** New concept for the wiki, distinct from [[coaching-mindset]] (which is about the manager coaching down) and [[stakeholder-relationship-health]] (which is about lateral partnership).

Key material:

(a) **Coaching up vs managing up — the intent distinction.** *Managing up* is about influencing an outcome / how you're perceived / career progression. *Coaching up* is about **exposing information the leader might not see, so their behavior can change** — with no career-progression motive. Hickman: *"It has nothing to do with career progression, nothing to do with making yourself seen in a better light. It's about helping someone get exposed to information they might not be open to."*

(b) **The Bill Campbell frame** (Cagan/SVPG canon, restated here): *"You can no longer be a good manager without being a good coach."* Rockstar leaders are the most receptive to being coached — same as rockstar athletes. Doing the job ≠ getting better at the job (Usain Bolt still has a coach; Steph Curry has multiple).

(c) **The taboo — why coaching up feels forbidden.** Hierarchical org assumption: leaders must know more than the people they lead. The high-performing counter: hire people smarter than you. And there's the mid-career unspoken rule that the moment you become a manager, phrases like *"I don't know / I'm not sure / I need help / I made a mistake / I was wrong / I'm sorry"* disappear — they were normal a day before.

(d) **If your manager doesn't coach you:** (1) *Ask directly for it* — being "hard to coach because you're a rock star" is real. Idiodi: *"Why does Usain Bolt need a coach? He's the fastest man on the planet."* (2) *Model out the framework you want to be coached with* — bring your leader the structure ("look, you see things I don't; you have more perspective; where do you think I can get better?"). (3) *Celebrate when they try* — "wow, I got tremendous value from your feedback" plants the seed that this is positive for your career and healthy for them to do.

(e) **The always-right manager — the jiu-jitsu move.** Convert challenge into *asking them for help*: *"Help me break this down — I need more insight."* Asking for help opens them up because they feel they're helping you, while your questions create the space to expose gaps. Diagnose the root: usually insecurity, imposter syndrome, or a prior environment that measured them on being knowledgeable about everything. Idiodi's rule: *"There's no price for being right in product work. There's just zero price."* Coaching mindset applied to the manager: shift from proving-right to shared learning. *"Confidence does not equal clarity; managers may be operating from fear, not certainty."*

(f) **The disengaged/checked-out manager** — Hickman's anecdote of the leader who kicks off a workshop declaring it the top priority, then keeps leaving the room. The technique: (i) start with positive intent (*"was there a fire? Were you pulled into something?"* — give them the benefit of the doubt), (ii) if not, *name the pattern* explicitly (*"when you kicked off you said this was the most important priority; I'm confused"*), (iii) offer a structured way for them to engage (*"would it help if I went to the meeting for you? Would it help if I sent you the summary?"*). If nothing changes across sessions, **skip-level escalation is the right move** — worst case is when everyone knows and no one talks; frame it as *"we need a leader who supports the team's success."* Cagan told Hickman herself once: *"I'm surprised you have waited so long."*

(g) **The User Manual — Idiodi's core tool for coaching up.** Every electronic in your house has a user manual you consult when it breaks. In corporate life, most people have one boss — often the same boss for years. Yet they've never deliberately, intentionally studied that person. A user manual is a personal playbook: **how they work, how they make decisions, how they like to communicate, how they receive feedback**. Content Idiodi puts in his: their goals (and how their manager measures *them*, so you can help them look good); their bonus structure and incentives; their family situation; their stakeholder battles; their strengths and weaknesses; their development plans and OKRs; their communication style ("some leaders read no emails — unless you say it in a one-on-one it will never stick; it's a $10 lunch away from getting by, cheaper than fighting all of these things"). *"It feels weird once. You only do it once."*

(h) **Reverse it too — Idiodi-as-manager writes his own user manual and gives it to every direct report proactively.** States his weaknesses explicitly (*"I suck at responding to emails — text me if you need me"*), his communication preferences, his crazy. *"Nobody hired me for my weakness. But you're going to see it. So why not put it out there? I hired you because you are great at this."* Rule for the user manual you write about someone else: **validate it** ("I took some time from my meeting — is your preference really email versus one-on-ones? Do you really get feedback this way?"). Guard against the *v1-vs-v5 problem*: the user manual you wrote when they were version 1 stops fitting when they're version 5 — and when you're version 5 too.

(i) **When NOT to coach up — when the culture doesn't support it.** Cagan (via Hickman): *"The most difficult thing to change in any organization is the culture."* If you've tried the techniques and the environment doesn't respond, the honest read is: consider whether you'll get what you need here. "It's hard to connect with someone who doesn't want to connect." At that point *"it's no longer about managing up, it's about managing risk."* (Idiodi's summary phrase.)

(j) **Idiodi's closing challenge to ICs.** *"There's no promise to you when you join a company that you should have a great relationship with your manager. Many people harshly judge their work environment by their poor relationship with their manager when they were not intentional about trying to shape that relationship."* What the company owes: clarity about where we're going. What you own: shaping the relationship.

(k) **The two core skills to become a better employee** (Hickman's closing): **ask questions and listen.** *"If you listen, people will tell you exactly who they are — but you have to listen."*
