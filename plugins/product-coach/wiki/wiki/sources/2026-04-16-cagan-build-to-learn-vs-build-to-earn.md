## Notes for the coach
- **Two ways to develop product, restated.** The **project model** (output-oriented: stakeholders prioritize features/projects, a feature-team PM writes a PRD, a designer designs to spec, engineers build the spec) vs. the **product model** (outcome-oriented: leaders identify a problem, an empowered cross-functional team discovers a solution worth building, then builds and delivers it). Notably: teams in the product model have been producing 10–20+ prototypes per week **long before generative AI** — Figma alone made this practical; AI didn't invent the practice, it accelerated it.
- **The bottleneck has moved.** Because delivery cost has dropped so far, building the feature is no longer the constraint — discovery (finding a solution worth building) is. The project model, unchanged, is now "a turbo-charged feature factory" — producing more bad products, faster, than ever.
- **Origin credit, in writing.** *"Years ago, product coach Jeff Patton... coined the phrase 'build to learn vs build to earn' to describe the difference between product discovery and product delivery."* This is the direct-attribution root the wiki previously only had via the 2026-04-14 talk.
- **Why this resonates now, per Cagan.** The phrase lands especially well with teams learning the product model in the generative-AI era, because AI has made "building" ambiguous — both discovery and delivery now visibly involve "building," just for different purposes, with different tools, and — crucially — a different meaning of "testing."
- **The PM's-contribution crisis (new to the wiki).** As teams move from project model to product model, many PMs don't know what their contribution is anymore — their old project-managing/facilitating value is gone, and they're unsure what replaces it. Some lean into engineering personally (fine for those with the technical foundation) — but the best PMs realize they're building for a **different purpose** than engineers: not to ship, but to learn whether a solution works.
- **What discovery-building actually is.** Testing a combination of technology, functionality, UX, and business constraints against [[the-four-big-risks|the four risks]] — value with users/customers, usability with users, feasibility with engineers, viability with stakeholders. 10–20 prototype iterations per week is "easy for just about anyone" today, without depending on a designer or engineer.
- **What delivery-building actually is.** A commercial-quality product to sell/service/support, with a very different risk list — scale, performance, fault tolerance, reliability, accuracy, privacy, security, operations, provisioning, internationalization. "Testing" here means confirming the product meets this list and works as advertised — categorically different from testing an idea.
- **Two practical shifts named for 2026.** (1) Live-data prototypes are now dramatically faster/cheaper than before — putting functional prototypes with real usage data in front of users much sooner. (2) Teams can test multiple approaches **in parallel** now, rather than the old largely-sequential iterate-until-confident-then-declare-victory pattern.
- **The hard part, restated.** Becoming proficient with prototyping tools and techniques is "the easy part" — the hard part is building [[product-sense|product sense]] to evaluate the learnings and guide direction.
- **The at-risk group, named plainly.** People who see themselves as facilitators/managers/"glue" for the team, uninterested in building of any kind, are "increasingly at risk." Those who embrace the builder/creator nature of the role and develop product sense + build-to-learn skill are, per Cagan, entering "a golden era."
- **Footnote 2 — an alternate phrasing worth knowing.** Y Combinator's version of the same idea: "building things that don't scale vs building things that do scale."
- **Footnote 3 — process vs. model (not yet an ingested wiki page).** Cagan is explicit build-to-learn/build-to-earn is *not a process* — it's a **conceptual model** (links to a separate, not-yet-ingested SVPG article, "Process vs Model"). Discovery and delivery aren't phases; both are continuous, and delivery generates learning too (especially once a product is live at scale) — but the operating principle is to fail (learn) in discovery rather than exposing bad ideas to all paying customers.
