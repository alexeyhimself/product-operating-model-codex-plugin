## Notes for the coach — organized by transcript section

### The reckoning framing (§ Reckoning for Product · GenAI Changes the Stakes)
- **"The reckoning has begun."** Cagan's Jan/Feb 2026 escalation from a years-long warning to a present-tense statement. The two-or-three-different-ways-of-doing-product-management debate is over — *"you can argue if you want, but your company's probably not going to be here."*
- **10× valuation multiplier.** Boards want to be valued like innovative companies — *"at least on the order of 10× more than the non-innovative companies."* A non-ideological driver of the shift.
- **Bay-Area salary bifurcation.** *"The people in the model that the good companies use, they're being rewarded like never before. Their salaries have nearly doubled in the Bay Area."* Simultaneously, old-model PMs are being laid off. Both happening.

### Feature vs empowered teams (§ Feature vs Empowered Teams)
- Tough-love restatement: in a feature team you never really needed a product manager — you needed a *project* manager to herd cats. That is what "PM theater" names.
- The [[the-four-big-risks|four-risks]] division: designer covers usability, engineer covers feasibility, **the PM covers value + viability** — the real role.
- Without a PM covering value+viability, *"your engineers aren't going to know this and your designer's not going to know this... your engineers are going to scream at you."*

### Build to learn vs build to earn (§ Build to Learn vs Earn · Prototyping Tools Boom · New PM Litmus Test)
- **Origin credit to [[jeff-patton|Jeff Patton]]** — *"in a modern product team, we're all builders. We are all builders."* The interview-first task: give the candidate a problem, ask what prototyping tool they'll use, show us the prototype and how you'll test it.
- **Two different tool suites.** Build-to-earn tools = Claude Code, Cursor (engineering); build-to-learn tools = **Lovable, Figma Make** (prototyping) — different jobs, different tools. *"You can use any tool for anything, but they're not for that."*
- **Cagan's Figma prediction:** *"I'm still expecting a real breakthrough from Figma. Figma make is clearly directional but it's not there yet but it will be there I believe. I really have a lot of admiration for that company. And if you think about it, they're perfectly positioned to do that well. If they don't do it well, somebody else will step in and take that from them."*
- **"Golden era for product people"** — the tools and methods; adoption is faster than anything before.
- **The new PM litmus test (verbatim):** *"if you don't truly love prototyping and then testing those prototypes with stakeholders, with users, with business owners, with other engineers, if you don't love that, that's not the role for you."*
- **Being great at Claude Code is nice-to-have, not essential** for a PM. Warning sign: PMs attracted to Claude Code because they're *"pining for the days where you weren't expected to build and test"* — using it as a project-management tool.

### Why coaching fails (§ Why Coaching Fails · AI Coaching Tipping Point)
- **Cagan's autobiographical coaching lineage.** *"I learned from a manager, actually many managers, who coached me over time. First as an engineer, then as an engineering leader, and then as an engineering manager, and then as a product manager, and then as a product leader."* Every product-native company (Apple, Google, Amazon, Netflix) prioritizes coaching as *the* management responsibility.
- **Google's #1 attribute of a good manager:** *"you're a good coach."* Named explicitly.
- **The 10+ year admission (verbatim).** *"We have acknowledged this problem for more than 10 years. It's just that we didn't know what to do about it."* Old-school companies want to transform, but only a small subset of leaders take coaching seriously.
- **The two reasons leaders don't coach:**
  1. *"I never worked this way myself, so I really don't know what the heck I'm talking about."* Some CEOs address this by hiring ex-Amazon/Google leaders.
  2. **Span of control** is rising *faster* than coaching efficiency.
- **SVPG's ~100-coach informal network** — helpful *"on the fringe. It's minor."* Not a scalable solution.
- **The 3-year LLM progression:**
  - **Year 1:** models were pretty weak — *"people are sending us these things saying that this is what the product model says and we're going like, where does this come from?"*
  - **Year 2:** getting significantly better — *severity* and *frequency* of bad answers both dropped.
  - **~9 months ago (summer 2025):** the tipping point — *"this new round of models from all three major players was like, damn. This is like as good as a typical manager right now. And you're not limited to a 1-hour-a-week coaching."*
- **The illegally-trained admission (verbatim):** *"We had already verified that the models had been illegally trained on our books and stuff anyway... you're not going to fight it. What are you going to do?"*

### Prompting for the product model (§ Prompting for Product Model)
- **Three prompting keys** — Cagan's canonical operational prescription:
  1. **Tell the model you want the *product* model, not the *project* model** — *"that one piece of guidance is huge."* Models were trained on a majority-project-model corpus and are otherwise self-contradictory.
  2. **Tell it to play the role of a coach** — not affirmation. It knows what "coach" means: challenge, push, point out flaws.
  3. **Tell it to prioritize** the content of [[teresa-torres|Teresa Torres]], [[shreyas-doshi|Shreyas Doshi]], SVPG — *"certain people that write about this model. When you tell it to prioritize that content, you'll get stuff that makes a lot more sense."*
- **The "wrong question" reframing.** For 6 months Cagan asked *"is the model a great product coach?"* — wrong question. **Right question:** *"is it better than what they get / good enough to reach competence?"* — a lower bar, and the answer is yes.
- Cagan's model of adoption: even friends still on the project model can prompt for that (the model will happily generate a PRD or roadmap) — *"sure, you can absolutely do that. But it's different advice."*

### Strategic context inputs (§ Load Strategic Context · Strategic Context Inputs)
- **Load:** product vision + product strategy + team topology + role/team/industry. Storage: projects, files, whatever. Reference examples at [svpg.com/examples](https://www.svpg.com/examples); the book *EMPOWERED* details strategic context.
- **The 5-minute team-topology doc.** *"If you don't have a document that has a team topology, it only takes a few minutes to create one. Just do that and then share it with the model and then it's amazing. It can tell you, well, you actually depend on these three product teams. So you're going to have to collaborate with them."*
- Load role + industry so advice is domain-specific — *"I'm on the shopping cart team in a transportation marketplace."*

### Project model prompts (§ Project Model Prompts)
- **"Garbage in, garbage out faster."** The vast majority of AI-in-product chatter is about *speeding up the project model* — collecting stakeholder votes, generating a roadmap, generating a PRD.
- **The executive realization (verbatim):** *"it doesn't take a very smart executive to realize that you are no longer necessary at this company"* if that's your job.

### AI coaching limits (§ AI Coaching Limits · Why Humans Still Matter)
- **The models are less and less wrong.** *"It's pretty dangerous to say it can't do this today."*
- **Surprisingly good at politics now.** The head-of-sales / big-ego "bro" example — Cagan tried this live with a company; the model gave the same advice a human would (earn trust; make it not "your opinion vs his opinion"; a lot published on this).
- **For product creators (PM, designer, engineer):** the model is good enough — time-to-competence used to be ~3 months with a good manager; Cagan guesses less than half that now, because it's 7×24 rather than one hour a week.
- **For product leaders:** *"every product leader should use an AI product coach. However, in my ideal world, every product leader has access to a human product leadership coach."* The two statements are not contradictory.
- **The "doesn't know it doesn't know" limit.** The models don't know **you** or your specific executives. Cagan's example: Jennifer Bailey (then a manager at Netscape; today SVP at Apple for payments; herself coached by [[bill-campbell|Bill Campbell]]) explaining to Cagan how each C-level peer reasoned — *"I was like a blind person reaching for the lights before. She's like shining a light."* No model has that.
- **SVPG's product-leadership-coach referral list** — email Cagan directly, no fee, no kickback; they'll refer to human coaches *"who have convinced us they actually understand the product model."*

### What coaching looks like in practice (§ What Coaching Looks Like · Building Product Sense Fast)
- **First phase: raise your product sense to table stakes** (Cagan: without this, *"you're useless really to everybody"*). Verbatim script:
  - Teach me our data.
  - Teach me the KPIs most important for our kind of business.
  - Teach me the industry — major players, how a company decides if it's healthy, the flywheel dynamics, the KPIs.
  - Teach me the different types of users and customers, their motivations, why they buy this product, why they use this kind of product, what other solutions they use.
- **Cagan does this himself** before engaging any new company — takes him *"from nothing"* (WSJ level) *"to some literacy."*
- **The typical agile PO contrast:** they sit down with a stakeholder who says *"you have no clue — you don't know our customers, our business, our data — you're not somebody I can partner with. It's ridiculous."*

### Frameworks for real work (§ Frameworks For Real Work)
- **Directed, problem-specific queries** once product sense is in place: *"I'm working on the customer onboarding problem. What's the best technique? Should I use the customer-discovery program here? Should I recruit users?"*
- **Named worked example: [[opportunity-solution-tree|Torres-style OST]].** *"I want to create an opportunity solution tree in the Teresa Torres style. Help me through that process."* Then dialogue on each option — risks, viability, how to test the compliance risk, what the compliance officer cares about.
- **The PM-theater contrast:** *"So many of the other people in the project model are just saying, oh, I can hit a button and generate a PRD. That's useless. That is such a worthless example use of good tokens... your job is to actually discover something worth documenting in a PRD."*

### Adoption curve reality (§ Adoption Curve Reality)
- **The two extremes.** Some CEOs say *"if you're not using these tokens every quarter, you're fired."* Other companies still don't let employees use AI at all.
- **The Standard Chartered Bank / Idiodi story.** Cagan tells a public-LinkedIn-posted story: [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] was at Standard Chartered Bank (one of Asia's largest banks) showing teams how to use AI to do their jobs. Employees said *"we're not allowed to use AI."* Idiodi asked who said that; the senior VP for compliance was introduced. Idiodi invited him to a session. The VP came, brought his entire staff within hours, and *"by the end of the day, he announced a major policy change for the bank that they are all using this"* — accelerating the adoption curve by weeks/months in one day.
- **Historical lineage:** early internet (data-in-cloud fear), personal computers, mobile — same adoption-curve pattern. Cagan: this one is *faster* than any technology change he's seen, because the competitive disadvantage of abstaining is so painful.

### Career advice + wrap (§ Career Advice And Wrap)
- **"It is beyond a tool. It is a game changer."** Cagan: *"I have never said that before about anything else... The internet was a game changer, mobile was a game changer, but this is I think really a defining moment."*
- **The 30-minute onramp.** *"Give it 30 minutes. Just use the defaults we provide."*
- **Feature-team PMs don't like their jobs.** *"I don't think I've ever met a feature team product manager that actually liked their job... they want to create great stuff that their customers love. They don't want to just be order takers delivering the next dumb feature that some executive decided."*
