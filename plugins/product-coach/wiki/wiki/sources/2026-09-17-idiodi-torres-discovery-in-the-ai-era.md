# Product Therapy Ep 44 — Discovery In The AI Era (Teresa Torres)

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[teresa-torres|Teresa Torres]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 44 · SVPG, September 17, 2026 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mLXlVnmmyfA). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local and is **never reproduced**.

## Role
**Primary — Torres's canonical statement on what 15 months of building AI products did and did not change about discovery.** Her first appearance on *Product Therapy* (the wiki already carries her via *[[continuous-discovery-habits|CDH]]* and the [[2025-06-10-torres-wille-what-is-product-ops|Torres & Wille product-ops conversation]]). Torres clears the wiki's [[teresa-torres|non-SVPG boundary test]] independently (`cagan-endorsed` — Cagan wrote the *CDH* Foreword and named it on the SVPG reading list), so this is filed `primary`, not `supporting`.

Key material:

(a) **The reversal, told as a hero's journey.** Torres's own framing of the last 15 months — the reluctant call to adventure. Her original position: *"my initial skepticism was rooted in — I don't want to advocate for anything that gives you an excuse not to think. I love AI, it's fun, it's like a better Google, but I don't want to use it in discovery. Do the work yourself."* Two catalysts flipped her: a badly broken ankle (surgery, months on the couch, time to experiment) and a research report.

(b) **What has *not* changed — the hidden structure.** *"We're not going to accomplish things if we don't start with outcomes. We're not going to accomplish things if we don't interview our customers and understand who they are. As we build solutions, we better enumerate and test our assumptions. One of my goals with Continuous Discovery Habits was: can I pull out the hidden structure underneath discovery that I really think will never change. Start with the end in mind, understand who your customers are, test your assumptions — that structure is probably universal."* And: *"It'll be 10 years from now and I think we're still going to say there's still these core fundamentals to discovery, and our tactics will probably look completely different — but I don't think the fundamentals are going to change."*

(c) **Raise the floor, raise the ceiling** — the research finding that changed her mind. *"For people that adopt AI in different skills, the impact it had for novices was it raised the floor. And the impact it had for experts was it raised the ceiling."* Coupled with the concrete jolt: *"I just taught Claude how to do [interview synthesis]. And Claude learned way faster than any human that I've taught."* She has taught customer-interview synthesis through a formal curriculum since 2017, and through coaching since ~2011.

(d) **The constraint is the organization, not the person.** *"[Discovery] requires hard cognitive thinking effort, which we already know humans don't have time for in the workplace. I want to be clear — I don't want to say people aren't capable of it. The organizational environment, the system of business, doesn't give us time to do this well."* The floor-raising bet follows directly from this: *"maybe this technology can help raise the floor given how busy everybody is."*

(e) **Did we spend 20 years fixing the wrong bottleneck?** Idiodi's framing, Torres's agreement: we drove the cost of delivery down for two decades only to find **decision quality** was the real bottleneck. Torres: *"For me, product is about decision-making. My whole career as a coach I've focused on good decision-making, critical thinking, problem solving. This to me is the heart of product… AI hasn't changed any of that."*

(f) **Judgment ≠ taste ≠ aesthetics.** Torres's pushback on the industry's taste discourse: *"I've literally heard big names in the industry say 'I will build my taste by looking at beautiful products.' Okay — that is aesthetics. That is one dimension of this."* Real judgment is *"does it meet a need I actually have and care about? Does it fully satisfy that need? Does it do it in a delightful way?"* And the correction that follows: *"your customer's aesthetics matter more than your aesthetics."* Both she and Idiodi land on judgment ≈ critical thinking, applied at the intersection of the risks — *Dark Sky* as the lovable-but-not-viable example (*"Apple's weather app is not a lovable product"*).

(g) **What a "customer interview" actually looks like in the wild — the episode's strongest new datum.** Torres now builds AI services for **Vistaly** (opportunity-solution-tree software): transcript → interview snapshot → after three snapshots, a generated OST. *"This means I have now seen what interview transcripts look like for hundreds of teams. We use this one word 'customer interview' to mean a lot of different things. I'm seeing story-based interviews — that's the style I teach. I'm seeing general interviews where people pepper the customer with all sorts of random questions. I'm seeing sales demos. I'm seeing stakeholder meetings — there's no customer in the room. I'm seeing interviews where they're just asked for their usability preferences… It's all over the place."* Her verdict after nine years of formal curriculum, plus the resources of Indi Young, Steve Portigal and Rob Fitzpatrick: *"we have plenty of resources on how to conduct a good customer interview, and we're not doing it."*

(h) **The case for an AI interviewer — and its hard limit.** *"This is why I'm open to the idea of an AI interviewer. An AI probably would be more consistently good than a human. And there's a reason for this — the human has a dog in the fight. They have an idea they are in love with and they're trying to get feedback from a customer, and they don't do that objectively."* The limit: *"I don't love it as a replacement for product teams actually interviewing customers themselves… an AI can talk to you about that, and I can learn a lot from that transcript, but I'm going to lose something in that human-to-human interaction."* Best use: *"a good AI interviewer can model what a good interview looks like, so that you might learn how to do a better interview."*

(i) **Synthetic users vs digital twins — two very different bets.** The common approach produces **population-level** trends — *"think about this like an election poll. We're not learning about an individual voter."* The second, *"typically called digital twins,"* builds an **individual** profile (a 400-question survey, or in a good version a 90-minute interview) and uses it to predict how *that person* would react. Torres: *"I actually think this is a much more interesting approach. But even in this approach, it is not yet predictive of humans. It still doesn't work."* Why she cares about the distinction: *"it's really important that product teams not lose sight of the individuals we're building for. I don't love design personas — design personas turn individuals into abstract generalizations. I introduced interview snapshots because I want you to remember every human you talk to is unique."* She grants a conditional: *"if [digital twins] help us better understand our individual customers, I'm all for it."* Idiodi adds the hyper-personalization angle.

(j) **Evals — LLMs do semantic tasks, which have better and worse, not right and wrong.** *"We use LLMs for semantic tasks. Semantic tasks don't have right and wrong answers. They tend to have better and worse answers — which is something I've been teaching forever; I talk about ill-structured problems that have better and worse solutions. It's really important we learn to let go of right and wrong and get a much better appreciation of better and worse. LLM-based products exacerbate this… so now a product team has to be able to define what does better mean, what does worse mean — for these very ambiguous semantic tasks."* See [[ai-evals]].

(k) **Defining "good" is a cross-functional team job; implementing the eval is not.** *"To do evals we have to define what does good look like in our context, put rules around that, and come up with metrics to measure those rules… This is a cross-functional team activity. It requires business knowledge — it has to be good in a way that creates business value. It requires customer knowledge. It has to be feasible."* And the disambiguation that does the most work: *"When we're talking about implementing an eval, it's typically an engineering task. A lot of people equate it to unit testing. Product managers don't define unit tests, so why would a PM define an eval? **An eval is not a unit test.** Unit tests test the logic of a function. **Evals are closer to acceptance criteria.**"*

(l) **Valuable vs desirable — a friendly, on-the-record terminology difference with Cagan.** *"I know Marty uses valuable. I sometimes still use desirable. I think that good is the same thing — it's the intersection of all the risks. This is the challenge I have with valuable: valuable is viable, valuable is feasible, valuable is usable. That's why I still use desirable — even though I understand Marty's argument that in enterprise a lot of solutions aren't desirable but we still use them. I just hope someday they become desirable."* See the lineage note on [[the-four-big-risks]].

(m) **Feasibility risk is rising again.** *"I think feasibility is becoming more important again."* Her analogy: web 2.0 required learning Ajax; mobile required the SDKs and separate Android/iOS teams before the roles merged. *"With AI it's a little bit different, because there's whole new skills around getting reliable outputs from non-deterministic systems."*

(n) **Role lines blur on teams that actually ship AI products.** *"For teams that are successfully getting a product to market, they're not worrying about who plays what role. I'm seeing product managers and designers learn how to write enough code to do evals — because to do evals well requires domain expertise your engineers may not have. I'm seeing data scientists partner with designers. I'm seeing engineers get involved on the business side… It doesn't matter what your title is. It matters if you're curious, if you want to build something, if you have a builder mindset."*

(o) **The ethics of traces.** *"If we want to build good AI products, we have to look at AI traces"* — the full back-and-forth between AI and human, tool calls and results — *"because we have to understand what the AI is getting wrong so we can start to improve it."* The problem: *"let's just talk about behavioral analytics and how not transparent we are about all the data we collect on our users. And now we're saying in order to have a good product, we have to look at every interaction you have with our AI. How often do you use an AI product and it clearly says 'by the way, we're going to look at all your conversations'? Almost never."* Her unresolved tension, and her only current answer: **be fully transparent with your customers.** A partial alternative some teams use: **synthetic data** (*"which is different from synthetic users, by the way"*) in place of production traces — *"there's problems with that"* too. Idiodi frames the same territory as the *should we build it?* question.

(p) **"AI transformations" don't transform organizations.** *"I've really internalized William Bridges's message that individuals change, not organizations… I do think individuals drive organizational change… And there's a second problem: we're already seeing that individual productivity does not necessarily lead to organizational benefit."* Torres names Cagan's article for this by title (Idiodi supplies it): *"the AI productivity paradox."* Her prediction: *"until we learn more is not better — which I'm not totally confident we're ever going to learn — I don't think people becoming AI-native will lead to AI-native organizations. I think we're going to see what we've always seen. We're not going to see companies transform. We're going to see AI-native companies replace traditional companies. It's going to be very rare that a traditional company will actually transform."* See [[transformation]] — **this converges with SVPG canon rather than contradicting it**; the divergence is about *where to intervene*, not about the failure rate.

(q) **Expect better, not more.** Idiodi: customers *"expect better."* Torres's amplification: *"They don't expect more. They expect better. But most companies are delivering more. And I think that's a really important distinction."*

(r) **Bright spots over org-level programs — and the cost to the individual.** *"I've really come around to: organizational context doesn't support good thinking. It's actually why I don't want to work at the organizational level. It takes a remarkably exceptional person on the axis of agency to do discovery well in an organization… unless it's supported up and down that organization, the only people who are going to change are the super-high-agency bought-in people. And so I don't have to work at the organizational level to reach those individuals. I actually think the best way to change an organization — whether it's discovery or AI adoption — is to create **bright spots** that show an organization what it looks like to work that way."* The honesty that follows: *"this puts an enormous burden on the individual, and I don't think we talk about that enough. It is incredibly hard to be the change maker, to constantly have agency, to be swimming upstream."* And the counterweight: *"I will also say it is way more fun. No human on earth wants to just do what they're told to do."* Her prescription is the *CDH* Ch 14 stance restated: *"pick one teeny tiny thing to do and then keep going."*

(s) **What teams should never outsource to AI.** Three boundaries, stated as such:
- **Firsthand customer exposure.** *"Keep talking to your customers. Even if you're using AI interviewers, even if it can now synthesize all that customer feedback you've been ignoring for decades because it was just too much volume — it's not enough. You need firsthand exposure to your customer. Full stop."*
- **Assumption-level testing.** *"Do use AI for building good interactive prototypes. But don't let it pull you back into testing the whole idea at a time. Still surface assumptions, test individual assumptions. That's what's going to allow you to move fast."*
- **Un-steered synthesis.** *"One of the things AI out of the box gets wrong with synthesis: it's not specific enough. We get really high-level generalized opportunities… I need to know where you are, what you're doing, why, in order to solve your problem for you."* Her bet is not on foundation labs fixing this but on **an opinionated expertise layer on top of the model**: *"we're going to see very opinionated software on how to do synthesis well. That's what's going to raise the floor — not just dumping things into NotebookLM."* Idiodi's parallel demonstration: the answer differs markedly when you tell an LLM *"I am a product manager working in the product model"* versus just *"I am a product manager."*

(t) **Choosing to be a beginner — with the privilege named.** *"I think I'm in a position a lot of people aren't in, in the sense that I can risk everything… If I had a bunch of kids and three mortgages and lived in Silicon Valley, I think I'd be a little more scared… If this has an existential impact for you, it's really hard to have agency, it's really hard to embrace change."* Her takeaway is deliberately not *you must do this*: *"we've never had this much access to new skill development. Never before. So the takeaway is: what do you want to do that is uniquely you, that you've been afraid to do? And you don't have to do it at work."*

(u) **Why she started *Just Now Possible*.** After building her own AI products (interview coach, outcome coach, a Teresa bot; then AI interview snapshots and AI opportunity solution trees) she found herself *"starting from scratch"* — the podcast exists to learn from cross-functional teams actually shipping AI products, deliberately not solo founders. Same motive as her discovery work: *"it's hard to do discovery well and you feel like you're the lone wolf in your company — how do we create a space that shows you're not alone?"*

## Notes for the coach
- Attribute **"AI raises the floor for novices and the ceiling for experts"** to the research finding *as Torres relays it* — she does not name the study on air, so cite it as her framing, not as an established result.
- Attribute the **"hundreds of teams' transcripts"** observation to Torres's Vistaly work — it is the most concrete evidence in the corpus that *"customer interview"* names wildly different activities in practice. Use it when a team claims they "already talk to customers": ask what the last three actually were.
- Attribute the **AI-interviewer case** carefully: Torres is open to it *because humans have a dog in the fight*, and explicitly not as a replacement for the team's own exposure. Never quote the first half without the second.
- Keep **synthetic users** (population-level, weak), **digital twins** (individual-level, interesting, *"not yet predictive"*) and **synthetic data** (an eval input, a partial answer to the traces problem) as three separate things. Torres separates them deliberately.
- **Evals are closer to acceptance criteria than to unit tests** is the line that unblocks most PM/engineer arguments about who owns them. Attribute to Torres.
- On **valuable vs desirable**: name it as a terminology difference between Torres and [[marty-cagan|Cagan]], not a disagreement about the underlying risks. Both say "good" is the intersection.
- On **transformation**: do **not** present Torres as contradicting SVPG. Cagan says *"most transformations fail… a lot of the things are outside of your control, they're more like the CEO's"* ([[2022-04-13-cagan-common-transformation-pitfalls-producttank-oslo|ProductTank Oslo, 2022]]) and names [[corporate-courage|corporate courage]] as Key #10 precisely because most leaders lack it. Torres's *"AI-native companies will replace traditional companies"* is the AI-era restatement. The real difference is the **intervention point** — SVPG works the CEO/org level (pilot teams, coaching the coaches); Torres has chosen to work the individual level and let bright spots propagate. Present both; the wiki does not pick.
- The **privilege caveat** is Torres's own and should travel with the be-a-beginner advice. Coaching someone into "just become a beginner again" without acknowledging the existential stakes is the misuse she pre-empts on air.
- Preserve the *Product Therapy* mental-health disclaimer when quoting the show.
