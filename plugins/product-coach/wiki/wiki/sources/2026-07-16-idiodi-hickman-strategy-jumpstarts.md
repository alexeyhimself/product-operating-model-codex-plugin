# Product Therapy Ep 42 — Coaching Strategy Jumpstarts (with Lea Hickman)

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[lea-hickman|Lea Hickman]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 42 · SVPG, July 16, 2026 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cKMZ3fsi9bg). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the wiki's canonical treatment of the *Strategy Jumpstart* as a coaching tool.** First named in Ep 37 ([[2026-02-19-idiodi-hickman-coaching-transformations|Coaching Transformations]]); this ep is the deep dive on how it's actually run — who's in the room, what homework is done, how to break through disagreement, what good alignment looks like, and how the artifact translates into team execution afterwards. Primary source for a new [[strategy-jumpstart]] framework page.

Key material:

(a) **What a Strategy Jumpstart IS.** Hickman's definition: *"An opportunity for an organization to work with an SVPG partner to either create or refine their overall product strategy. Taking all of the things we teach in the master class and putting them into practice for that organization specific to their business and their product."*

(b) **How it differs from a typical strategy offsite.** *"It's very specific about getting the right people in the room, gathering all the insights they have — telemetry, financial numbers, industry insights, user insights — bringing them together and finding areas of connection. Then making really hard decisions about where we're going to focus and quantifying what success looks like."* Assumes the corporate/business strategy is already laid out; the Jumpstart works one level below — the *product* strategy.

(c) **Who needs to be in the room.**
- **Head of product** (CPO).
- **Head of engineering / CTO** (technology decisions).
- **Head of design** (portfolio-fit decisions).
- **Key go-to-market stakeholders** (product marketing, sales) — because *"a key part of your product strategy is not only building it right and solving a problem for the customer, but also being able to bring that solution to market."*

(d) **The right number of people — no more than 8–10.** *"People who can sit around a conference table."* And critically: **people who have agency to make decisions.** *"What you don't want is people who then have to take the output of the strategy jumpstart and go get approval. You want the decision-makers in the room."*

(e) **When product teams should join.** Not by default. Only if the strategy area is one where leadership doesn't have the required depth. But *product teams are the primary source of the insights that feed the room* — their experiments, learnings, qualitative discovery on top-level ideas — so their material is present even when they aren't.

(f) **The homework before the Jumpstart.** Gather all the insights: data (telemetry, financials), industry, user, competitive. Hickman then works through the insights ahead of the meeting. What emerges is diagnostic — *"how much muscle memory or skill does the collective leadership team have around identifying areas of connection and delivering artifacts that expose those insights. So it also becomes a learning opportunity — the output of a product vision or Jumpstart is only as good as the inputs. And so the insights are those inputs."* Mitigating stakeholder bias is a major reason to have an outside coach in the room.

(g) **Why focus is the hardest part.** *"Not all ideas and not all problems to solve yield the same result for your customer and yield the same result for the business. Peanut-butter product strategy is the same as not having a strategy. You can spread all your resources across multiple items but that does not yield the impact for the customer or the business."*

(h) **Where leaders avoid the hard calls.** *"A lot of justification takes place for why we need to do the 15 different things — 'we have so many resources.' But not all resources are the same. You want your highest-skilled team on your highest-priority problem."*

(i) **False-constraints coaching move.** *"If you could draw the line — let's say you had limited resources. Assume you don't have 500 engineers or 50 product teams. What if you only had 10? Where would you focus?"* Force a real prioritization by imposing a constraint the org doesn't actually have.

(j) **Communicating the hard call.** *"The communication piece seems to be the most difficult for senior leaders — because there's concern that if a team is not represented in the top priorities, they'll believe the team is not valuable to the business, which is not the case."* Coaching frame: *"just because you're not on the priority list doesn't mean you're not important — if you weren't important you wouldn't be funded. It just means that's not how the organization is measuring success this period."*

(k) **Coaching through disagreement — questioning the sacred.** *"A particular team might already be thinking about the solution because they have a team focused on fixing it — and instead of letting them focus on the higher-order bit, they want to keep the sacred team off to the side. It's about questioning things that have never been questioned. As an outsider working with the team, it's easier to ask because sometimes it's naive; sometimes it's just being open without fear of political repercussions."*

(l) **Weaponizing metrics — Hickman's guard.** *"The implied agreement going in is take our bias, put it to the side. Use an open mind. This isn't about your pet projects or your organization — this is about how we identify the biggest problems to solve within this period."* Bias tends to expose itself in the artifact during pre-work; a good coach spots it before the room does.

(m) **What good alignment looks like coming out.** The outputs:
- **Prioritized list of problems to solve.**
- **For each, a quantifiable measure of success.**
Directional measures ("increase adoption") are easy — *quantifying* takes work. *"I might think 75% is success but the CFO knows success is 85%. Aligning on that magnitude is critical. It's when we start identifying — okay, to get to 85% we'll have to let go of some things not on the prioritized list. That's where the rubber hits the road. Real leadership-team decisions with impact for the business."*

(n) **Tumbling the rocks.** *"You can't do this via an artifact — you need to be in the room. We always talk about *tumbling the rocks* — we need to tumble those rocks together, because there's going to be friction, and that's OK. It's OK to have that constructive friction."*

(o) **Communicating after the Jumpstart — broadly and often.** *"Every time you have the meeting, every time you bring people together, put it in a place where everyone has access — not just the problem to solve, but how we're measuring success and how you track that measurement as time goes on. The repetitive evangelism of the product strategy is almost as important as the choices."*

(p) **One priority list across the organization.** *"The head of engineering, head of product, head of design, head of sales — we're all aligned on the product strategy. We don't have our own definition of what that is. Where huge technical challenges exist, guess what? The head of engineering and head of product have one priority list."*

(q) **Idiodi's individual-alignment check.** *"You communicate broadly and frequently — but coach it individually too. Nobody raises their hand and says 'that's a dumb strategy.' Once you have alignment, go through your next one-on-one — 'tell me what the priorities are. Tell me what the focus is.' See how well your communication has made its way down."*

(r) **Building the discipline to hold focus as priorities shift.** *"The only thing that should change your strategy is data that warrants a change. Have flexibility for existential crises or genuinely new data — a new competitor, a new startup gets funded, a new technology change. When that happens you may be warranted to go revisit the strategy."*
