# Discovery vs. Design

> **Citation:** [[marty-cagan|Marty Cagan]] · *Discovery vs. Design* · SVPG, Jul 9, 2021 — [svpg.com](https://www.svpg.com/discovery-vs-design/). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the canonical answer to "does product discovery apply to a feature team?"**, and the cleanest statement anywhere in the corpus of **who owns which risk under each team model**. This is the article that makes [[feature-teams-vs-product-teams]] a *risk-ownership* distinction rather than only an empowerment one.

- **Nomenclature note Cagan flags himself:** in this article alone, "design" means *both* user-experience design *and* software/architecture design.
- **The short answer.** Discovery is *"coming up with an effective solution to the problem we have been asked to solve."* Feature teams are given features and projects, not problems. *"So by these definitions, discovery is not relevant for feature teams."*
- **The long answer — the risk split.** On a **feature team**, *"it is the stakeholder that is implicitly taking responsibility for value and viability"*; the team is counted on for **usability and feasibility**, which is why designers and engineers are still needed. So *some* discovery remains relevant. Corollary Cagan draws in passing: this is *"why on a feature team, the product manager plays more of a project manager role."*
- **The skill gap is real, not rhetorical.** *"It's one level of skill to design a usable experience and a feasible architecture for a specific feature, but it's a whole other level of skill to be able to discover an effective solution to the problem."*
- **And the accountability gap.** On a feature team, if the solution disappoints, *"that's ultimately on the stakeholder."* On an empowered team, *"that's on us. Being accountable to results is hard."*
- **The escape route — practical advice for PMs stuck on feature teams.** The more a feature-team PM can demonstrate that she understands *viability* (the business dimensions) and has in-depth customer understanding (*value*), *"the more likely the leaders will be to let the feature team try tackling a hard problem to show what they can do."* A concrete [[coaching-up]] play.

## Notes for the coach
The most useful card for the very common coachee who says *"I'm a PM on a feature team, is any of this relevant to me?"* — because Cagan answers honestly (mostly no) and then gives a real path forward rather than telling them to quit. The risk-ownership table is also the fastest way to show a leader what they are implicitly signing up for when they hand a team a roadmap: they have just taken personal ownership of value and viability.
