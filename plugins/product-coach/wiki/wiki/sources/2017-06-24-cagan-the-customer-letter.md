# Cagan — The Customer Letter

> **Citation:** Marty Cagan · "The Customer Letter" · svpg.com, 2017-06-24 — [read the original](https://www.svpg.com/the-customer-letter/). Slim provenance card; the article is **not reproduced** here.

## Role
**Primary (SVPG canon) — root source for the customer-letter discovery *framing* technique**, and SVPG's own treatment of Amazon's working-backwards / PR-FAQ practice. Cagan positions it explicitly as a sibling of the [[opportunity-assessment]], for efforts too large for one.

## Notes for the coach
- **When to reach for it.** "For smaller and more typical size product discovery efforts, the opportunity assessment is usually sufficient." The customer letter is for larger efforts with **multiple objectives or several customer problems** — his worked example is a redesign intended to serve both existing and new customers at once.
- **The Amazon original.** The working-backwards process starts an effort with an **imagined press release** describing the future state: how does this improve the customer's life, what are the real benefits. The point is to counter the pull toward "an enumeration of all the features they plan to build, with little real thought into the actual benefits" — i.e. it is an **outcome-not-output** device.
- **Who actually reads it.** The product team, related and impacted teams, and leadership. Cagan's test: "if people don't see the value after reading then the product manager has more work to do, or perhaps should reconsider the effort."
- **The Nordstrom variation — the article's actual contribution.** Credited to **Walker Lockhart**, a long-time Amazonian who moved to Nordstrom. Instead of a press release, write an imagined **letter from a well-defined persona** to the CEO explaining why they're so happy and grateful and how the product changed their life — plus an imagined congratulatory CEO reply to the team explaining how it helped the business. The CEO-reply half is what carries the *viability* story, which the press-release form tends to drop.
- **Why Cagan prefers the letter.** Two stated reasons: the press-release format "is a bit dated" and no longer universally familiar; and the letter "does an even better job of creating the empathy for the customer's current pain."
- **Classification caution.** Cagan is careful that this is a **framing** technique, not a validation one. Some treat it as qualitative demand validation, but "it's only validating demand or value with your colleagues rather than real customers." Don't let a team count a compelling letter as evidence against value risk.
- **Aside worth keeping.** He notes real customer letters — including critical ones — are "extremely motivating," because they help a team "viscerally understand the problem."
