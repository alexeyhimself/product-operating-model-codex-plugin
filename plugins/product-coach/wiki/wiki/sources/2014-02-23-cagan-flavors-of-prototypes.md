## Notes for the coach
- **The four types, as named here (2014), each with its own one-line test:**
  1. **Feasibility prototypes** — engineer-written, just enough code to answer a technical-risk question (new technology, a new algorithm, performance under real load); may be throwaway or may become leverageable production code.
  2. **Low-fidelity user prototypes** — a "simulation," essentially an interactive wireframe; doesn't look real; fast to make (Balsamiq is Cagan's example tool); good for early workflow/comprehension and some usability testing, but represents only the information/workflow dimension — nothing about visual design or real data.
  3. **High-fidelity user prototypes** — "smoke and mirrors" that *looks* real but uses realistic-but-fake (not live) data; good for usability testing, stakeholder communication, and rapid learning — **but not for proving anything will sell**. Cagan's own favorite use isn't confirming people like it, but learning *why they don't* — each test subject as one piece of a puzzle.
  4. **Live-data prototypes** — hit real data sources, optionally take real traffic; the tool for actually *proving* whether an idea works, normally via A/B test (or opt-in/invite-only). A fraction of the effort of a real product; requires developers, not designers; not something you can run a business on even if it tests well.
  - Plus **hybrids** — commonly, access to live data sources without sending live traffic (useful for e.g. search/recommendations relevance, learned by observation and discussion rather than a live test).
- **The failure mode this article names directly:** people narrow "prototype" to whichever type they were first exposed to, and then reach for the wrong one — e.g. defaulting to a high-fidelity user prototype when the real question is feasibility, or vice versa. "Some people get themselves into trouble trying to use the wrong type of prototype for what they need to test."
- **The organizing principle:** "the key principle in product discovery is to come up with the fastest, cheapest way to test out your ideas" — pick the flavor that matches the risk and the situation, not a personal favorite; strong teams are skilled with all of them, not just one.
