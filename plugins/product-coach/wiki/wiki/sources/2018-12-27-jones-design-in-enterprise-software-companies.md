# Design in Enterprise Software Companies

> **Citation:** [[chris-jones|Chris Jones]] · *Design in Enterprise Software Companies* · SVPG, Dec 27, 2018 — [svpg.com](https://www.svpg.com/design-in-enterprise-software-companies/). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the wiki's canonical rebuttal to the B2B "design is a luxury" argument**, and the companion to Jones's own [[2016-08-30-jones-the-product-designer-role|Product Designer Role]] for the enterprise context specifically. Its subtitle is its thesis: *Enterprise Software Companies Are Getting Design Wrong.*

Key material:
- **The excuse, stated fairly.** The standard B2C/B2B shorthand: in B2C user and buyer are the same person; in B2B they differ — buyers need ROI, users need usability and workflow fit. Jones notes this is most prevalent at vendors with "a large, expensive, specialized field sales force selling into highly-placed buying centers."
- **Why it is a mistake.** These companies use the split "to justify an underinvestment in product design," treating design as "a luxury that serves the end-users who will be fully served only after some degree of commercial success." Jones is explicit that he is **not** making a focus-on-the-user argument: "My motivation for investing early in product design has as much to do with buyers as it does the users." The product is not merely the thing the sales force sells — "the product itself must also connect customers to its value."
- **Three ways design reaches the *buyer*.**
  1. **Design communicates [[positioning|positioning]].** Enterprise deals involve many buyers and influencers having conversations with no salesperson present; the sale depends on the customer using the terms and concepts that frame your solution — and "in many cases it is carried by the product itself."
  2. **Design is fundamental to the core value proposition.** Against the waterfall sequence (requirements → UI → technical solution), the three are "deeply intertwined": "Often, it is a design insight that motivates a technical approach which uncovers new possibilities… In some cases, a design insight may actually motivate the core product value itself."
  3. **Design enables new paths to market.** Consumerization of IT keeps raising the acceptable-experience bar; departments and working groups increasingly buy their own tools, so "users and buyers converge" and the experience demands become B2C-like.
- **The Vontu case (Jones's own).** He was an early PM at enterprise security company **Vontu** (later acquired by Symantec), whose product monitored outbound network traffic for leaking confidential data — the category that became **Data Loss Prevention (DLP)**. They invested heavily in design "*in order to better educate and influence the buyer*," showing how the product mapped to the customer's existing notions of confidential data, policy, incident and remediation. Through demos and screenshot workflows they "put the product at the center of the sales process," and it "quickly became the solution to which all other venders were compared." One design decision — how non-compliant data was highlighted within a transmission — "was the most frequently cited reason why a customer purchased our product," had "huge architectural ramifications," and "was not something we could have simply added later either to our product or to its positioning."
- **The Slack case.** Named as "the Poster Child for this Go-To-Market dynamic": initial growth entirely organic, little marketing spend, no direct sales; painless installation and very short time-to-value drove word-of-mouth adoption. "The product experience itself was a primary driver of customer adoption." Jones's warning: "If you are not investing in design there's a good chance a new competitor is, and that competitor may be in a position to attack your market in a whole new way because of it."
- **The closing reframe.** "You should never fixate on just the user or the buyer in isolation. Instead, your notion of *the customer* must include both." He prefers to think of the customer as "an ecosystem of interests each relating to different facets of buying, using and supporting the solution" — which makes enterprise software "really not that different from any other type of product."

## Notes for the coach
The highest-leverage use is with an enterprise leader who has ranked design below sales and engineering on a budget. Jones deliberately argues on the buyer's terms rather than the user's, which is the only framing that lands in that room. The Vontu detail — a single design decision being the most-cited *purchase* reason, and one that could not have been retrofitted — is the concrete counter to "we'll do design after we have traction." Two named exemplars enter the wiki here: **Vontu/DLP** (design as category definition) and **Slack** (design as go-to-market).
