## Notes for the coach
- **Cagan's definition of a stakeholder, verbatim:** "someone not explicitly a member of a product team, yet represents a key constituency, area of the business, or special expertise." Narrow and usable — cite it when a team calls everyone with an opinion a stakeholder.
- **The asymmetry claim that makes this article worth citing.** "Creating a solution that customers love is usually not that hard (assuming the product team has direct, unencumbered access to the users and customers, and the product data). But creating a solution that the customers love *and* also meets the many, often competing needs of the different parts of the business, can be very challenging." This is the clearest SVPG statement that **viability, not value, is the hard half** — pair it with [[2022-02-07-cagan-value-and-viability]] and with *Strong Opinions, Loosely Held* #1.
- **The precondition is on leadership, not on the stakeholder.** Emphasized in the original: "the onus is on the product leadership to ensure that every product team has a competent product manager that has put in the work to understand the various constraints of the business." And: "Expecting stakeholders to trust a product manager that does not have this foundation is not only unrealistic, but also unwise." Use this against the common coaching complaint that "our stakeholders won't let go" — the first question is whether the PM has earned it.
- **The central mechanism: preview the prototype before building.** Whenever a solution *might* affect a stakeholder, the PM shows a prototype so the stakeholder can consider implications **before anything is built**. If the PM understood the constraints, this takes minutes and a thumbs-up; if not, the team iterates on the prototype — not on shipped code. Directly grounds [[prototypes]]'s "prototype as the cheapest place to be wrong" framing.
- **Why not just convene everyone.** Two reasons given: hundreds-to-thousands of decisions make all-stakeholder convening impractical, and "committees very rarely innovate." The deeper reason is that stakeholders aren't working directly with the enabling technology, so they can't know **what's just now possible** — Cagan's italicized line: "*It is this combination of enabling technology with real customer problems and real business constraints that produces innovative products*."
- **The hard commitment.** "The pledge is to not build – and certainly not to ship – any solution that does not meet the critical needs of the business." Plus transparency: stakeholders are welcome in user testing, welcome to see prototypes, welcome to see usage data and live-data test results.
