# Product Therapy Ep 36 — Coaching Stakeholders

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[chris-jones|Chris Jones]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 36 · SVPG, January 22, 2026 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CmU4QaaP8CE). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the wiki's most complete podcast-form teaching on coaching *stakeholders* (the other half of "empowered teams").** Deepens [[stakeholder-relationship-health]] and [[cross-functional-partnering]] with Chris Jones's rare direct treatment of the topic — the operating-model incentives that create the animosity, the three signs of health, the *because-I-said-so* diagnosis with three root causes, the shift from funding projects to funding teams-and-outcomes, and the objection-by-objection playbook.

Key material:

(a) **Who is a stakeholder — the definition.** *"You're a stakeholder if you're not directly in the product organization but still very reliant on that group to create technology solutions that support your business needs."* Operating units with P&L, support functions, admin functions (finance, legal, compliance) — all can be very concerned with what happens in the product org without being part of it.

(b) **Why they *feel like* the enemy — the operating-model incentives.** Jones's frame: *"I'm always about bringing operating-model discussions around to incentives. There is not an incentive in the project model for deep partnership between the product organization and the business. In the product model, there is a very very strong incentive for that."* In the project model, product exists to serve — with implied status, endless impossible requirements, and blame when things don't deliver. Empowered teams *have* to actually look to stakeholders as partners because the teams themselves are on the hook for whatever solution ends up viable.

(c) **What healthy stakeholder relationships look like — the three signs.**
1. **Stakeholders communicate in the currency of outcomes** — not solutions. If you get one thing right, get this one.
2. **Stakeholders freely share business context** — the business constraints, the market realities, the go-to-market obligations.
3. **Stakeholders provide direct access to customers and to data** — the two things a team can't do its job without, and the two things traditional-model stakeholders most jealously guard.

(d) **Product has to make the first move.** Jones: *"If we've historically been a product organization that just takes requests and serves them, there is very little reason for stakeholders to trust that we can actually come up with better solutions. We've never been asked to. We've never demonstrated that we can."* → **The product org has to get its own house in order first.** That may involve training. It may involve new people. Then it has to *demonstrate results*, not just claim readiness.

(e) **The Idiodi diagnostic** — *"You have never met the head of product on this leader's mind. When I ask a CEO 'what's the most important initiative? Who do you go to first?' — the answer is often the VP of ops. Why? 'She understands the business, gets things done, has all the relationships.' That IS a product manager. Imagine if you had someone like that on every team."*

(f) **The "because I said so" pattern — three root causes.** When a stakeholder shuts down the *why* conversation, ask which of these three it is:
1. **They don't trust the team** — usually deserved; the team hasn't done its homework. Fix on the product side.
2. **They're threatened by the model** — see themselves as the acknowledged customer expert; new PM feels like a threat to their role. Sell them on the benefits of a partner who's on the hook with them.
3. **They don't know themselves** — genuinely not doing their own job. Common more than you'd think.

(g) **Meeting the stakeholder where they are — the less-threatening questions.** Instead of *"help me understand the business context"* (abstract, hard to bite into) or *"I hear the solution, tell me the problem"* (sounds like a rebuke), Jones's practical openers:
- *"OK, I hear you. You want this feature. How do we know we've actually solved the problem — beyond shipping it, what can we look at to say 'good has happened'?"*
- **The counterfactual:** *"If we don't build this feature, what problem still exists?"*
Both are *"I get it, I'm on board, we're going to do this — we just want to ensure that what we do is actually going to be effective in the way you need it to be."*

(h) **The "empowerment" misunderstanding — what teams do and don't get to choose.** Jones: *"Empowerment is a great word but it also does us a disservice — it's so big and warm that both teams and stakeholders overhear it. Teams don't get to solve the problems that they want to solve — stakeholders still own what problems need to be solved."* Solutions are the team's; the *process* of getting to solutions should include *regular lightweight check-ins with the stakeholder* so they can react to prototypes and flag context the team hasn't seen yet.

(i) **Coaching different types of stakeholders.**
- **Senior executives** → coach on **focus** (how many things can we actually be doing simultaneously? Is there enough strategy that limits and focuses these teams?).
- **Business-ops folks** → coach on **getting to root problems** vs a parade of solutions.
- **Finance** → coach on **how work is funded** (project funding → team + outcome funding).

(j) **Finance — moving from project funding to team-and-outcome funding.** Jones's frame: *"You switch it from output to outcome and you start small. It sounds big and scary because it's so different but it really is that simple. A finance person should understand that buying an outcome is way better than buying output that has no guaranteed outcome."* Deliverable: not project X, but *"a 20% increase in revenue"* or *"a growth-within-segment"* metric or *"value delivery moving from physical channel to digital channel by a percentage-point count."* Concrete AB-test-what-happens-when-you-do-it works — start with a pilot area.

(k) **The Idiodi finance-test story.** *"My CFO was funding projects. I said 'what do you get when you fund a project?' He said 'well, sometimes it comes in late, over budget, other projects fail…' I said 'well, do a test. Same budget for project X and same budget for people to solve the problem you want. Let me see how many projects I can give you at the end of the year.' It took us a year — he counted eight projects. More importantly, I got eight things that worked."*

(l) **The regulated/compliance stakeholder — "there are just things we have to do."** Jones's counter: *"There are often more than one way to get something done, and the particular way you're asking may not be the best. What we need is for you to bring us into your universe enough so that we're accountable for the actual problem you're trying to solve."* All those constraints ARE context. And [[keep-the-lights-on]] work exists — trust teams' judgment on the constant buzz.

(m) **Idiodi's compliance/security concrete illustration** — *"Are you suggesting that if you showed your team the security audit report and they saw all these reds, they would NOT be like 'you know what, let's not work on that other thing, let's work on this'? Are you suggesting that if you told them 'we're at risk of losing an $8 million client on this platform if we don't fix something', they will NOT say 'this is important, we should do it, and now we understand why'?"*

(n) **Pride of ownership as the trust dynamic Jones adds.** *"Trust with the stakeholder — is it a people thing, do I trust you? — you're bringing something equally important: teams have pride of ownership. If they're not feeling deep pain and embarrassment and frustration about the things the stakeholders are feeling, those teams are mercenaries. That's not a trust problem with the stakeholder, that's the product organization not having created genuine areas of ownership and staffing teams with people who care deeply about their respective products' success."*

(o) **What dysfunction looks like — Idiodi's list.**
- **Lip service to outcomes, still feeding teams roadmaps** (most common; not always malicious).
- **Confused team, overcommitted, reactive** — rarely a team problem; usually stakeholder-alignment problem.
- **The "shadow roadmap"** — back-channel commitments to features that leaders keep in private lists regardless of any pre-agreed OKRs or objectives.

(p) **Common objections — Jones's short playbook.**
- *"We need predictability."* → **Predictable *outcomes*, not predictable features.** Some things do need dates → carve them out as [[high-integrity-commitments]] with real prior discovery (not everything gets a date early).
- *"This just sounds slower — where do we plug discovery in?"* → The frame of the objection is wrong: it imagines layering discovery on top of the project model. In the product model, *discovery is where the iteration happens* — much cheaper and faster than iteration in delivery. Fewer things build, more of them work.
- *"We're too big for this."* → *"Are you bigger than Amazon? Google? Even so — big companies are complex; carve out a pilot and build on that success."*
- *"How do I hold anyone accountable if we do this?"* → In the product model, **teams are signing up for outcomes** — the accountability lands squarely on product managers and their leaders. When failure happens: **blameless postmortem** — could be team execution, could be stakeholder failing to provide context, could be a chosen problem that couldn't be cracked. *"Ensure that we do better next time."*

(q) **The visibility rule** (avoid the "black hole"). Jones: *"If you are in the product organization and you find yourself wanting to say 'leave us alone, give us a month' — you are creating a black hole and that will hamper trust with stakeholders."* Show the prototypes. Signal what could be coming. **Don't say "here's a roadmap of features" — say "here are some candidate ideas under consideration, so you know we're not just in kumbaya outcome space."**

(r) **Jones's single-thing-to-tell-stakeholders takeaway.** *"We are trying to bring the product organization in as a partner to figure out the best solution. How can you help them deeply understand the problem you need solved?"* And to product people: *"Get to the thing behind the thing. How do we know for you that this solution is actually working?"*
