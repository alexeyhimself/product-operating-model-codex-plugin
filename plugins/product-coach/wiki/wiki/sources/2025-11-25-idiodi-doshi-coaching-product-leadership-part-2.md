# Product Therapy Ep 33 — Coaching Product Leadership with Shreyas Doshi (Part 2)

> **Citation:** [[christian-idiodi|Christian Idiodi]] and [[shreyas-doshi|Shreyas Doshi]] · *Product Therapy* podcast, Ep 33 · SVPG, November 25, 2025 — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GfO2x7DJgr0). Slim provenance card; raw file stays local.

## Role
**Primary — the second half of the Doshi conversation** (Part 1 → [[2025-08-21-idiodi-doshi-coaching-product-leadership-part-1]]). Where Part 1 was about the role and hiring, Part 2 goes to the coaching of already-in-role leaders: how new leaders erode trust, why saying *"I don't know"* is treated like a crime, the *frameworks-as-packaged-intuition* concept (with its inverse *framework theater*), and how AI is changing what product leaders are for.

Key material:

(a) **What new senior leaders need coaching on — what NOT to do.** *"These individuals are already incredibly talented, accomplished, intelligent, strong communicators. Communication 101 is not going to be a good use of anybody's time. What they need to understand is what NOT to do — because it's through those many ideas and through doing many of those things that they erode trust."*

(b) **Doshi's own case study — how he eroded trust as a domain-expert senior leader.** Joined a company with deep domain expertise. Went in *"talking about everything that is wrong, with an intent, with a passion for fixing it, for inspiring people."* His execution: *"basically sounded like I know better than you — we are going to discount all your efforts over the last 3–5 years."* Only realized the folly two years after leaving. With some individuals he could never rebuild the trust. **Rule: take things a little slower than your instinct is.** *"You want to come in as a leader and leave your imprint and make a bunch of changes in your first 30 days. Sometimes there is a better way."*

(c) **Listening as art, not skill.** *"Most product leaders severely underestimate the value — I almost call it an art, not even a skill — of listening. People can tell very easily if you're not truly listening. Even if they can't put it in words, they feel like something was off."* Especially critical in the first weeks (one-on-ones with the team) — you get more content, you get context of team + product + company, and the individual walks away with *"appreciation and some degree of baseline confidence — I'm excited to see what happens with this leader."*

(d) **The "I don't know" is a punishable crime problem.** Doshi's poll of PMs — *would it be acceptable to say 'I don't know' in a product review with the CEO on an edgy detail about one customer using one feature?* → 90%+ say the manager would give feedback the next day: *"you're supposed to know these things."* Logically it's impossible to know every detail about a non-trivial product with 200 customers. But if you rattle off partial BS or misdirect the question like a politician, *"you will be seen as smart and capable and on top of all the details."* Organizations incentivize this. Root cause: *"a lot of insincerity and overall emphasis on the optics in pretty much every company. It doesn't matter how great of a culture it is — particularly once you exceed a certain size."* Even Google delayed it to thousands of employees; almost no other company has.

(e) **Judgment + courage — and when it's really "not having the right words."** For most product leaders, the block is not lack of courage but lack of the right words. *"When the right words emerge somehow, it's not that we're no longer afraid — but the fear is much lower."* Coaching CEOs and founders is often about helping them find the words that unlock the courage they already have.

(f) **The Idiodi story — modeling by contrast.** Idiodi coaching a CEO who swore at his direct reports in a meeting; CEO's rationale: *"the former CEO screamed at us all the time — look at me now, I'm CEO."* Idiodi asked permission to reframe the same message in a different style in front of the reports. The CMO said: *"we're used to him talking to us that way, but the way you explained it, I could already see two or three things I needed to do."* CEO: *"I didn't know there was a different way to do this."* Rule: *"most leaders don't know how, because it's never been modeled."*

(g) **Accurate diagnosis of the problem — the 2 min / 20 min / 2 hr / 2 days / 2 weeks rule.** *"In practice, most product failure — particularly in B2B — is not that we built the wrong product or couldn't get distribution. It's that we didn't understand the customer problem. We were destined to fail the day we decided to solve this problem in this manner."* Yet leaders don't apply the same rigor to their own daily leadership/communication/negotiation problems — *"an incorrect problem diagnosis followed by defaulting to past patterns."* Coaching prescription: **pause for 2 minutes, 20 minutes, 2 hours, 2 days, and sometimes 2 weeks** — use judgment for how long the specific problem deserves. *"In order to save those 2 days, you're wasting months. In order to save the 2 weeks, you are now wasting 2 years."* The old philosophy line finally rings true: *"the answer is in the question; the solution is in the problem."*

(h) **The Idiodi corollary — "if you want better X, work on the people who do X."** Coaching PMs is coaching PMs; process only helps at the margins. This ep restates the scaling-through-people theme that runs through the Bufrem eps (Ep 31 [[2025-10-30-idiodi-bufrem-coaching-how-to-coach|Coaching How to Coach]]).

(i) **Frameworks — good vs framework theater.** Doshi's realization at Stripe when asked to scale strategic capability across the PM team: *"a good framework packages the intuition of someone who is world-class at the given thing."* Can I make everyone in the world have Cagan's intuition? No. But if I reverse-engineer that intuition — *"how is this person thinking, what are they thinking, why are they thinking it"* — and package that in a framework, the framework is *practical and implementable.* It won't make everyone world-class, but it *substantially raises their capability very quickly.* Inverse — **framework theater** — *"an octagonal framework with eight colors with opposing vertices and some backronym launched on LinkedIn — 5,000 likes. The problem is it doesn't work."*

(j) **AI and product leadership — where models are surprisingly good.** *"Every model I've tried shows greater human empathy than the median director or VP-level person at a tier-one company in the Valley."* Concrete evidence: chat with Claude/GPT/Gemini about an interpersonal situation with a report — the model figures out (after 2–3 reprompts of *"no, this answer is incorrect, try again"*) why the conventional response is wrong. That same conversation with the median VP would take *"3–4 hours and even then there is very little success of the individual developing that insight."*

(k) **AI and existing companies — the incumbent trap.** *"A lot of existing companies, one mistake they're making is they're just bolting AI on top of an existing product — I understand why, classic innovator's dilemma and user-retraining tax — but as a user I can see it's not working well. If you are indeed going to commit to 'we are AI first,' in some cases it's okay to be a little bolder."*

(l) **Team-size shift and second-order effects.** *"Teams of the future are going to be smaller and still able to get the amount of work done that they're getting with 10x the size right now. I can totally see a team of 3 doing what 30 does today."* Fewer people = less coordination time. But more capacity ships more things → **who is the entity that decides what is good?** For the foreseeable few years, *"it's got to be a human."* Doshi's challenge: *"are you going to be that person? That's the highest-leverage job."*

(m) **The skills that separate — judgment, product sense, strategic sense, taste.** *"It turns out it's the very same skills that were always important."* Taste as defense in the AI world: **taste is not just recognizing good, it is being able to explain *why* it's good and communicate that to others.** Reference: Steve Jobs on this exact point. *"We've all worked with a product leader with great taste who gives a presentation, doesn't like it, shares that — but there is no explanation for why, and we're just left confused."* Consistently successful product leaders — regardless of company, product, domain, stage — had or developed **critical/analytical/strategic thinking, product sense, influence, listening, cognitive empathy** (same skill spike list from Part 1).
