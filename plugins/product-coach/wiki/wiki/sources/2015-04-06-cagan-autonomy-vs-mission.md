# Cagan — Autonomy vs. Mission

> **Citation:** Marty Cagan · "Autonomy vs. Mission" · svpg.com, 2015-04-06 — [read the original](https://www.svpg.com/autonomy-vs-mission/). Slim provenance card; the article is **not reproduced** here.

## Role
**Primary (SVPG canon) — part 2 of 4 in the *Autonomy* series**, and the most important of the four. It is an early, clear statement of the mechanism the wiki now calls [[strategic-context]]: **leadership controls vision and objectives; the team controls how.**

## Notes for the coach
- **The four provoking questions** Cagan opens with — all still live in coaching: a team wanting to pivot to a different customer type; a team not wanting to do the work to support a company initiative (e.g. internationalisation); a team convinced it must change course for a new platform opportunity while owning only part of the experience; a team wanting a different UX direction from everyone else.
- **The reconciliation mechanism — the core of the article.** "In healthy teams and organizations, the way we normally reconcile these views is that the leadership has control of two major inputs to the decision process. The first is the overall product vision, and the second are the specific business objectives assigned to each team." And the failure mode: "Problems arise if the leadership does not provide clarity on these two critical pieces of context. If they don't, there's a vacuum and that leads to real ambiguity over what a team can decide and what they can't." That vacuum diagnosis is the single most reusable line in the series.
- **The autonomy boundary, stated exactly.** "while the product vision and the key KPI's are provided to the team by leadership as part of the context, nothing is said about *how* to do that. That's where the team has the autonomy and flexibility." Cagan's framing of teams as "collaborations to solve hard problems."
- **Objectives — historical note for [[okrs]].** He describes team objectives as "a prioritized set of KPI's (also known as a **product scorecard**) or increasingly a set of OKR's," and welcomes "the resurgence of OKR's" because "when used properly, they help to reframe this situation from output (features on roadmaps) to outcome (business results)." This is a **2015** statement of outcomes-over-output, useful for dating the wiki's claim.
- **The roadmap as the actual dividing line.** "Assuming the leadership is providing this product vision and KPI's, the real difference between an autonomous team and others, often turns out to be whether or not there's a company roadmap." If leaders or stakeholders hand a team a list of committed features customers are already counting on, "there's little room for the teams to figure out the best way to solve the underlying business problems." Cite on [[outcome-based-roadmap]].
- **Special case 1 — design consistency vs. team autonomy.** How do you stop each embedded designer optimising for their own team's goals when "users could care less about your team structure"? Options range from a design manager reviewing all work to automating with pattern libraries, style guides and style sheets. Cagan's stated preference is **automation plus tolerating some "design debt"**, because putting the design manager in every review cycle "tends to slow things way down, as well as undermine autonomy." He explicitly wants designers embedded and first-class, not in an internal-agency model.
- **Special case 2 — company initiatives.** Internationalisation, responsive design, taking mobile seriously. Hopefully aligned to each team's KPIs and carried by an explicit OKR, but "sometimes an important initiative is not a clear and obvious win for every team. Yet if every team doesn't do its part, the initiative fails." Cagan's honest answer: "sometimes we just need to do our part, as part of a larger company." Part 4 of the series takes this up in full.
