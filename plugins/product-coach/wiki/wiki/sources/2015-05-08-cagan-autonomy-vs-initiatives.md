# Cagan — Autonomy vs. Initiatives

> **Citation:** Marty Cagan · "Autonomy vs. Initiatives" · svpg.com, 2015-05-08 — [read the original](https://www.svpg.com/autonomy-vs-initiatives/). Slim provenance card; the article is **not reproduced** here.

**Primary (SVPG canon) — part 4 of 4 in the *Autonomy* series**, closing the arc opened in part 1. Names the **three strategies for running discovery on an effort that spans many teams** — material the wiki had no source for.

## Notes for the coach
- **What "initiative" means here.** "Those product efforts that necessarily span many teams" — his examples: a site usability redesign, a move to responsive design, internationalisation/localisation for a significant new geography such as China. **Explicitly excluded:** re-platforming and other tech-debt work, which "we handle … differently."
- **The framing question.** "By definition, we have a complex project that we need to figure out, and we have many teams that will be contributing to this … the bigger question is how and who will lead this?" His reductio: if the initiative is adapting for the Chinese market, "do we send every team to China to figure this out? That wouldn't make much sense, and it's very possible that each would come to somewhat different conclusions."
- **The three strategies:**
  - **Lead team** (the main one used) — one team takes the primary role in both discovery and delivery, depending on work from the others but driving and coordinating; pick a team heavily impacted by the work. *Benefit:* clear ownership and responsibility. *Risk:* if the lead team doesn't keep the others involved in learnings and decisions, "when it comes time to spread out the work, these other teams may not understand or agree with the decisions, and that's when the very real challenges to sense of autonomy will arise."
  - **Transient team** — a special, initiative-lifetime discovery team, "normally … a product manager, a senior UX designer, and at least one senior engineer," who return to their normal teams once the delivery work is identified. *Benefit:* dedicated focus. *Risks:* accountability and ownership leave with them; the same not-understanding-the-decisions risk; and **it works against team durability**, which Cagan calls out explicitly.
  - **Combo team** — several teams doing discovery together. Cagan: "something I don't usually advocate, but I need to mention because it is sometimes used." *Benefit:* shared learning. *Main disadvantage:* "it usually suffers from **design by committee**. Way too many cooks in the kitchen." See [[2008-06-03-cagan-avoiding-design-by-committee]].
- **The honest conclusion.** "It is hard to argue that any of these are really better in terms of autonomy than others, because the very nature of an initiative is about doing something good for the organization and not necessarily what any single team decides they want to do. It's probably more accurate to say that you can **choose your approach to minimize the sense of loss of autonomy**." That reframing — minimise the felt loss rather than pretend there isn't one — is the practical takeaway for a coach.
